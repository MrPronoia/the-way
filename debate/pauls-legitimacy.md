# Is Paul a Reliable Source for What Jesus Taught?

**Position:** Paul never met the living Jesus, never studied under the men who did, and says so in writing. His gospel came from a private vision, he declared that the Jerusalem apostles "added nothing" to it, and he contradicted the binding ruling of James — Jesus's brother and the head of the Jerusalem church — the moment he was out of the room. The earliest Jewish-Christian communities, the ones with direct lineage back to that church, named him as an opponent, not an authority. We are not claiming Paul was a fraud or a villain; he was a brilliant, sincere man working from a Pharisaic framework and a vision he genuinely trusted. The claim is narrower and harder to dodge: Paul is a primary source for Paul's theology, and he is not a source for what Jesus taught. Thirteen of twenty-seven New Testament books are his, which is why the distinction matters so much.

---

## Strongest Evidence

### 1. Paul says his gospel did not come from the eyewitnesses
> "The gospel I preached is not of human origin. I did not receive it from any man, nor was I taught it; rather, I received it by revelation from Jesus Christ." — Galatians 1:11-12

He goes further: "I did not go up to Jerusalem to see those who were apostles before me" (Galatians 1:16-17), and of James, Peter, and John — "whatever they were makes no difference to me... they added nothing to my preaching" (Galatians 2:6). This is Paul's own testimony, in his own undisputed letter. On the question "where did this theology come from?" he has already answered: not from the Twelve.

### 2. He fails the apostleship test the Twelve themselves set
> "Therefore it is necessary to choose one of the men who have been with us the whole time the Lord Jesus was living among us, beginning from John's baptism to the time when Jesus was taken up from us. For one of these must become a witness with us of his resurrection." — Acts 1:21-22

Two criteria, established before Paul appears: present for the whole ministry, and witness to the resurrection. Paul meets neither. His commission rests on a roadside vision he narrates three different ways in the same book (Acts 9:3-8; 22:6-11; 26:12-18), including a flat contradiction about whether his companions heard the voice (9:7 vs. 22:9).

### 3. James ruled against him — and Jesus named James the successor
> "It is my judgment that we should not make it difficult for the Gentiles who are turning to God. Instead we should write to them, telling them to abstain from food polluted by idols, from sexual immorality, from the meat of strangled animals and from blood." — Acts 15:19-20

James presides and decides; Paul does not overrule him in the room. Then in his letters Paul reverses the first decree outright: "Eat anything sold in the meat market without raising questions of conscience" (1 Corinthians 10:25). And the Jerusalem church's authority is not a modern inference — Thomas records Jesus appointing James directly: "Wherever you are, you are to go to James the righteous, for whose sake heaven and earth came into being" (Gospel of Thomas 12, Lambdin).

### 4. James's letter reads as a direct rebuttal, and the church knew it
> "You see that a person is considered righteous by what they do, and not by faith alone." — James 2:24
> "A person is justified by faith apart from the works of the law." — Romans 3:28

These are answers to the same question that cannot both stand. Luther understood this perfectly well: his resolution was to downgrade James as "an epistle of straw" rather than question Paul. He chose Paul over Jesus's brother, and Protestantism inherited that choice.

### 5. The Jewish-Christian tradition names him as the enemy
> "For some from among the Gentiles have rejected my legal preaching, attaching themselves to certain lawless and trifling preaching of the man who is my enemy." — Epistle of Peter to James 2 (prefixed to the Clementine Homilies)

The Clementine Homilies stage Peter against an opponent whose credentials are a vision: "If, then, our Jesus appeared to you in a vision, made Himself known to you, and spoke to you, it was as one who is enraged with an adversary... if you were seen and taught by Him, and became His apostle for a single hour, proclaim His utterances, interpret His sayings, love His apostles, contend not with me who companied with Him" (Homilies 17.19). The named target is "Simon," but the argument is about a man who claims apostleship from a vision and whom Peter says "stood condemned" — Paul's own word for Peter at Galatians 2:11, reversed. Epiphanius (*Panarion* 30) records the Ebionites and Nazarenes rejecting Paul as an apostate from the law.

## Objections & Rebuttals

### "The Jerusalem church accepted Paul — they gave him the right hand of fellowship (Galatians 2:9)."
That's Paul's account of a meeting whose outcome he then overrode in practice. Acts 21:20-25 shows the Jerusalem leadership still anxious about reports of Paul teaching Jews to abandon Moses, and asking him to publicly prove otherwise. Acceptance of his Gentile mission is not endorsement of his theology — and Paul himself describes opposing Peter to his face and losing Barnabas, his own sponsor, to the other side (Galatians 2:11-13).

### "Peter personally endorses Paul — 2 Peter 3:15-16 calls him 'our beloved brother Paul.'"
2 Peter is the most widely doubted book in the New Testament; the mainstream scholarly consensus, including conservative-leaning critics, dates it well into the 2nd century and treats Petrine authorship as pseudonymous. Even the passage itself concedes Paul's letters contain "some things hard to understand, which the ignorant and unstable twist." A disputed 2nd-century letter is thin ground for settling a 1st-century dispute.

### "James and Paul mean different things by 'works' — James means the fruit of faith, Paul means law-keeping. No contradiction."
That harmonization is honest and widely held, and it is the strongest version of the objection. But both men argue from the same example — Abraham — to opposite conclusions (James 2:21-24 vs. Romans 4:1-5), which is hard to explain if they're discussing different topics. And it doesn't touch the question that matters here: Jesus, asked directly, said "keep the commandments" (Matthew 19:17). James agrees with Jesus. Paul needs explaining.

### "The Clementines are 3rd-4th century anti-Pauline fiction — you can't use them as history."
Agreed that they are not 1st-century documents, and we don't cite them as eyewitness testimony. We cite them as evidence of something narrower and uncontested: that Jewish-Christian communities claiming descent from the Jerusalem church preserved a tradition in which Peter's authority and a visionary apostle's authority were in direct conflict. That such a tradition existed at all is the point.

### "Paul's prophecies about the return didn't fail — he spoke of a general future hope."
He used first-person plural and framed it as revelation: "we who are still alive, who are left until the coming of the Lord" (1 Thessalonians 4:15-17); "we will not all sleep, but we will all be changed" (1 Corinthians 15:51); "the night is nearly over" (Romans 13:12). Ehrman, Allison, and Schweitzer all read this as genuine imminent expectation. Deuteronomy 18:22 supplies the test Paul the Pharisee knew: "if the word does not come to pass or come true, that is a word that the Lord has not spoken."

## Sound Bites

- Paul told us himself where his gospel came from. It wasn't from anyone who knew Jesus.
- The men who ate with Jesus for three years "added nothing" to his message — his words, not ours.
- Peter set the qualifications for apostle in Acts 1. Paul meets neither one.
- When Jesus's brother and the self-appointed thirteenth apostle disagree, the church picked the apostle.
- Luther couldn't harmonize Paul and James either. He just decided James had to go.
