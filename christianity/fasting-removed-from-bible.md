# Was Fasting Edited Out of the New Testament?

Fasting was one of the central spiritual practices of the early Jesus movement. Jesus practiced it, taught it, assumed his followers would continue it, and declared that certain spiritual work could only be accomplished through it. Yet modern English Bibles have quietly removed or downgraded key fasting references from the New Testament text. In Mark 9:29, "prayer and fasting" became just "prayer." Matthew 17:21 -- an entire verse about prayer and fasting -- was deleted from some translations altogether. Acts 10:30 dropped Cornelius's fasting. These are not interpretive differences. They are textual changes, traceable in the manuscript record. The question is not whether the changes happened -- they did. The question is why, and what was lost. The manuscript evidence, the testimony of early church fathers, the practice of the Didache community, and the convergent witness of every other major world religion all point in the same direction: fasting was not peripheral to Jesus's teaching. It was essential. And its removal represents one of the most consequential editorial decisions in the history of the Bible.

---

## The Evidence

### The Key Textual Changes

| Verse | Earlier Reading (KJV / Majority Text) | Modern Reading (ESV / NIV / NASB) | What Changed |
|-------|---------------------------------------|-----------------------------------|-------------|
| **Mark 9:29** | "This kind can come forth by nothing, but by **prayer and fasting**" | "This kind cannot be driven out by anything but **prayer**" | "And fasting" removed |
| **Matthew 17:21** | "Howbeit this kind goeth not out but by **prayer and fasting**" | *Entire verse omitted* (jumps from v. 20 to v. 22) | Whole verse deleted |
| **Acts 10:30** | "Four days ago **I was fasting**, and at the ninth hour I prayed" | "Four days ago, at this hour, I was praying" | Cornelius's fasting removed |
| **Tobit 12:8** | "Prayer is good with **fasting** and alms" | "Prayer is good with **truth** and alms" (Sinaiticus reading) | "Fasting" replaced with "truth" |

The pattern is consistent: references to fasting as a spiritual discipline are systematically reduced or eliminated.

### The Manuscript Evidence

The modern editorial decisions rest primarily on two manuscripts:

- **Codex Sinaiticus** (4th century) -- Omits "and fasting" from Mark 9:29. However, a later corrector added it back in the margin, indicating awareness that the omission was contested even in antiquity.
- **Codex Vaticanus** (4th century) -- Also omits the fasting references. Additionally changed "fasting" to "truth" in Tobit 12:8.

These two codices are the foundation of modern critical texts (the Nestle-Aland / United Bible Societies Greek New Testament). Because they are among the earliest complete manuscripts, modern text critics give them preferential weight.

**But the counter-evidence is substantial:**

- **Papyrus P45** (early-mid 3rd century) -- One of the oldest surviving New Testament manuscripts. P45 has a lacuna (physical damage) at this point in Mark 9:29; the UBS apparatus lists it as "vid" (videtur -- "it appears"), meaning the evidence is uncertain but consistent with the longer reading that included "and fasting."
- **The Byzantine Majority Text** -- The vast majority of surviving Greek manuscripts (thousands) include "and fasting." The two Alexandrian codices that omit it are the outliers, not the norm.
- **Early church fathers who quote the verse with "and fasting":**

| Church Father | Date | Attestation |
|--------------|------|-------------|
| **Clement of Alexandria** | 2nd century | Cited fasting as integral to apostolic teaching |
| **Origen** | 3rd century | "Let us attend to this: this kind goes out saved by prayer and fasting" |
| **Jerome** | 4th century | Used Clement's text in his defense of prayer and fasting |
| **Epiphanius** | 4th century | Treated the fasting reading as apostolic; cursed those who opposed fasting |
| **Chrysostom** | 4th century | "This kind goeth out not but by prayer and fasting" -- applied to all evil spirits |

The patristic evidence is unanimous. Not one early church father quotes the verse without "and fasting." The reading is attested across geographic regions (Alexandria, Antioch, Rome, Constantinople) and across centuries (2nd through 5th). The omission appears only in two Alexandrian codices produced in the same scribal tradition.

### Why Was It Removed?

Textual critics generally apply two criteria:

1. **The shorter reading is preferred** -- the assumption that scribes were more likely to add material than remove it.
2. **The more difficult reading is preferred** -- scribes were more likely to smooth out theologically awkward passages.

In this case, both criteria actually support the *longer* reading (with fasting):

- **Criterion of embarrassment**: If "and fasting" were added by later scribes, it would have been added to make the passage *harder* -- requiring fasting on top of prayer. Scribes don't typically make texts more demanding.
- **Criterion of similarity**: Removing fasting protects a "high Christology" -- the theological position that the divine Jesus would not need to fast to cast out demons. An editor concerned with presenting Jesus as fully divine had reason to remove it. A scribe with no theological agenda had no reason to add it.

Two historical factors may explain the Alexandrian omission:

- **Anti-ascetic movements in Alexandria** -- The Apocalypse of Elijah (2nd century) explicitly warns against those "whose god is in their belly, who say there is no fast," suggesting an active controversy over fasting in the early Alexandrian church.
- **Christological disputes** -- As the church moved toward creedal formulations of Jesus's divinity (culminating at Nicaea in 325 CE), passages implying Jesus needed spiritual disciplines could be seen as undermining his divine nature.

Bruce Metzger, the leading textual critic of the 20th century, acknowledged in *A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament* that the external evidence for "and fasting" is strong, but sided with the shorter Alexandrian reading on methodological grounds. Bart Ehrman, in *The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture* (1993), documented how early scribes altered texts to support emerging orthodox Christology -- a pattern that would explain exactly this kind of removal.

## What Jesus Actually Said About Fasting

Whatever the manuscript debate over Mark 9:29, the canonical gospels themselves -- in passages that are textually undisputed -- present fasting as a core practice Jesus taught and expected.

| Reference | Text | Significance |
|-----------|------|-------------|
| **Matthew 6:16-18** | "**When** you fast, do not look somber as the hypocrites do... But when you fast, put oil on your head and wash your face, so that it will not be obvious to others that you are fasting" | Jesus says "**when** you fast," not "**if** you fast." He gives detailed instructions on *how* to fast properly. This is not optional advice -- it is guidance for an assumed practice. |
| **Matthew 9:15** | "The days will come when the bridegroom will be taken away from them, and **then they will fast**" | Jesus explicitly predicts that his followers *will* fast after his departure. This is a direct statement of expectation. |
| **Matthew 4:1-11** | Jesus fasts forty days in the wilderness before beginning his ministry | Jesus himself practiced extended fasting. The forty-day fast mirrors Moses (Exodus 34:28) and Elijah (1 Kings 19:8) and appears to be an initiation into his public work. |
| **Luke 2:37** | Anna the prophetess "never left the temple but worshiped night and day, **fasting** and praying" | Fasting is presented as the mark of the most devout -- the prophetic life. |
| **Acts 13:2-3** | "While they were worshiping the Lord and **fasting**, the Holy Spirit said, 'Set apart for me Barnabas and Saul'... So after they had **fasted** and prayed..." | The early church fasted before major decisions. The Holy Spirit spoke during fasting. |
| **Acts 14:23** | "Paul and Barnabas appointed elders for them in each church and, with prayer and **fasting**, committed them to the Lord" | Church leadership was appointed through prayer and fasting -- not voting, not credentials. |

Even without the disputed verses, the New Testament presents fasting as something Jesus practiced, taught, predicted his followers would do, and that the early church relied on for spiritual discernment. The undisputed passages make the removal of "and fasting" from Mark 9:29 and Matthew 17:21 all the more striking -- it was not a marginal practice being inflated, but a central one being diminished.

## Early Church Practice

### The Didache (c. 50-120 CE)

The Didache -- the earliest known Christian liturgical manual, likely predating several New Testament books -- prescribes fasting in two specific contexts:

**Didache 7:4** (on baptism): "Before the baptism, let the baptizer fast, and the one being baptized, and whatever others can. And order the one being baptized to fast one or two days before."

Fasting was required preparation for the most important Christian rite. Not recommended -- required. Both the person being baptized and the one performing the baptism were expected to fast.

**Didache 8:1** (on regular fasting): "Let not your fasts be with the hypocrites, for they fast on Monday and Thursday. Rather, fast on Wednesday and Friday."

This passage is remarkable for two reasons. First, it assumes regular fasting as a given -- the only question is which days. Second, it distinguishes Christian fasting from Pharisaic fasting not by rejecting fasting but by choosing different days. The earliest Christian community fasted twice a week as standard practice.

### The Apostolic Witness

Multiple early sources document fasting as universal among the original apostles:

- **Eusebius** (*Proof of the Gospel* 3.5, 4th century): All the apostles abstained from flesh and wine.
- **Hegesippus** (2nd century, preserved in Eusebius): James the Just "drank no wine nor strong drink, nor did he eat flesh" from his mother's womb. James was Jesus's brother and the leader of the Jerusalem church -- the person closest to the original teaching.
- **Acts of Thomas** (early centuries): Thomas "fasted continually and prayed and ate bread only with salt."
- **Pseudo-Clement, Two Epistles on Virginity** (2nd century): "Visit those who are harassed by evil spirits according to the teaching of our Lord, who has said: this kind goes out but by prayer and fasting offered unceasingly."
- **Clementine Homilies 9.10** (2nd century): "In order to the putting of demons to flight, the most useful help is abstinence and fasting."

The Clementine literature, which multiple scholars (Robert Eisenman, Schoeps, Vanvoorst, Koester) date in its kernel form to the 1st century, preserves a portrait of the original movement in which fasting was not supplementary but foundational.

## Why This Matters

The evidence trail is straightforward. Jesus fasted for forty days before beginning his ministry. He gave his followers specific instructions on how to fast (Matthew 6:16-18). He predicted they would fast after he was gone (Matthew 9:15). He said certain spiritual work required fasting (Mark 9:29, in the majority of manuscripts). The Didache community fasted twice a week and required fasting before baptism. Every apostle whose dietary practice is recorded practiced fasting and abstinence. Every church father who quoted Mark 9:29 included "and fasting." The practice is unanimously attested across the first four centuries of Christianity.

Then it was edited out. Not argued against. Not theologically refuted. Simply removed from the text -- quietly, in two Alexandrian manuscripts that became the basis for all modern critical editions. The result is that a practice Jesus taught, modeled, and assumed his followers would continue is now functionally absent from the version of the Bible most English-speaking Christians read.

This is not a conspiracy theory. It is textual criticism -- the same discipline that modern Bible translators rely on. The manuscripts speak for themselves. The question is whether to follow two Alexandrian codices or the combined witness of thousands of Greek manuscripts, every church father who quoted the passage, and the universal practice of the early church. Modern translation committees chose the two. Whether that was the right call is, at minimum, a question worth asking.

## Cross-Tradition Connections

Fasting is not a uniquely Christian practice. It is one of the most universal spiritual disciplines in human history. Every major religious tradition prescribes it, often in strikingly similar ways.

| Tradition | Fasting Practice | Source |
|-----------|-----------------|--------|
| **Islam** | Ramadan -- one full month of dawn-to-sunset fasting annually; considered one of the Five Pillars | Quran 2:183: "O you who believe, fasting is prescribed for you as it was prescribed for those before you, that you may attain taqwa [God-consciousness]" |
| **Judaism** | Yom Kippur (Day of Atonement) -- 25-hour complete fast; additionally, historical practice of fasting on Mondays and Thursdays | Leviticus 16:29-31; Psalm 35:13: "I humbled my soul with fasting" |
| **Buddhism** | Monks eat only before noon daily; extended fasting retreats in multiple traditions; the Buddha's own ascetic period before enlightenment | Vinaya Pitaka (monastic rules); Theravada practice of one meal per day |
| **Hinduism** | Ekadashi fasting (11th day of each lunar fortnight); Navratri (nine nights of fasting); Maha Shivaratri; various tradition-specific fasts throughout the year | Bhagavad Gita 17:5-6 (on proper vs. improper austerity); Manusmriti prescriptions |
| **Taoism** | Bigu ("grain avoidance") fasting practices for spiritual refinement and longevity; Zhuangzi's "fasting of the heart/mind" (xinzhai) | Zhuangzi, Chapter 4; Taoist alchemical texts on dietary restriction |
| **Native American** | Vision quest -- extended fasting (typically 1-4 days) in isolation to receive spiritual guidance and identity | Black Elk, *The Sacred Pipe*; widespread across Plains, Woodland, and Southwest traditions |
| **Zoroastrianism** | Fasting on specific holy days; emphasis on moderation in eating as spiritual discipline | Gathas; Vendidad prescriptions |

The Quran's reference is particularly notable: "fasting is prescribed for you **as it was prescribed for those before you**" (2:183) -- explicitly acknowledging fasting as a practice inherited from earlier traditions, including Judaism and Christianity.

Christianity is the only major world religion in which fasting has been functionally deemphasized. Not formally rejected -- just quietly sidelined, partly through the textual changes documented above, partly through centuries of institutional drift away from ascetic practice. The result is an anomaly: the one tradition whose founder explicitly taught, practiced, and prescribed fasting is the one where the practice has most completely disappeared from mainstream observance.
