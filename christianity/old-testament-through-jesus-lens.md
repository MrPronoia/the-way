# Should a Follower of Jesus Trust the Old Testament?

Jesus did not treat the Old Testament as a single, uniformly authoritative book. He quoted it when it aligned with his message of love, mercy, and inner transformation — and he openly overrode it when it didn't. Six times in the Sermon on the Mount alone, he used the formula "You have heard it said... but I say to you," directly superseding Old Testament law with his own teaching. He quoted the anti-sacrifice prophets, dismissed purity laws, relaxed Sabbath rules, and physically shut down the Temple sacrifice system that the Old Testament prescribed in painstaking detail. The Old Testament is not one book — it's a library of 39 texts written across roughly a thousand years by dozens of authors with conflicting agendas. Some of those voices sound exactly like Jesus. Others sound like the system he came to dismantle. The question isn't whether to read the Old Testament — it's learning which voices in it Jesus himself endorsed, and which ones he walked into the Temple and overturned tables over.

---

## What Is the Old Testament, Actually?

Before asking whether to trust the Old Testament, it helps to understand what it is. It's not a single book written by one person. It's a collection of 39 texts (in the Protestant canon; 46 in the Catholic; 81 in the Ethiopian) spanning roughly 1,000 years of composition, editing, and redaction.

The major sections:

| Section | Books | Character |
|---------|-------|-----------|
| **Torah / Pentateuch** | Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy | Law, origin stories, ritual legislation. Traditionally attributed to Moses but composed by multiple authors across centuries ([Documentary Hypothesis](#who-wrote-the-old-testament--the-documentary-hypothesis)). |
| **Historical Books** | Joshua, Judges, 1-2 Samuel, 1-2 Kings, etc. | National history of Israel — conquests, kings, wars, exile. Written and edited with theological agendas. |
| **Wisdom Literature** | Psalms, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Job, Song of Solomon | Poetry, philosophy, practical wisdom. The most internally diverse section — ranges from sublime mysticism to bitter despair. |
| **Prophetic Books** | Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Hosea, Amos, Micah, etc. | The prophets — God's critics of institutional religion. This is the section Jesus quoted most and aligned with most directly. |

These texts were not written as a unified whole. They were composed by different people, in different centuries, with different — and often contradictory — understandings of God, morality, and worship. The priestly editors who assembled the final version had their own institutional interests. Jeremiah said so explicitly: "The lying pen of the scribes has made it into a lie" (Jeremiah 8:8).

Understanding this is not an attack on the Bible. It's the key to reading it honestly.

## What Did Jesus Say About the Old Testament?

### The "Fulfill" Question — Matthew 5:17

The single most cited verse in defense of full Old Testament authority is Matthew 5:17:

> "Do not think that I have come to abolish the Law or the Prophets; I have not come to abolish them but to fulfill them."

This gets used to argue that Jesus endorsed every word of the Old Testament. But the Greek word translated "fulfill" — **plēroō** — does not mean "keep doing everything forever." It means to bring to completion, to bring to its intended destination, to fill up what was lacking.

Jesus was saying: **I'm where this was heading.** The law was a stepping stone, not the destination. And then he immediately demonstrated what "fulfillment" looks like — by overriding six Old Testament laws in the very same sermon.

### The Sermon on the Mount — Six Direct Overrides

In Matthew 5:21-48, Jesus uses the formula "You have heard it said... but I say to you" six times. Each time, he cites an Old Testament law and then replaces it with something higher:

| Old Testament Law | Jesus's Override | Citation |
|-------------------|------------------|----------|
| "You shall not murder" (Exodus 20:13) | "But I say to you that everyone who is angry with his brother will be liable to judgment" | Matthew 5:21-22 |
| "You shall not commit adultery" (Exodus 20:14) | "But I say to you that everyone who looks at a woman with lustful intent has already committed adultery with her in his heart" | Matthew 5:27-28 |
| Divorce certificate required (Deuteronomy 24:1) | "But I say to you that everyone who divorces his wife, except on the ground of sexual immorality, makes her commit adultery" | Matthew 5:31-32 |
| "Do not swear falsely" (Leviticus 19:12) | "But I say to you, do not take an oath at all... Let what you say be simply 'Yes' or 'No'" | Matthew 5:33-37 |
| "An eye for an eye" (Exodus 21:24) | "But I say to you, do not resist the one who is evil. If anyone slaps you on the right cheek, turn to him the other also" | Matthew 5:38-39 |
| "Love your neighbor" (Leviticus 19:18) and implied "hate your enemy" | "But I say to you, love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" | Matthew 5:43-44 |

This is not commentary on the Old Testament. This is a rabbi standing in front of a crowd and saying: **the old law said X, but I'm telling you Y.** In the Jewish context of the time, this was extraordinary — no rabbi claimed personal authority over Torah. Jesus did.

### What Jesus Kept from the Old Testament

Jesus did not reject the Old Testament wholesale. He specifically affirmed:

**The two greatest commandments:**
> "You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind. This is the great and first commandment. And a second is like it: You shall love your neighbor as yourself. On these two commandments depend all the Law and the Prophets." — Matthew 22:37-40

Both commandments come from the Old Testament (Deuteronomy 6:5 and Leviticus 19:18). But notice what Jesus did — he took the entire Old Testament and distilled it down to two principles. Everything else "depends" on these. Anything in the OT that contradicts love of God and love of neighbor fails Jesus's own filter.

**The prophetic tradition:**
Jesus quoted the prophets constantly — especially their critiques of institutional religion:
- Hosea 6:6 — "I desire mercy, not sacrifice" (quoted twice, in Matthew 9:13 and 12:7)
- Isaiah 56:7 — "My house shall be called a house of prayer for all nations" (quoted during the Temple cleansing)
- Isaiah 61:1-2 — "The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he has anointed me to proclaim good news to the poor" (his inaugural sermon in Luke 4:18-19 — notably, he stopped mid-sentence, omitting Isaiah's "day of vengeance")

**The Psalms:**
Jesus quoted Psalms more than any other OT book — particularly Psalm 22 ("My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" — Matthew 27:46) and Psalm 110 (Matthew 22:44).

**The creation ideal:**
When challenged on divorce, Jesus bypassed the Mosaic law entirely and appealed to Genesis: "From the beginning it was not so" (Matthew 19:8). His instinct was to go back before the law, to the original design.

### What Jesus Directly Contradicted or Dismissed

**Dietary purity laws:**
> "It is not what goes into the mouth that defiles a person, but what comes out of the mouth; this defiles a person." — Matthew 15:11

Mark's Gospel adds the editorial comment: "Thus he declared all foods clean" (Mark 7:19). This single statement effectively nullifies the entire kosher system of Leviticus 11.

**Sabbath law:**
> "The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath." — Mark 2:27

When criticized for his disciples picking grain on the Sabbath — a violation of Old Testament law — Jesus didn't argue within the system. He reframed the entire purpose of the law: it exists to serve people, not the other way around.

**The purity/holiness system:**
Jesus touched lepers (Leviticus 13-14 mandated isolation). He allowed a "sinful woman" to touch him (Luke 7:37-39). He ate with tax collectors and sinners. He spoke with Samaritan women. Every interaction violated OT purity boundaries — and he did it deliberately, publicly, and without apology.

**Retributive justice:**
The Old Testament operates on retribution: obey and be blessed, disobey and be cursed (Deuteronomy 28). Eye for an eye (Exodus 21:24). Jesus replaced this with radical non-retaliation, enemy-love, and unconditional forgiveness — "seventy times seven" (Matthew 18:22).

**Capital punishment:**
The Old Testament prescribes death for adultery (Leviticus 20:10), blasphemy (Leviticus 24:16), working on the Sabbath (Exodus 31:15), and disobedient children (Deuteronomy 21:18-21). Jesus's response to the woman caught in adultery: "Let him who is without sin among you be the first to throw a stone" (John 8:7).

**The Temple sacrifice system:**
Jesus physically shut it down. He overturned the money changers' tables, drove out the animals, and blocked the flow of sacrificial goods through the Temple courts (Mark 11:15-17). He quoted Jeremiah 7:11 — the same chapter where Jeremiah denied God ever commanded sacrifice. This confrontation is recorded in all four Gospels and was the direct cause of his arrest and execution.

## Does the God of the Old Testament Match Jesus's Father?

This is the uncomfortable question at the center of the whole discussion. When you line up the character of the Old Testament God against the God Jesus described, the tension is severe.

| Attribute | Old Testament God (Yahweh) | Jesus's Father |
|-----------|---------------------------|----------------|
| **Temperament** | Jealous ("I the LORD your God am a jealous God" — Exodus 20:5). Wrathful. "The LORD is a man of war" (Exodus 15:3). | Unconditional love. "He makes his sun rise on the evil and on the good" (Matthew 5:45). |
| **Violence** | Commands genocide: "Now go and strike Amalek and devote to destruction all that they have. Do not spare them, but kill both man and woman, child and infant, ox and sheep, camel and donkey" (1 Samuel 15:3). | "Love your enemies." "Turn the other cheek." "Put your sword back in its place, for all who draw the sword will die by the sword" (Matthew 26:52). |
| **Scope** | National God of Israel. Chooses one people, commands them to conquer and destroy others. | Universal Father. "Go and make disciples of all nations" (Matthew 28:19). "My house shall be called a house of prayer for all nations" (Mark 11:17). |
| **Sacrifice** | Demands animal sacrifice in elaborate detail (Leviticus 1-7). Commands Abraham to sacrifice his son (Genesis 22). | "I desire mercy, not sacrifice" (Matthew 9:13, quoting Hosea). Overturned the sacrifice system in the Temple. |
| **Forgiveness** | Conditional. Requires blood, ritual, intermediaries. "Without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness" (Hebrews 9:22 — actually Paul/Hebrews author, not OT, but reflecting the OT system). | Unconditional. "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do" (Luke 23:34). No blood required. No intermediary. |
| **Judgment** | Punishes children for parents' sins "to the third and fourth generation" (Exodus 20:5). | "Judge not, that you be not judged" (Matthew 7:1). |
| **Accessibility** | Requires priests, temples, sacrifices, and ritual purity to approach. Kills anyone who touches the Ark (2 Samuel 6:7). | "The kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:21). No intermediary needed. |

The early church noticed this immediately. In the second century, **Marcion of Sinope** argued these were literally two different beings — the creator God of the Old Testament (whom he called the Demiurge) and the higher, loving Father that Jesus revealed. The mainstream church declared Marcion a heretic, but his question never went away.

The **Gnostics** took a similar position. The Nag Hammadi texts describe the Old Testament creator as a lesser, ignorant deity — not evil necessarily, but limited and jealous, exactly as the OT describes him. The true God, in Gnostic teaching, is beyond this — ineffable, loving, accessible only through direct inner knowledge (gnosis). Jesus came to reveal this higher God.

The **Nazarenes and Ebionites** — the earliest Jewish Christians, led by Jesus's own brother James — solved this differently. They kept the parts of the OT that aligned with Jesus and rejected the parts that didn't, believing the sacrificial laws had been added by corrupt scribes (exactly what Jeremiah 8:8 says). They didn't need two Gods — they just recognized that one library had been edited by people with different agendas.

## Who Wrote the Old Testament? The Documentary Hypothesis

Understanding *why* the Old Testament contradicts itself requires understanding how it was assembled. The Torah (first five books) was not written by Moses. Scholars have identified at least four major source traditions woven together by later editors:

| Source | Date | Character | Contribution |
|--------|------|-----------|--------------|
| **J (Yahwist)** | c. 950 BCE | Uses "YHWH" for God. Anthropomorphic — God walks in the garden, argues with Abraham, changes his mind. | Much of Genesis and early Exodus narratives. |
| **E (Elohist)** | c. 850 BCE | Uses "Elohim" for God. More distant, transcendent deity. | Parallel accounts in Genesis and Exodus. |
| **D (Deuteronomist)** | c. 621 BCE | Associated with Josiah's reforms. Emphasizes covenant loyalty. | Deuteronomy, editorial framework of Joshua-Kings. |
| **P (Priestly)** | c. 550-400 BCE | Written during and after the Babylonian exile. Priestly institutional interests. | **The vast majority of sacrificial legislation** — Leviticus, ritual sections of Numbers and Exodus. Genealogies, purity laws, temple regulations. |

The critical insight: **the Priestly source was written last** but was inserted throughout the earlier material to make it look ancient. The elaborate sacrificial system of Leviticus was composed during the exile, when the priestly class needed to reassert institutional authority. Many of the prophets who condemned sacrifice (Amos, Hosea, Isaiah, Micah, Jeremiah) were writing *before* this material existed in its current form.

This is why Jeremiah could say "I did not command your ancestors concerning burnt offerings" (Jeremiah 7:22) — because the sacrificial legislation may not have existed in the Torah of his time. It was added later by the "lying pen of the scribes" he condemned (Jeremiah 8:8).

For the full evidence on a pre-sacrificial version of Deuteronomy, see the [Moses Scroll deep dive](moses-scroll-deuteronomy-without-sacrifice.md).

## The Parts of the Old Testament That Sound Like Jesus

Not all of the Old Testament is at odds with Jesus. Some sections are so aligned with his teaching that he quoted them directly. These are the OT's gold:

### The Prophetic Tradition

The prophets were the dissenters of ancient Israel — speaking against the religious establishment, calling for justice over ritual, mercy over sacrifice. Jesus identified himself with this tradition explicitly (Luke 4:24 — "No prophet is accepted in his hometown").

Key prophetic texts that align with Jesus:

| Prophet | Passage | Teaching |
|---------|---------|----------|
| **Hosea** | 6:6 | "I desire mercy, not sacrifice." Jesus quoted this twice. |
| **Micah** | 6:8 | "What does the LORD require of you? To do justice, love kindness, and walk humbly with your God." |
| **Isaiah** | 1:11-17 | God rejects sacrifice and demands instead: "Seek justice, rescue the oppressed, defend the orphan, plead for the widow." |
| **Isaiah** | 61:1-2 | "Good news to the poor... freedom for the captives... liberty to the oppressed." Jesus read this aloud as his mission statement (Luke 4:18-19). |
| **Jeremiah** | 7:22-23 | "I did not command your ancestors concerning burnt offerings... but this: Obey my voice." |
| **Jeremiah** | 31:33 | "I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts." — The internalized law, not external ritual. |
| **Amos** | 5:21-24 | "I hate, I despise your festivals... But let justice roll down like waters." |

For the complete prophetic record, see the [full deep dive on the prophetic tradition against sacrifice](prophetic-tradition-against-sacrifice.md).

### The Psalms — The Devotional Heart

The Psalms are the most internally diverse section of the Old Testament. They contain everything from profound mystical intimacy with God to calls for enemies' children to be dashed against rocks (Psalm 137:9). Jesus quoted the devotional and prophetic Psalms — never the violent ones.

Psalms that align with Jesus's teaching:
- **Psalm 51:16-17** — "You do not delight in sacrifice... The sacrifice acceptable to God is a broken spirit; a broken and contrite heart."
- **Psalm 40:6** — "Sacrifice and offering you did not desire."
- **Psalm 46:10** — "Be still, and know that I am God."
- **Psalm 23** — The Lord as shepherd (Jesus adopted this image directly — John 10:11).
- **Psalm 103:8** — "The LORD is merciful and gracious, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love."

### Wisdom Literature

Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and Job all contain insights compatible with Jesus's teaching — particularly the emphasis on wisdom, humility, and the limits of human understanding. Proverbs 3:5-6 ("Trust in the LORD with all your heart and lean not on your own understanding") is pure Jesus-energy. Ecclesiastes' skepticism about wealth and status anticipates the Sermon on the Mount. Job's conclusion — that God is beyond human theological systems — anticipates Jesus's challenge to every religious authority who claimed to have God figured out.

### Genesis 1-2 — The Original Design

When Jesus wanted to make a point about God's actual intention, he went back to Genesis — before the law, before the priests, before the sacrificial system. "From the beginning it was not so" (Matthew 19:8). Genesis presents a vegetarian creation (Genesis 1:29 — "I have given you every seed-bearing plant... and every tree that has fruit with seed in it. They will be yours for food"), a God who walks with humans directly, and a world without violence or blood. Jesus's appeal to "the beginning" is an appeal over the heads of the entire legal and sacrificial tradition to something older and simpler.

## The Parts of the Old Testament That Don't Sound Like Jesus

These are the passages that create the deepest tension for anyone trying to follow Jesus while treating the entire Old Testament as authoritative:

### Commanded Genocide

> "Now go, attack the Amalekites and totally destroy all that belongs to them. Do not spare them; put to death men and women, children and infants, cattle and sheep, camels and donkeys." — 1 Samuel 15:3

> "In the cities of the nations the LORD your God is giving you as an inheritance, do not leave alive anything that breathes." — Deuteronomy 20:16

These are not ambiguous. They describe God commanding the systematic extermination of entire peoples — including children and infants. There is no way to reconcile "kill every infant" with "love your enemies." These commands come from a tribal war theology that Jesus explicitly dismantled.

### Sanctioned Slavery

> "Your male and female slaves are to come from the nations around you; from them you may buy slaves." — Leviticus 25:44

> "If a man beats his male or female slave with a rod and the slave dies as a direct result, he must be punished, but he is not to be punished if the slave recovers after a day or two, since the slave is his property." — Exodus 21:20-21

The Old Testament does not merely tolerate slavery — it regulates it, normalizes it, and treats slaves as property. Jesus never endorsed this. His entire ethic of human dignity, his teaching that the last shall be first (Matthew 20:16), and his identification with "the least of these" (Matthew 25:40) are fundamentally incompatible with a system that treats people as property.

### Death Penalties for Minor Offenses

The Old Testament prescribes execution for:
- Working on the Sabbath (Exodus 31:15)
- Cursing your parents (Leviticus 20:9)
- Adultery (Leviticus 20:10)
- Blasphemy (Leviticus 24:16)
- Being a "rebellious son" (Deuteronomy 21:18-21)
- Picking up sticks on the wrong day (Numbers 15:32-36 — a man is executed for gathering wood on the Sabbath)

Jesus's response to this entire system was John 8:7: "Let him who is without sin cast the first stone." He didn't reform the death penalty — he dismantled its moral foundation.

### Collective Punishment and Generational Guilt

> "I, the LORD your God, am a jealous God, punishing the children for the sin of the parents to the third and fourth generation." — Exodus 20:5

Jesus rejected this framework entirely. When asked whose sin caused a man's blindness — his own or his parents' — Jesus answered: "Neither" (John 9:3). The entire premise of inherited guilt is foreign to Jesus's teaching.

### Violent Psalms

Not all Psalms are devotional poetry:

> "Happy is the one who seizes your infants and dashes them against the rocks." — Psalm 137:9

> "Break the teeth in their mouths, O God." — Psalm 58:6

These express raw human rage and desire for revenge against enemies. They are the opposite of "love your enemies" and "pray for those who persecute you." Jesus never quoted these psalms.

## How Did the Early Church Handle This Tension?

The question of what to do with the Old Testament was one of the first and most divisive debates in early Christianity:

### Marcion (c. 85-160 CE)

Marcion drew the sharpest line. He argued that the Old Testament God and Jesus's Father were entirely different beings. The OT God was a creator deity — powerful but limited, jealous, violent, tribal. Jesus came to reveal a higher God of pure love previously unknown to humanity. Marcion rejected the entire Old Testament and kept only a modified Luke and ten of Paul's letters.

The mainstream church declared Marcion a heretic — but his challenge forced them to define what the Old Testament actually meant for Christians. The answer they settled on (allegorical reading, typological fulfillment) was essentially a compromise that kept the OT in the canon while acknowledging it couldn't be read at face value.

### The Gnostics

The Nag Hammadi texts (discovered in Egypt in 1945) preserve multiple Gnostic perspectives. Most agreed with Marcion's basic insight — the OT creator was not the highest God — but developed more elaborate cosmologies. The Gospel of Philip, the Apocryphon of John, and other texts describe the OT God as a lesser being (sometimes called Yaldabaoth or the Demiurge) who created the material world but is ignorant of the true divine realm. Jesus descended from the higher realm to awaken people trapped in the Demiurge's system.

### The Nazarenes and Ebionites

Jesus's own family and earliest followers took a different approach. They kept the ethical and prophetic parts of the Old Testament and rejected the sacrificial legislation, believing it had been added by corrupt scribes. Epiphanius records that the Nazarenes "believe Moses received some legislation, but not this legislation" — they accepted ethical commandments but denied that God ever commanded animal sacrifice. James the Just, Jesus's brother and leader of the Jerusalem church, was a vegetarian who never participated in Temple sacrifice (Eusebius, *Church History* 2.23, citing Hegesippus).

This position didn't require two Gods. It just required recognizing that the Old Testament was edited by humans with agendas — which is exactly what Jeremiah said.

### The Orthodox Compromise

The mainstream church kept the entire Old Testament but developed interpretive strategies to manage the tension:
- **Allegory** — Origen and the Alexandrian school read violent OT passages as spiritual metaphors
- **Typology** — OT events were read as "types" or foreshadows of Christ (e.g., Isaac's near-sacrifice as a preview of the crucifixion)
- **Progressive revelation** — God revealed himself gradually, so the OT represents an earlier, less complete picture
- **Dispensationalism** — different rules for different eras (a much later development, systematized by John Nelson Darby in the 1830s)

Each of these strategies is essentially an admission that the Old Testament cannot be read at face value by someone trying to follow Jesus.

## A Practical Filter — How to Read the Old Testament as a Follower of Jesus

Jesus gave his followers a clear filter for evaluating scripture. It's not complicated:

### The Jesus Test

**1. Does it sound like something Jesus would say?**

"Love your enemies" — yes. "Dash their infants against the rocks" — no. "I desire mercy, not sacrifice" — yes. "Kill everything that breathes" — no. This isn't cherry-picking. It's applying the filter Jesus himself used.

**2. Does it pass the Two Commandments test?**

Jesus said all the Law and the Prophets hang on love of God and love of neighbor (Matthew 22:40). Any OT passage that violates love of God (by misrepresenting God's character) or love of neighbor (by commanding violence against them) fails the test that Jesus himself established as the standard.

**3. The "from the beginning" principle**

When Jesus wanted to get back to God's actual intention, he went behind the law to the beginning — to Genesis, before the fall, before the priests, before the sacrificial system. "From the beginning it was not so" (Matthew 19:8). The closer an OT text is to the original creation ideal — peace, vegetarianism, direct communion with God, no violence — the more likely it reflects what Jesus understood as God's actual will.

**4. Prophets over priests**

Jesus aligned himself with the prophetic tradition — the voices calling for justice, mercy, and inner transformation — against the priestly tradition of ritual, sacrifice, and institutional religion. When in doubt, lean toward Isaiah, Hosea, Micah, and Jeremiah. Be cautious with Leviticus and Numbers.

### A Quick Reference Guide

| OT Section | Jesus's Relationship to It | How to Read It |
|------------|---------------------------|----------------|
| **Genesis 1-2** (creation) | Appealed to it as God's original design | High value — the "before the fall" ideal |
| **The Ten Commandments** | Affirmed and deepened them (Sermon on the Mount) | Core ethical foundation, but Jesus took them internal |
| **Prophetic books** (Isaiah, Hosea, Micah, Jeremiah, Amos) | Quoted constantly, identified himself with this tradition | **The gold of the OT.** Most aligned with Jesus's teaching |
| **Psalms** (devotional) | Quoted frequently, especially 22, 23, 51, 110 | Powerful devotional and mystical texts — read selectively |
| **Psalms** (violent/imprecatory) | Never quoted | Human rage, not divine instruction |
| **Proverbs / Wisdom literature** | Compatible with his emphasis on humility and wisdom | Valuable, read through the love-filter |
| **Levitical law** (sacrifice, purity) | Explicitly overrode dietary laws, purity codes, and sacrifice | Product of the priestly class, not Jesus's tradition |
| **Conquest narratives** (Joshua, Judges) | Never referenced, fundamentally incompatible with his ethic | Tribal war theology — the furthest thing from "love your enemies" |
| **Deuteronomic law code** (Deut 12-26) | Overrode specific laws, dismissed others | Mixed — some ethical value, heavy priestly editing |

## What Would Jesus Say?

If Jesus were asked directly, "Should my followers read the Old Testament?" — based on everything he actually said and did, the answer would likely be something like:

*Read the prophets. They knew what God wanted — mercy, justice, love. Read the Psalms that bring you close to the Father. Go back to the beginning, before the priests added their laws and the scribes made their changes. But do not let any text — no matter how old, no matter who claims wrote it — override the two things that matter: love God, and love each other. Everything hangs on that. If something in those scrolls contradicts love, it is not from my Father. You will know the truth by its fruit.*

That's not a direct quote. But it's what every recorded word of Jesus points toward.
