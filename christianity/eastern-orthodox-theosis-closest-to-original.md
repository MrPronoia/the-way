# Which Denomination Is Closest to What Jesus Actually Taught?

**Date:** 2026-03-12
**Type:** Research synthesis
**Sources:** Athanasius — *On the Incarnation* 54.3 (c. 318 CE); Irenaeus — *Against Heresies* 5.Preface (c. 180 CE); Clement of Alexandria — *Protrepticus* 1 (c. 195 CE); Maximus the Confessor — *Ambigua* (c. 630 CE); 2 Peter 1:4 ("partakers of the divine nature"); John 10:34 (quoting Psalm 82:6 — "you are gods"); John 17:21–23; Matthew 5:48 ("Be perfect as your heavenly Father is perfect"); Didache 1–6 (c. 70 CE); James 2:14–26; Gregory of Nyssa — *On the Making of Man* (c. 379 CE); Vladimir Lossky — *The Mystical Theology of the Eastern Church* (1944)

---

## The Question Everyone Asks

With 45,000+ Christian denominations worldwide, the question is inevitable: which one is closest to what Jesus and his first followers actually believed and practiced?

The answer is not Catholic, not Protestant, and not Evangelical. Based on the historical evidence, **Eastern Orthodoxy** — despite its own institutional problems — preserves more of the original framework than any other major denomination. The reason comes down to one word: **theosis.**

---

## What Is Theosis?

Theosis (θέωσις) — also called divinization or deification — is the Eastern Orthodox doctrine that the purpose of human life is **gradual participation in the divine nature** through repentance, virtue, prayer, and service. Not just being forgiven. Not just going to heaven when you die. Actually becoming like God.

Athanasius stated it with maximum clarity:

> "God became man so that man might become God." — Athanasius, *On the Incarnation* 54.3

Irenaeus, writing even earlier:

> "The Word of God, our Lord Jesus Christ, who did, through his transcendent love, become what we are, that he might bring us to be what he is himself." — Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 5.Preface

This is not metaphor. Eastern Orthodoxy teaches that human beings can genuinely participate in God's nature — not becoming God in essence (that would be pantheism), but sharing in divine qualities through a lifelong process of transformation.

---

## Why Theosis Is Closer to Jesus's Teaching

### Jesus Taught Transformation, Not Transaction

Western Christianity (Catholic and Protestant) frames salvation as a **transaction** — Jesus pays the debt of sin, and believers receive the benefit through faith. This is Paul's framework (Romans 3:25, 5:9).

Jesus framed salvation as **transformation** — a process of becoming:

> "Be perfect, therefore, as your heavenly Father is perfect." — Matthew 5:48

> "I pray that they may all be one, as you, Father, are in me and I in you, that they also may be in us." — John 17:21

> "Is it not written in your Law, 'I have said you are gods'?" — John 10:34, quoting Psalm 82:6

Jesus didn't teach people to wait for a legal transaction. He taught them to **become** something — to participate in divine life through practice, virtue, and communion with God.

### The Didache Confirms Works-Based Soteriology

The Didache — the earliest surviving Christian instruction manual, dated to approximately 70 CE — contains **no Pauline theology whatsoever.** No blood atonement. No faith alone. No substitutionary sacrifice. Instead, it teaches:

- The Two Ways: the Way of Life and the Way of Death (chapters 1–6)
- Ethical instruction rooted in the Golden Rule
- Fasting before baptism
- The Lord's Prayer three times daily
- Communal meal practice (Eucharist without blood atonement language)

This is salvation through transformed living — exactly what theosis describes and exactly what Western Christianity replaced with Paul's transactional model.

### James Over Paul

Eastern Orthodoxy has always given more weight to the Epistle of James than Western churches. James — Jesus's brother and successor as leader of the Jerusalem church — wrote:

> "What good is it, my brothers and sisters, if someone claims to have faith but has no deeds? Can such faith save them?" — James 2:14

> "Faith without works is dead." — James 2:26

Martin Luther called James "an epistle of straw" because it contradicted Paul's faith-alone teaching. Eastern Orthodoxy never accepted that dismissal. Their soteriology — salvation through faith **expressed in** works, virtue, and transformation — aligns with James, not Luther.

## The Theosis Framework

Eastern Orthodoxy describes the process of theosis in stages:

| Stage | Description | Jesus Parallel |
|-------|-------------|---------------|
| **Katharsis** (Purification) | Cleansing from passions and sin through repentance and ascetic practice | "First clean the inside of the cup" (Matthew 23:26) |
| **Theoria** (Illumination) | Direct experience of divine light through contemplative prayer | "The eye is the lamp of the body. If your eye is healthy, your whole body is full of light" (Matthew 6:22) |
| **Theosis** (Union) | Participation in divine nature; becoming by grace what God is by nature | "I and the Father are one" (John 10:30); "that they may be one as we are one" (John 17:22) |

This three-stage process — purification, illumination, union — appears in virtually every major mystical tradition worldwide:

| Tradition | Purification | Illumination | Union |
|-----------|-------------|--------------|-------|
| **Eastern Orthodoxy** | Katharsis | Theoria | Theosis |
| **Kabbalah** | Tikkun (repair) | Binah (understanding) | Devekut (cleaving to God) |
| **Sufism** | Tawbah (repentance) | Kashf (unveiling) | Fana (annihilation in God) |
| **Hinduism** | Tapas (austerity) | Dhyana (meditation) | Samadhi (absorption) |
| **Buddhism** | Sila (ethical conduct) | Samadhi (concentration) | Prajna (wisdom/liberation) |

## What Eastern Orthodoxy Gets Right (and Wrong)

**Closer to the original:**
- Theosis as the purpose of life (transformation, not transaction)
- Works and virtue as essential to salvation (James over Paul)
- Emphasis on mystical experience and contemplative prayer
- Less systematically Pauline than Western churches
- Preservation of early church liturgical practices
- Apophatic (negative) theology — God is beyond human concepts

**Still divergent from the Nazarene original:**
- Accepts Paul's epistles as scripture (though interprets them less literally)
- Maintains Trinitarian theology (developed at Nicaea and Chalcedon, not by Jesus)
- Does not practice vegetarianism (the Nazarene standard)
- Does not reject animal sacrifice theology outright
- Institutional hierarchy absent from the earliest communities

Eastern Orthodoxy is the closest major denomination to the original — but "closest" is still not identical. The Nazarene original was more radical than any surviving institutional form of Christianity.
