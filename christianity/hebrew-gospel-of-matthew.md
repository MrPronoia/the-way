# What Was the Original Gospel of Matthew?

Every major church father from the 2nd through 5th century attests the same fact: the Gospel of Matthew was originally composed in Hebrew (or Aramaic) for a Jewish audience. This is not a fringe claim. It is one of the most unanimously agreed-upon statements in all of patristic literature. Papias, Irenaeus, Origen, Eusebius, Jerome, and Epiphanius all say it. No church father disputes it.

The implications are enormous. If Matthew's gospel existed first in Hebrew, then the Greek text we have today is a translation -- and translations can be edited. Fragments preserved by Epiphanius and the Clementine writings reveal that the Hebrew version contained explicitly anti-sacrifice sayings attributed to Jesus that do not appear in the canonical Greek Matthew. The most striking: *"I came to abolish the sacrifices. Until you cease from sacrificing, the wrath of God will not cease from you."* This saying survived only because the groups who kept the Hebrew Matthew -- the Nazarenes and Ebionites, the original Jewish followers of Jesus -- preserved it until hostile heresiologists quoted it to condemn them.

The question is not whether a Hebrew Matthew existed. The church fathers settled that. The question is what was in it -- and why the Greek version removed it.

---

## The Patristic Evidence

The following table documents every major church father attestation of a Hebrew original for Matthew's gospel. These are not secondary references or scholarly reconstructions. They are direct statements from early Christian authorities, preserved in their own writings.

| Church Father | Date | Statement | Source Text |
|---------------|------|-----------|-------------|
| **Papias of Hierapolis** | c. 60-130 CE | "Matthew composed the sayings [*ta logia*] in the Hebrew language, and each person interpreted them as he was able." | Quoted in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.16 |
| **Irenaeus of Lyon** | c. 180 CE | "Matthew also issued a written Gospel among the Hebrews in their own dialect, while Peter and Paul were preaching at Rome and laying the foundations of the Church." | *Against Heresies* 3.1.1 |
| **Origen of Alexandria** | c. 185-254 CE | "The first [Gospel] is written according to Matthew, the same that was once a publican, but afterwards an apostle of Jesus Christ, who having published it for the Jewish believers, composed it in the Hebrew language." | Quoted in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 6.25.4 |
| **Eusebius of Caesarea** | c. 260-340 CE | "Matthew had first preached to the Hebrews, and when he was on the point of going to others also, he transmitted in writing in his native tongue the Gospel according to himself, and thus supplied by writing the lack of his own presence to those from whom he was sent." | *Ecclesiastical History* 3.24.6 |
| **Jerome** | c. 347-420 CE | "Matthew, who is also Levi, and who from a publican came to be an apostle, first of all the evangelists composed a Gospel of Christ in Judea in the Hebrew language and characters for the benefit of those of the circumcision who had believed." He further states he personally saw a copy "in the library at Caesarea which the martyr Pamphilus so diligently collected." | *De Viris Illustribus* 3; *Commentary on Matthew*, Prologue |
| **Epiphanius of Salamis** | c. 310-403 CE | Reports that the Nazarenes "have the Gospel according to Matthew quite complete, in Hebrew" and that the Ebionites used a version of it. Provides direct quotations from this text, including anti-sacrifice sayings not found in the Greek. | *Panarion* 29.9.4 (Nazarenes); 30.3.7, 30.13.1-8, 30.16.5 (Ebionites) |

**What makes this significant:** There is no counter-testimony. No church father says Matthew wrote in Greek first. The unanimity is total -- and it comes from writers separated by centuries and geography, from Asia Minor to Alexandria to Caesarea to Dalmatia.

## The Anti-Sacrifice Content

The most consequential difference between the Hebrew Matthew tradition and the canonical Greek text is the anti-sacrifice material. Epiphanius, writing to refute the Ebionites, inadvertently preserved their scriptures by quoting them. The most important surviving fragment:

> **"I came to abolish the sacrifices. And until you cease from sacrificing, the wrath of God will not cease from you."**
>
> -- Epiphanius, *Panarion* 30.16.5, quoting the Ebionite Gospel (Hebrew Matthew tradition)

This saying is absent from the canonical Greek Gospel of Matthew. Yet it is consistent with everything the canonical Jesus says and does regarding sacrifice:

| Canonical Evidence | Reference | Significance |
|-------------------|-----------|--------------|
| "I desire mercy, not sacrifice" | Matthew 9:13, 12:7 (quoting Hosea 6:6) | Jesus quotes this *twice* -- the only Hebrew Bible verse he repeats in Matthew |
| Temple cleansing -- overturns tables, frees animals | Matthew 21:12-13; John 2:14-16 | A direct physical intervention against the sacrificial economy |
| Replaces Passover lamb with bread and wine | Matthew 26:26-28 | Substitutes a non-animal ritual for the central sacrificial act of Judaism |
| "Not one sparrow falls without your Father" | Matthew 10:29 | Asserts divine concern for animal life |
| "The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep" | John 10:11 | Reverses the sacrificial logic: the shepherd dies, the animal lives |

The anti-sacrifice saying preserved by Epiphanius is not an alien insertion. It is the explicit articulation of a position the canonical gospels encode implicitly.

A second fragment from the Hebrew Matthew tradition is preserved in connection with the Passover meal:

> **"I have no desire to eat the flesh of this Passover lamb with you."**
>
> -- Epiphanius, *Panarion* 30.22.4, from the Ebionite Gospel

In the canonical Greek Matthew, this refusal does not appear. Jesus simply celebrates Passover. The Hebrew tradition records a Jesus who rejected the sacrificial meal itself.

## The Three Related Texts

Scholarship identifies three overlapping but distinct texts in the Hebrew Matthew tradition. Untangling them is difficult because no complete manuscript of any has survived. What remains are quotations in the church fathers.

### Gospel of the Nazarenes

- Used by the Nazarenes, the Jewish sect directly descended from Jesus's original disciples
- Epiphanius (*Panarion* 29.9.4) states they possessed Matthew's gospel "quite complete, in Hebrew"
- Jerome reports consulting this text at the library in Caesarea and translating portions into Greek and Latin
- Contained material close to -- but not identical with -- canonical Matthew
- May be the closest surviving witness to what Papias described

### Gospel of the Ebionites

- Used by the Ebionites ("the poor ones"), a related Jewish-Christian sect
- Known entirely from Epiphanius's quotations in *Panarion* 30
- Contained the anti-sacrifice sayings quoted above
- Presented Jesus as opposing the Temple system from the beginning of his ministry
- The Ebionites rejected Paul's authority and maintained vegetarianism, Torah observance, and communal living

### Gospel of the Hebrews

- A separate text, possibly independent of the Matthew tradition
- Referenced by Clement of Alexandria, Origen, and Jerome
- Jerome (*De Viris Illustribus* 2) reports it was used by "the Nazarenes" and that he translated it
- Contains unique material including a post-resurrection appearance to James (Jerome, *De Viris Illustribus* 2)
- Some scholars argue this is the same text as the Gospel of the Nazarenes; others distinguish them

**The relationship:** All three texts circulated among Jewish followers of Jesus who maintained Hebrew/Aramaic as their liturgical language and rejected the Pauline theological framework. Whether they represent one text, two texts, or three distinct documents remains debated. What is not debated is that Jewish Christians possessed a Hebrew gospel tradition with material absent from the Greek canon.

## Why the Hebrew Original Was Lost

The Hebrew Gospel of Matthew did not vanish by accident. Its disappearance tracks precisely with the marginalization of Jewish Christianity by the Gentile-majority church.

**The timeline of suppression:**

1. **1st century:** The Jerusalem church under James the Just uses Matthew's Hebrew gospel. After the destruction of the Temple (70 CE), the community flees to Pella in the Trans-Jordan (Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 3.5.3; Epiphanius, *Panarion* 29.7.8). The Nazarene sect continues there.

2. **2nd century:** Paul's letters and the Greek gospels gain dominance in the Gentile churches across the Mediterranean. The Nazarene and Ebionite communities, still using the Hebrew text, become increasingly isolated. Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 1.26.2) already describes the Ebionites as heretics for rejecting Paul and using "only the Gospel according to Matthew."

3. **3rd-4th century:** As the church defines orthodoxy, the Hebrew Matthew becomes evidence of heresy rather than a historical artifact. The groups that preserved it are condemned. Epiphanius writes the *Panarion* specifically to catalogue and refute heresies -- his quotations of the Hebrew Matthew survive only because he was trying to disprove it.

4. **325 CE and after:** The Council of Nicaea and subsequent councils standardize doctrine. The Greek texts become the only authorized gospels. Jerome, remarkably, still finds a copy in Caesarea's library and recognizes its importance -- but by his time it is a curiosity, not an authority.

5. **5th century onward:** The Nazarene and Ebionite communities disappear from the historical record. With them goes any living transmission of the Hebrew text.

**The pattern:** The Hebrew Matthew was not refuted. It was not disproven. It was rendered irrelevant by the institutional victory of Gentile Christianity, which had no use for a Hebrew text and no sympathy for the anti-sacrifice theology it contained. The communities that preserved it were declared heretical -- and once the communities were gone, the text was gone.

This is not a conspiracy theory. It is documented institutional history. Jerome himself -- a pillar of Latin orthodoxy -- acknowledged the Hebrew text's existence, saw it with his own eyes, and translated parts of it. The question is not whether it existed but why the church chose the Greek translation over the Hebrew original.

## What This Changes

If the Hebrew Matthew preserves authentic Jesus tradition -- and the unanimous patristic testimony that Matthew wrote in Hebrew makes this plausible -- then the canonical Gospel of Matthew is a filtered version of the original.

The filter removed:

- **Explicit anti-sacrifice teaching** -- "I came to abolish the sacrifices" becomes the softer "I desire mercy, not sacrifice" (a quote from Hosea, easier to spiritualize away)
- **Jesus's refusal of the Passover lamb** -- replaced with Jesus participating in the Passover meal normally
- **Any content incompatible with Pauline theology** -- the Greek Matthew was shaped for communities already reading Paul's letters

What remains in the canonical text still points in the same direction. Jesus still cleanses the Temple. He still quotes Hosea 6:6 twice. He still replaces the lamb with bread and wine. He still says God notices every fallen sparrow. The Greek editors could soften the explicit statements, but they could not erase the trajectory without rewriting the entire gospel.

The Hebrew Matthew is the missing link between the canonical Jesus and the Nazarene Jesus -- between the teacher who "desired mercy, not sacrifice" and the teacher who said, plainly, *"I came to abolish the sacrifices."*

## Cross-Tradition Connections

The anti-sacrifice theology preserved in the Hebrew Matthew is not unique to Jewish Christianity. It resonates across multiple traditions:

- **The Essene Gospel of Peace** teaches explicit non-harm: "Kill not, neither eat the flesh of your innocent prey, lest you become the slaves of Satan" -- placing Jesus squarely in the Essene anti-sacrifice tradition. **[Note: The Essene Gospel of Peace is widely considered a modern composition by Edmond Bordeaux Szekely (first published 1937), not an authenticated ancient manuscript. No independent scholar has verified the existence of the original Aramaic/Old Slavonic manuscripts Szekely claimed to have translated. The text's provenance is disputed and it should not be treated as an ancient primary source.]**
- **The Didache** (c. 50-70 CE), the earliest Christian liturgical document, describes a Eucharist with no blood atonement language -- the cup is "the holy vine of David," the bread is "life and knowledge"
- **The Clementine Writings** (Recognitions and Homilies), attributed to the Petrine tradition, argue that sacrificial passages were interpolated into the Torah by "the lying pen of the scribes" (echoing Jeremiah 8:8)
- **The Dead Sea Scrolls** (Community Rule, 1QS 9:4-5) record the Essene practice of replacing animal sacrifice with "prayer as sacrifice" -- righteous living offered in place of slaughtered animals
- **Hinduism** (Ahimsa tradition) and **Buddhism** (First Precept) independently arrive at non-harm as foundational spiritual law
- **Pythagoras** (6th century BCE) taught vegetarianism and the kinship of all living beings -- a tradition that influenced both Greek philosophy and, through it, early Christian asceticism

The Hebrew Matthew does not stand alone. It belongs to a global convergence of traditions that identify non-violence and the rejection of blood sacrifice as markers of genuine spiritual advancement.
