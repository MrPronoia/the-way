## The Core Question

What did Jesus himself actually say about "the end times"? Did he teach anything resembling the apocalyptic framework that 200+ million evangelicals now associate with his name — the rapture, the tribulation, the Antichrist, the Book of Revelation?

The short answer: **No.** But the longer answer is more interesting — and more contested among scholars — than a simple no.

This deep dive examines seven specific questions:

1. Jesus never wrote Revelation — so who did, and what's the distinction?
2. The Olivet Discourse (Matthew 24, Mark 13, Luke 21) — was Jesus talking about the end of the world or the fall of Jerusalem in 70 CE?
3. Matthew 24:40-41, "one will be taken and one will be left" — does this support the rapture? (No. The opposite.)
4. The wheat and tares parable (Matthew 13:24-30) — who gets removed?
5. Did Jesus teach the Kingdom as present or future?
6. Matthew 16:28 — did Jesus predict his own return within a generation, and was he wrong?
7. The scholarly debate: was Jesus an apocalyptic prophet or a wisdom teacher? How does the Gospel of Thomas change the argument?

## 1. Jesus Never Wrote Revelation

This is the single most important distinction that most Christians never learn: **Jesus did not write the Book of Revelation. He is not the author. He did not dictate it. He did not endorse it.**

### Who Wrote It?

The author identifies himself as "John" (Revelation 1:1, 1:4, 1:9, 22:8). He says he was on the island of Patmos "because of the word of God and the testimony of Jesus" (1:9) — likely exiled or imprisoned.

But which John?

**The early church was not unanimous.** The question of whether "John of Patmos" was the same person as the apostle John was debated from the beginning:

- **Dionysius of Alexandria** (mid-3rd century), one of the most important early church bishops, conducted a detailed linguistic analysis and concluded the author of Revelation was **not** the same person who wrote the Gospel of John. His evidence: the Gospel is written in "nearly flawless Greek," while Revelation "contains grammatical errors and stylistic abnormalities" indicating its author "was not as familiar with the Greek language." He also noted theological differences — the Gospel emphasizes eternal life and love; Revelation emphasizes wrath and judgment. His conclusion: there were two early Christian leaders named John active in Asia Minor.

- **Eusebius of Caesarea** (c. 260-340 CE), the most important early church historian, quoted Dionysius's analysis approvingly in *Ecclesiastical History* 7.25. Eusebius classified Revelation among the **antilegomena** — books "spoken against" — noting that opinions on its canonicity were "evenly divided." He attributed it to a separate "John the Elder" or "John the Presbyter."

- **The Eastern churches were skeptical.** Gregory of Nazianzus (329-390 CE) excluded Revelation from his canonical list. The Council of Laodicea (c. 363 CE) omitted it. The Syrian church did not accept it for centuries. It was one of the last books accepted into the New Testament canon — and the doubts about it were never fully resolved.

### The Modern Scholarly Consensus

The majority of modern biblical scholars agree with Dionysius: **the author of Revelation is not the same person who wrote the Gospel of John.** The evidence:

| Feature | Gospel of John | Revelation |
|---------|---------------|------------|
| **Greek quality** | Polished, sophisticated, nearly flawless | Rough, grammatical errors, Semitic idioms forced into Greek |
| **Word for "lamb" (Jesus)** | *amnos* | *arnion* (different word entirely) |
| **Eschatology** | Realized (eternal life begins now through believing) | Futurist (cosmic judgment is coming) |
| **Christology** | Jesus as Logos, Word, divine self-revelation | Jesus as warrior king returning in vengeance |
| **Tone** | Meditative, mystical, focused on love | Violent, graphic, focused on wrath and destruction |
| **Self-identification** | Author never names himself | Author explicitly names himself "John" four times |

**The bottom line:** Revelation was written by an early Christian prophet named John — probably a Jewish-Christian leader in Asia Minor — writing around 90-96 CE during the reign of the emperor Domitian. He was not Jesus. He was not writing down Jesus's words. He was recording his own visionary experience, shaped by his own theology, his own political context (Roman persecution), and his own reading of Jewish apocalyptic literature (especially Daniel, Ezekiel, and 1 Enoch).

### What This Means

When someone says "Jesus predicted the apocalypse" and cites Revelation, they are attributing to Jesus a text he did not write, authorize, or necessarily agree with. The author of Revelation was writing about **his own time** — Rome, Nero, Domitian, the persecution of his community — using the symbolic language of Jewish apocalyptic literature. Bart Ehrman's analysis in *Armageddon* (2023) demonstrates this conclusively:

- **666 = Nero.** "Caesar Nero" in Hebrew gematria equals 666. Spelled without the final *nun* (Nero Caesar), it equals 616 — which is the alternate reading found in some ancient manuscripts. Both point to the same person.
- **"Babylon" = Rome.** The "great city" sitting on seven hills (Revelation 17:9) that has "dominion over the kings of the earth" (17:18). Rome sits on seven hills. Rome destroyed Jerusalem in 70 CE, just as Babylon had in 586 BCE.
- **The seven churches** (Revelation 2-3) are real churches in real cities in Asia Minor. John was writing to his own community about their own situation.

Revelation is a first-century political-theological text written in apocalyptic code. It was not a prediction about the 21st century.

## 2. The Olivet Discourse — What Was Jesus Actually Talking About?

The Olivet Discourse (Matthew 24, Mark 13, Luke 21) is Jesus's longest sustained speech about future events in the canonical gospels. It is also the most misread passage in the entire New Testament — the primary source text for every "end times" prediction from Darby to Hal Lindsey to *Left Behind*.

### The Setting

Jesus and his disciples are leaving the Temple in Jerusalem. The disciples point out the grandeur of the buildings. Jesus responds:

> "Do you see all these great buildings? Not one stone here will be left on another; every one will be thrown down." (Mark 13:2)

The disciples then ask — and the phrasing of their question is critically important:

- **Mark 13:4:** "Tell us, when will these things happen? And what will be the sign that they are all about to be fulfilled?"
- **Matthew 24:3:** "Tell us, when will these things happen, and what will be the sign of your coming and of the end of the age?"
- **Luke 21:7:** "Teacher, when will these things happen? And what will be the sign that they are about to take place?"

Notice: **Mark and Luke record a single question about "these things" — the destruction of the Temple.** Matthew adds "and of the end of the age." This is significant because Matthew is the most eschatologically developed of the Synoptics. The question of whether Jesus was talking about one event or two depends partly on which Gospel you trust most.

### What Jesus Describes

The discourse includes:

1. **Wars, earthquakes, famines** (Mark 13:7-8) — "the beginning of birth pains"
2. **Persecution of followers** (Mark 13:9-13)
3. **The "abomination of desolation"** standing in the holy place (Mark 13:14, citing Daniel 9:27)
4. **Great distress** / tribulation (Mark 13:19)
5. **False messiahs and false prophets** (Mark 13:21-22)
6. **Cosmic signs** — sun darkened, moon not giving light, stars falling (Mark 13:24-25)
7. **The Son of Man coming on clouds** (Mark 13:26, citing Daniel 7:13)
8. **"This generation will not pass away until all these things have taken place"** (Mark 13:30)

### The Four Interpretive Options

| View | Position | Key Scholars |
|------|----------|-------------|
| **Preterist** | Everything was fulfilled by 70 CE — the destruction of Jerusalem and the Temple | R.C. Sproul, N.T. Wright (partial), Kenneth Gentry |
| **Futurist** | Everything (or most of it) refers to the distant future — a literal tribulation, literal cosmic destruction, literal second coming | Darby, Scofield, Hal Lindsey, Tim LaHaye |
| **Historicist** | The prophecies map onto the sweep of church history from the 1st century to the present | Luther, Calvin, many Reformers |
| **Hybrid / Telescoping** | Some parts refer to 70 CE, others to the end of history — Jesus "telescoped" near and far events | Most mainstream scholars |

### The Case That Jesus Was Talking About 70 CE

The evidence that the Olivet Discourse is primarily about the fall of Jerusalem in 70 CE is substantial:

**1. The triggering question is about the Temple.** Jesus says the Temple will be destroyed. The disciples ask when. Everything that follows is an answer to *that* question.

**2. "This generation will not pass away."** Mark 13:30 / Matthew 24:34 / Luke 21:32. A "generation" in Jewish reckoning is roughly 40 years. Jesus spoke these words around 30 CE. Jerusalem fell in 70 CE. That is 40 years. If "this generation" means the people standing in front of him, the prophecy was fulfilled precisely.

**3. The "abomination of desolation."** The phrase comes from Daniel 9:27, 11:31, and 12:11, originally referring to Antiochus IV Epiphanes desecrating the Temple in 167 BCE. In 70 CE, the Roman armies carried standards bearing the emperor's image into the Temple before destroying it. Luke's version makes this explicit: "When you see Jerusalem surrounded by armies, then know that its desolation has come near" (Luke 21:20). Luke removes the cryptic Daniel reference and replaces it with a plain description of the Roman siege.

**4. The advice to flee.** "Let those who are in Judea flee to the mountains" (Mark 13:14). This is specific, practical, geographic advice for people living in a specific place at a specific time. It makes sense as advice for the Jewish War of 66-70 CE. It makes no sense as advice for a cosmic end-of-the-world event.

**5. "Pray that your flight may not be in winter or on a Sabbath" (Matthew 24:20).** Sabbath travel restrictions only matter if you are a Jew living under Jewish law in Judea. This is local, historical advice — not universal eschatological instruction.

**6. The cosmic language is prophetic metaphor.** "The sun will be darkened, and the moon will not give its light; the stars will fall from heaven" (Matthew 24:29). This language is drawn directly from the Old Testament prophets — and in every prior case, it refers to historical political upheavals, not literal astronomical events:

- Isaiah 13:10 uses identical language about the fall of **Babylon**
- Isaiah 34:4 uses it about the judgment on **Edom**
- Ezekiel 32:7-8 uses it about the fall of **Egypt**
- Joel 2:31 uses it about the **Day of the Lord** (applied by Peter in Acts 2 to Pentecost — already fulfilled)

When Isaiah said "the stars of heaven will not give their light" about Babylon's fall, no one reads that as a prediction that literal stars went dark in 539 BCE. It is prophetic hyperbole — the standard Jewish way of saying "a world-shattering political event is happening." Jesus is using the same convention.

**7. "The Son of Man coming on clouds" draws from Daniel 7:13-14.** In Daniel, the "one like a son of man" comes to the Ancient of Days and receives dominion. The movement is **upward** — toward God — not downward to earth. N.T. Wright argues this is a vindication scene, not a descent scene. The destruction of the Temple would prove that Jesus was right about the corrupt Temple system and that God had vindicated his message.

### What This Means

If the Olivet Discourse is about 70 CE — and the textual evidence strongly supports this — then the primary passage used to construct "end times" theology is actually about a historical event that already happened. Jesus predicted the fall of Jerusalem within a generation. He was right. The Romans destroyed the Temple in 70 CE, exactly as described.

The futurist/dispensationalist reading requires ignoring "this generation" (or redefining "generation" to mean something other than the people alive at the time), ignoring the Temple context, ignoring the geographic specificity, and reading prophetic metaphor as literal astronomy. It is the weaker reading by every standard of biblical interpretation.

## 3. "One Will Be Taken and One Will Be Left" — The Great Reversal

> "Then two men will be in the field; one will be taken and one will be left. Two women will be grinding at the mill; one will be taken and one will be left." (Matthew 24:40-41)

This is perhaps the most famous "rapture" verse in the Bible. It is the image behind *Left Behind*, behind every rapture movie, behind every bumper sticker that says "In case of rapture, this car will be unmanned."

**The interpretation is exactly backwards.**

### The Context Everyone Ignores

The verses immediately preceding Matthew 24:40-41 establish the interpretive framework:

> "As it was in the days of Noah, so it will be at the coming of the Son of Man. For in the days before the flood, people were eating and drinking, marrying and giving in marriage, up to the day Noah entered the ark; and they knew nothing about what would happen until the flood came and **took them all away**. That is how it will be at the coming of the Son of Man." (Matthew 24:37-39)

**Who was "taken" in Noah's flood?** The wicked. The people who drowned. The ones swept away in judgment.

**Who was "left"?** Noah and his family. The righteous. They remained on the earth and inherited it.

Jesus then says: "One will be taken and one will be left." He has just established that being "taken" = being removed in judgment (like the flood victims), and being "left" = surviving (like Noah).

**The rapture reading inverts this entirely.** It claims the "taken" are the blessed — snatched to heaven. But Jesus's own analogy, stated three verses earlier, says the opposite: the taken are the destroyed, and the left are the saved.

### Luke's Devastating Clarification

Luke's version of this passage (Luke 17:34-37) includes a follow-up question the disciples ask — a question Matthew omits:

> The disciples asked him, "Where, Lord?" He replied, "Where there is a dead body, there the vultures will gather." (Luke 17:37)

The disciples ask: where are the "taken" ones going? Jesus's answer: **to where the corpses are.** To where vultures circle dead bodies. This is a reference to judgment and death — not to heaven. As J. Richard Middleton argues: "Luke 17:37 provides decisive textual support. When disciples ask 'Where, Lord?' regarding those taken, Jesus responds with a Gehenna reference — clearly referencing judgment, not heaven."

### The Greek Words

The Greek word for "took" in verse 39 (the flood sweeping people away) is *airo* — "to take up, to take away" in a destructive sense.

The Greek word for "taken" in verses 40-41 is *paralambano* — "to take alongside, to receive." Some scholars argue this word shift indicates a different kind of "taking." But Jason Staples proposes an alternative rendering using "seized" and "released" rather than "taken" and "left," arguing this better captures the evaluative judgment implied: "better to be released than seized." The flood context governs the meaning regardless of which Greek verb is used.

### The Bottom Line

Matthew 24:40-41 is not a rapture text. In context, it is a judgment text. The "taken" are removed in judgment. The "left" remain to inherit the earth. Every rapture sermon built on this verse has the meaning reversed.

As Middleton concludes: "If the two primary scriptural supports for the rapture — Matthew 24 and 1 Thessalonians 4 — fail, the rapture doctrine lacks biblical foundation."

## 4. The Wheat and the Tares — Who Gets Removed?

> "The kingdom of heaven is like a man who sowed good seed in his field. But while everyone was sleeping, his enemy came and sowed weeds among the wheat, and went away." (Matthew 13:24-25)

The servants ask if they should pull up the weeds. The master says no — wait until the harvest:

> "Let both grow together until the harvest. At that time I will tell the harvesters: **First collect the weeds and tie them in bundles to be burned; then gather the wheat into my barn.**" (Matthew 13:30)

Jesus later explains the parable explicitly (Matthew 13:36-43):

- The good seed = children of the kingdom
- The weeds (tares) = children of the evil one
- The harvest = the end of the age
- The harvesters = angels

> "The Son of Man will send out his angels, and they will **weed out of his kingdom** everything that causes sin and all who do evil. They will throw them into the blazing furnace. **Then the righteous will shine like the sun in the kingdom of their Father.**" (Matthew 13:41-43)

### The Anti-Rapture Parable

The sequence is explicit:

1. **First** the weeds (wicked) are gathered and burned
2. **Then** the wheat (righteous) remain and shine in the kingdom

The righteous **stay**. The wicked are **removed**.

This is the exact opposite of rapture theology, which claims the righteous are removed first (raptured to heaven) while the wicked remain (left behind for tribulation). Jesus's own parable describes the opposite sequence: **the wicked are removed first, and the righteous inherit the earth.**

If the word "rapture" means "to be gathered up," the only ones "raptured" in the Parable of the Tares are the wicked — and they are gathered up for destruction.

## 5. Did Jesus Teach the Kingdom as Present or Future?

This is the central question. Everything else — the Olivet Discourse, the rapture verses, the apocalyptic debate — hinges on whether Jesus understood the Kingdom of God as something coming later or something already here.

### The "Kingdom Is Here NOW" Texts

**Luke 17:20-21** — The most explicit statement:

> Being asked by the Pharisees when the kingdom of God would come, he answered them, "The kingdom of God is not coming with signs to be observed, nor will they say, 'Look, here it is!' or 'There!' For behold, the kingdom of God is in the midst of you [*entos hymon*]."

The Greek phrase *entos hymon* can mean "within you" or "among you" / "in your midst." The translation debate is significant:

- **"Within you"** = the Kingdom is an interior, spiritual reality — already present inside every person. This aligns with Thomas, Hermeticism, and the mystical reading.
- **"Among you"** / **"in your midst"** = the Kingdom is present because Jesus himself is present among them. This is the majority scholarly translation.

**Note:** The objection that Jesus wouldn't say the Kingdom is "within" the Pharisees (who were his opponents) assumes he was speaking only to the Pharisees as individuals. But Jesus regularly made universal statements about human nature. Either translation supports the same core point: **the Kingdom is not a future event to be watched for. It is already present. It will not come "with signs to be observed."**

This single verse demolishes the entire dispensationalist framework, which is built on watching for signs.

**Gospel of Thomas, Saying 3:**

> "If your leaders say to you, 'Look, the kingdom is in the sky,' then the birds of the sky will precede you. If they say to you, 'It is in the sea,' then the fish will precede you. Rather, **the kingdom is within you and it is outside of you.** When you know yourselves, then you will be known."

Thomas resolves the *entos hymon* debate: the Kingdom is *both* within and without. It is not in the sky (no rapture up). It is not somewhere else. It is here, now, accessible through self-knowledge.

**Gospel of Thomas, Saying 113:**

> His disciples said to him, "When will the kingdom come?" He said, "**It will not come by watching for it.** It will not be said, 'Look, here!' or 'Look, there!' Rather, the Father's kingdom **is spread out upon the earth, and people don't see it.**"

This is the parallel to Luke 17:20-21 — but even more explicit. The Kingdom is not coming. It is already here. The problem is not its absence but human blindness.

**Gospel of Thomas, Saying 51:**

> His disciples said to him, "When will the rest of the dead come about, and when will the new world come?" He said to them, "**What you are looking for has already come, but you do not know it.**"

**Matthew 12:28 / Luke 11:20:**

> "But if I drive out demons by the Spirit of God, then **the kingdom of God has come upon you.**"

*Has come* — past tense. Already here.

**Luke 10:9:**

> "Heal the sick who are there and tell them, '**The kingdom of God has come near to you.**'"

**Mark 1:15:**

> "**The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God has come near**; repent, and believe in the good news."

### The "Kingdom Is Future" Texts

In fairness, some canonical sayings do carry future or imminent language:

**Mark 9:1:**

> "Truly I tell you, there are some standing here who will not taste death before they see that **the kingdom of God has come with power.**"

**The Lord's Prayer (Matthew 6:10):**

> "Your kingdom come, your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven."

**Mark 14:25:**

> "Truly I tell you, I will not drink again from the fruit of the vine until that day **when I drink it new in the kingdom of God.**"

### How to Reconcile These

The most sophisticated scholarly framework is **"inaugurated eschatology"** — the Kingdom has already arrived in Jesus's ministry but is not yet fully realized. George Eldon Ladd (Harvard PhD, Fuller Seminary) developed this framework: the Kingdom is "already but not yet." It is present as a spiritual reality, but its full manifestation is still unfolding.

But there is another option: **the "future" sayings may be later additions.** If Thomas preserves an earlier, independent tradition (as Koester, DeConick, Patterson, and Crossan argue), then the historical Jesus may have taught a purely present Kingdom, and the future/apocalyptic layer was added later — either by the gospel writers or by the early church community that was processing Jesus's death and needed to explain why the Kingdom hadn't fully arrived in the way they expected.

The chronological evidence supports this:

| Source | Approximate Date | Kingdom Teaching |
|--------|-----------------|-----------------|
| Gospel of Thomas (early layer) | ~50-70 CE | Purely present: "spread out upon the earth" |
| Q Source (reconstructed) | ~50-60 CE | Mixed: some present, some future/imminent |
| Mark | ~70 CE | Mixed: present but also "coming with power" |
| Matthew | ~80-85 CE | Escalated: more apocalyptic than Mark |
| Luke | ~80-85 CE | De-escalated: softens some apocalyptic elements |
| John | ~90-100 CE | Almost entirely realized: eternal life begins NOW through believing |

The pattern: **the further you get from the original teaching, the more the apocalyptic content either escalates (Matthew) or begins to fade (Luke, John). Thomas, if early, represents the non-apocalyptic baseline.**

## 6. Matthew 16:28 — A Failed Prediction?

> "Truly I tell you, some who are standing here will not taste death before they see the Son of Man coming in his kingdom." (Matthew 16:28)

> "Truly I tell you, there are some standing here who will not taste death before they see that the kingdom of God has come with power." (Mark 9:1)

> "Truly I tell you, some who are standing here will not taste death before they see the kingdom of God." (Luke 9:27)

### The Problem

If this is a prediction of the Second Coming — a literal, visible, cosmic return of Christ — it failed. Everyone standing there died. The Second Coming (as traditionally conceived) did not happen within their lifetimes. This is the verse that led Albert Schweitzer to declare Jesus a "failed apocalyptic prophet" in 1906.

### The Proposed Solutions

**1. The Transfiguration.** The most common apologetic answer: all three Gospels place the Transfiguration immediately after this saying (about a week later). Peter, James, and John saw Jesus glorified on the mountain — "the Son of Man coming in his kingdom" in a visionary sense. This is the position of most conservative commentators. It works grammatically but feels like a stretch — "some standing here will not taste death" implies a longer timeframe than six days.

**2. The Fall of Jerusalem (70 CE).** N.T. Wright and preterist scholars argue that "the Son of Man coming in his kingdom" refers to the vindication of Jesus's message through the destruction of the Temple — proof that the old system was over and Jesus's Kingdom teaching was validated. "Some standing here" would still be alive in 70 CE. This reading draws on Daniel 7:13-14, where the Son of Man's "coming" is an upward movement to receive authority, not a downward descent to earth.

**3. Pentecost / the resurrection.** Some argue the "coming in power" refers to the outpouring of the Holy Spirit at Pentecost (Acts 2) or to the resurrection itself — both of which some disciples witnessed.

**4. It was a failed prediction.** Schweitzer's position, adopted by Ehrman: Jesus genuinely expected the Kingdom to arrive in its fullness within his contemporaries' lifetimes. It did not. This is the honest reading if you take the words at face value and reject all the harmonizing explanations.

**5. It was added by the early church.** A tradition that originated not with Jesus but with the early community, which expected an imminent return and retroactively attributed that expectation to Jesus.

### What This Reveals

The diversity of explanations itself reveals something important: **the canonical gospels contain material that is very difficult to reconcile with the later church's theology.** If you believe in a future Second Coming that hasn't happened yet, Matthew 16:28 is a problem. If you believe Jesus was teaching a present Kingdom (Thomas's position), the verse either doesn't belong to the original Jesus tradition or refers to something other than a literal cosmic return.

The most intellectually honest options are: (a) Jesus was talking about the fall of Jerusalem and was right, (b) Jesus was talking about a cosmic event and was wrong, or (c) the saying was added by the early community and doesn't represent Jesus's actual teaching.

## 7. The Scholarly Debate: Apocalyptic Prophet or Wisdom Teacher?

This is the biggest unresolved question in historical Jesus scholarship. The answer determines everything.

### Camp 1: Jesus as Apocalyptic Prophet

**Albert Schweitzer** (1906): Launched the modern debate with *The Quest of the Historical Jesus*. Schweitzer argued that Jesus was a Jewish apocalyptic prophet who genuinely expected God to intervene in history and establish the Kingdom within his own lifetime. When it didn't happen, Jesus "threw himself on the wheels of history" — provoking his own crucifixion as a way to force God's hand. In Schweitzer's reading, Jesus was a tragic figure: a brilliant teacher whose core prediction failed.

**Bart Ehrman** (1999, 2023): The most prominent contemporary advocate. In *Jesus: Apocalyptic Prophet of the New Millennium*, Ehrman argues:
- The earliest sources (Mark, Paul's letters, the reconstructed Q source) all portray an apocalyptic Jesus
- Jesus's ministry began with John the Baptist, who was unambiguously apocalyptic ("the axe is laid to the root of the trees" — Matthew 3:10)
- The earliest church was apocalyptic (Paul expected Jesus to return in his own lifetime — 1 Thessalonians 4:15-17)
- If Jesus's predecessor (John) was apocalyptic and his followers were apocalyptic, the simplest explanation is that Jesus himself was apocalyptic
- The apocalyptic sayings pass the "criterion of dissimilarity" — they are embarrassing to the later church, which had to explain why the end didn't come, so they are unlikely to have been invented

**Dale Allison** (1998): In *Jesus of Nazareth: Millenarian Prophet*, Allison identifies specific sayings that declare "ordinary time has almost run out": Mark 9:1, Mark 13:30, Matthew 10:23. He argues Matthew 23:34-35 / Luke 11:49-51, declaring that "all blood shed from the foundation of the world will be required of this generation," implies that "this generation" is the last generation. Allison sees Jesus as a millenarian prophet similar to other Jewish apocalyptic figures of his era.

**E.P. Sanders** (1993): In *The Historical Figure of Jesus*, Sanders places Jesus firmly within Jewish eschatological expectation while acknowledging the complexity of his teaching.

**Paula Fredriksen, Geza Vermes**: Also support the apocalyptic prophet model.

### Camp 2: Jesus as Wisdom Teacher / Non-Apocalyptic

**John Dominic Crossan** (1991): In *The Historical Jesus*, Crossan argues Jesus was not an apocalyptic prophet but a **wisdom teacher** who preached a "sapiential eschatology" — a present Kingdom that could be entered through wisdom, justice, and right action in the here and now. Crossan's Jesus is closer to a Cynic philosopher than a doomsday preacher. The Kingdom is a "brokerless Kingdom" — no intermediary, no institutional hierarchy, no waiting. Jesus makes no claim to be its king. He simply points at it and says: it's here, enter it.

Crossan argues Jesus explicitly rejected John the Baptist's apocalyptic framework. John preached coming judgment; Jesus preached present transformation. The shift from apocalyptic to sapiential eschatology was deliberate.

**Marcus Borg** (1994): In *Meeting Jesus Again for the First Time*, Borg describes the pre-Easter Jesus using four characteristics: (1) **spirit person** — someone with direct experiential awareness of God, (2) **teacher of alternative wisdom** — using parables to subvert conventional thinking, (3) **social prophet** — challenging economic, political, and religious elites, (4) **movement founder** — creating an alternative community. Borg's Jesus is "cut free from the apocalyptic and eschatological ethos of first-century Judaism." He posits "a noneschatological, present-tense, this-world-affirming Jesus."

Borg's two keywords for Jesus: **spirit** and **compassion** — not judgment and wrath.

**The Jesus Seminar** (1985-2006): The collaborative scholarly project led by Robert Funk, Crossan, and Borg. The Seminar voted on the authenticity of Jesus's sayings using colored beads (red = authentic, pink = probably, gray = probably not, black = not authentic). Their conclusion: **most of the apocalyptic sayings in the Gospels are not authentic** — they were added by the early church community, which was processing Jesus's death through an apocalyptic framework borrowed from Jewish tradition and Paul's theology.

### How the Gospel of Thomas Changes Everything

The Gospel of Thomas is the single most important piece of evidence in this debate. Here's why:

**If Thomas is early and independent** (Koester, DeConick, Patterson, Crossan — dating the core layer to ~50-70 CE):

- Thomas contains **zero apocalyptic content**. No Second Coming. No Last Judgment. No tribulation. No rapture. No cosmic destruction.
- Thomas's Jesus explicitly teaches against watching for future events: "It will not come by watching for it" (Saying 113), "What you are looking for has already come" (Saying 51)
- Thomas's Kingdom is entirely present, entirely interior, entirely accessible through self-knowledge
- If this represents an independent tradition as old as or older than Mark, then we have a window into a Jesus movement that was **completely non-apocalyptic** — suggesting the apocalyptic layer was added later

**If Thomas is late and dependent on the Synoptics** (Goodacre, Gathercole — dating to ~120-140 CE):

- Thomas represents a later de-apocalypticizing of Jesus's teaching
- A 2nd-century community deliberately removed the apocalyptic content
- This still proves the apocalyptic material was controversial enough that some early Christians rejected it

**Ehrman's counterargument:** The apocalyptic material cannot simply be dismissed because Thomas lacks it. Mark, Q, M, and L all contain apocalyptic sayings. Thomas is one source; the canonical tradition has multiple independent sources. You cannot privilege one non-apocalyptic source over multiple apocalyptic ones. Ehrman also notes the chronological argument: when you arrange the sources chronologically, the apocalyptic preaching is **prominent in the earliest sources** (Mark, Paul, Q), begins to **fade** in later sources (Luke softens it, John largely replaces it with realized eschatology), and is **completely absent** in Thomas. Ehrman reads this as later communities removing embarrassing failed predictions — not as Thomas preserving the original.

**Crossan's counterargument to Ehrman:** The Q source can be divided into layers. The earliest layer of Q (Q1) is **wisdom sayings with no apocalyptic content** — very similar to Thomas. The apocalyptic material appears in the later Q layers (Q2, Q3). So even within Q, the pattern holds: the earliest material is non-apocalyptic, and the apocalyptic content was added later.

**DeConick's "rolling corpus" model:** Thomas was not composed all at once. It has an early kernel (~30-50 CE) with later accretions (~60-120 CE). The early kernel is the non-apocalyptic wisdom teaching. Some later sayings in Thomas may reflect the community's engagement with (and rejection of) apocalyptic ideas.

### The Synthesis Position

The most nuanced position — and the one most consistent with the full evidence — may be something like this:

**Jesus taught primarily about a present Kingdom** — accessible through transformation of consciousness, right action, and direct experience of the divine. This is the teaching preserved in Thomas, in the earliest Q layer, in Luke 17:20-21, and in the "Kingdom within" strand of all four canonical gospels.

**Jesus also used apocalyptic language and imagery** — because he was a first-century Jew operating within a culture saturated with apocalyptic expectation. But the question is whether he used that language **literally** (as Schweitzer and Ehrman argue) or **metaphorically/prophetically** (as the Old Testament prophets did when they described stars falling to announce political events).

**The apocalyptic framework was amplified after his death** — by a community that needed to explain why their teacher had been killed and the Kingdom hadn't fully arrived. The answer: it's coming soon. Paul took this further. Mark took it further still. Matthew escalated it. And then the trajectory reversed — Luke softened it, John replaced it with realized eschatology, Thomas rejected it entirely.

This synthesis explains why both camps have evidence: the apocalyptic sayings exist in the canonical tradition because Jesus operated within an apocalyptic culture and may have used its language, but the core teaching was about present transformation — and as the tradition developed, the apocalyptic frame was both amplified by some communities and rejected by others.

## Summary: What Jesus Actually Taught vs. What Got Attributed to Him

| Claim | What Jesus Actually Said | What Got Attributed to Him Later |
|-------|------------------------|--------------------------------|
| **The Rapture** | Never mentioned it. The wheat-and-tares parable (Matthew 13) and Noah analogy (Matthew 24:37-41) describe the wicked being removed and the righteous remaining. | Darby (1830s) invented the rapture from Paul's failed prophecy in 1 Thessalonians 4:15-17. Not taught by Jesus, not believed by the early church. |
| **The Book of Revelation** | Did not write it. Did not dictate it. A separate author (John of Patmos) wrote it about Rome/Nero ~90-96 CE. | Attributed to Jesus's authority by inclusion in the canon; used as if Jesus endorsed every word. |
| **End of the World** | Taught about the end of the Temple system and predicted the fall of Jerusalem (fulfilled 70 CE). The Olivet Discourse is about a historical event, not a cosmic one. | The early church and later interpreters reframed local/historical predictions as universal/cosmic ones. |
| **The Kingdom** | "The Kingdom is within you / among you" (Luke 17:21). "The Kingdom is spread out upon the earth, and people don't see it" (Thomas 113). "What you are looking for has already come" (Thomas 51). Present, here, now. | Paul: "We who are still alive will be caught up in the air" (1 Thess 4:17). Future, elsewhere, later. Darby: a seven-dispensation framework with the Kingdom delayed until after the rapture and tribulation. |
| **Signs of the Times** | "The kingdom of God is not coming with signs to be observed" (Luke 17:20). Explicitly said NOT to watch for signs. | Dispensationalism built an entire industry around watching for signs — blood moons, wars, natural disasters, political events mapped onto Revelation. |
| **Who Gets Removed** | The wicked (wheat and tares — Matthew 13:30). The unprepared (Noah analogy — Matthew 24:37-39). The "taken" in judgment, not the righteous. | Rapture theology: the righteous are removed first, the wicked are "left behind." Exact inversion of Jesus's own parables. |
| **Matthew 16:28** | Probably about the fall of Jerusalem (fulfilled), the Transfiguration (fulfilled), or a failed prediction (Schweitzer). | Used as evidence of an imminent Second Coming — but then requires explaining why it didn't happen. |

## Cross-Tradition Connections

Every mystical tradition agrees with the "Kingdom NOW" Jesus and disagrees with the apocalyptic framework:

| Tradition | Teaching | Parallel to Jesus |
|-----------|---------|------------------|
| **Gospel of Thomas** | "The Kingdom is spread out upon the earth, and people don't see it" (Saying 113) | Direct parallel to Luke 17:20-21 |
| **Advaita Vedanta** | Brahman is always already the case — liberation is recognizing what is already true | The Kingdom is already here — seeing it is the transformation |
| **Buddhism** | Nirvana and samsara are the same thing rightly seen | The Kingdom is not elsewhere — it is this world perceived without delusion |
| **Taoism** | "The Tao flows everywhere, to the left and to the right" (TTC Ch. 34) | The Kingdom is spread out upon the earth |
| **Hermeticism** | "As above, so below; as within, so without" — the divine is already present in all things | "Split a piece of wood; I am there" (Thomas 77) |
| **Kabbalah** | Sparks of holiness are trapped in all matter, awaiting recognition | The Kingdom is hidden in plain sight |
| **Law of One** | "Look within. The Creator is within" (Ra: Session 10) | "The kingdom is within you" (Luke 17:21) |
| **Sufism** | The Beloved is already present — the veil is your own inattention | The Kingdom will not come "by watching for it" — you are looking past it |

The apocalyptic framework — a future cosmic judgment, a world getting worse by design, believers extracted before destruction — is **unique to the Pauline/dispensational tradition.** No other major mystical tradition teaches it. Every tradition that produces genuine transformative practice emphasizes present realization over future rescue.

### 2. Present Transformation vs. Future Escape — Confirmed as a Perennial Pattern

This is not just a Christian issue. The tension between "do the work NOW" and "wait to be rescued LATER" appears in every tradition that gets institutionalized. The original teacher says the Kingdom/liberation/enlightenment is here. The institution says it's coming later (and you need us to get there). Dispensationalism is the most extreme version, but the pattern is universal.

### 3. The Scholarly Debate Is Not Settled — And That's Important

Honest research requires acknowledging that scholars like Ehrman and Allison make a serious case for an apocalyptic Jesus. The evidence is not one-sided. The strongest position is probably the synthesis: Jesus taught primarily about present transformation, used apocalyptic language metaphorically (as the prophets did), and the literal-apocalyptic reading was amplified by later communities. But this is an argument, not a certainty.

### 4. The Gospel of Thomas Is the Key Text

Thomas is the single most important document for understanding what Jesus actually taught about the Kingdom. If it is early and independent — and serious scholars argue it is — it represents a Jesus tradition that is completely free of apocalyptic eschatology. A tradition of **present Kingdom, self-knowledge, direct experience, no waiting.**
