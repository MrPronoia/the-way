## Summary

This is a personal-reflective video — closer to an essay than a lecture — in which Tabor argues that **the Hebrew Bible and the New Testament are a troubled coupling**, not two halves of one coherent revelation. The two corpora come out of different worlds. The Hebrew Bible reflects an ancient Near Eastern prophetic faith centered on the one Creator, on humans made of dust and capable of moral choice, on a global ethical mission for Israel as light to the nations, and on a future kingdom of righteousness that is *promised but not guaranteed*. The New Testament, by contrast, is a thoroughly Hellenized literature soaked in dualistic cosmology, an apocalyptic certainty that the end is imminent, a cosmic-Christ Christology featuring a pre-existent divine Son seated at the right hand of God, and a salvation framework about escaping this world for a heavenly one. Tabor finds himself drawn to the former and put off by the latter — and he says so plainly.

The video also serves as the personal preface to Tabor's book *Restoring Abrahamic Faith* (4th edition). He traces his journey from an evangelical upbringing where the Old Testament was treated as "mere background" and "the old law done away," through his academic career, to his settled conviction that Jesus must be understood as a Jew of his time who lived and died for his ancestral Hebrew faith — what Paula Fredriksen calls "that brief period when Christians were Jews." The Hebrew Bible closes open-ended — with ambiguity, doubt, lament, and the final word "*let him go up*" (2 Chronicles 36:23). The New Testament closes the questions with a loud, resounding yes — and that closure, Tabor argues, is where the trouble lives.

## Key Teachings

### What the Hebrew Bible Actually Teaches

The Hebrew Bible's vision, stripped to its core: one true and living Creator — Tillich's "conscious ground of our being." A nameless God Most High who calls Abraham and his extended family for a *global historical mission* — light to the nations. Humans are mortal, made of dust, taken seriously precisely because death and history are real. They are made in the image of God, capable of reason, capable of free choice for good or evil. God reveals divine laws — the way for humankind that brings blessings, not curses. The human race is shown in its wayward and sinful condition, and yet there are those who love and follow this God in the midst of it all. Their mission is to be a witness to the nations and to bring about righteousness, justice, and peace on earth.

Key Hebrew Bible texture Tabor emphasizes: **the questioning**. Psalms, Job, Ecclesiastes, Habakkuk, Psalm 89 — the texts themselves are full of lament, doubt, despair, and unanswered queries. The ways of God are far from clear. There is expectation of intervention, longing for help and care, but the Hebrew canon **rejects a simplistic view of things**. Much is left open — individual immortality, broader historical schemes. Even the prophetic restoration promises come wrapped in lament about when and whether they will ever come.

### The Open-Ended Hebrew Canon: "Let Him Go Up"

The Hebrew canon's final word is 2 Chronicles 36:23 — Cyrus's decree ending with the phrase *let him go up*. Tabor reads this as symbolically loaded. **All is open-ended.** History is linear — it rhymes but does not repeat itself. The future offers all things new. Israel's future, and that of "our troubled but wonderful planet," is still unwritten. Individuals are called to respond — to *go up* to the land of promise in terms of these ideals of peace, justice, love, truth, and righteousness filling the earth as the waters cover the seas. The canon closes mid-invitation. The work is unfinished, by design.

### The New Testament's Hellenistic Inheritance

The New Testament comes out of a wholly different milieu. Tabor names four features that mark it as a Hellenistic religious literature, not a continuation of the Hebrew prophetic stream:

1. **Vast expanded dualistic cosmos** — heaven/earth, flesh/spirit, this world/world to come
2. **Emphasis on immortality and personal salvation** — escaping this world for a better heavenly life
3. **Absolute apocalyptic worldview** — all will soon be resolved by decisive divine intervention, the end of the age, the last great judgment, the eternal kingdom
4. **Cosmic Christology** — Jesus the human transformed into the pre-existent divine Son of God who sits at the right hand of God and is Lord of the cosmos

Add the whole complex: multiple levels of heaven, fate, angels, demons, miracles, magic. It is, Tabor says, **as if all the questions the Hebrew Bible only begins to explore — theodicy, justice, human purpose, history, death, sin — are suddenly answered with a loud and resounding yes**. There is little struggle left. Few haunting questions. No genuine tragedy or meaningless suffering. All is guaranteed. All will shortly be worked out.

### Why Demythologization Cannot Rescue Pauline Christianity

Various attempts have been made to reinterpret early Christianity for modern people — usually in terms of ethics or some existential core of truth. Tabor's response: early Christianity rests on **two essential points that resist easy demythologization**:

1. It is built on an **apocalyptic view of history**
2. It is built on an **evaluation of Jesus as a Hellenistic deity** — a pre-existent divine savior God in whom all ultimate meaning rests

If these two pillars are unacceptable in the modern world, or incompatible with the fundamental Hebrew view of things, the whole system becomes difficult if not superfluous. You cannot extract the ethics and keep the framework intact. The Hebrew Bible, by contrast, is also interventionist (Abraham, Exodus, Sinai, the land, the promised return of twelve tribes), but it leaves the two big Hellenistic problems — *human mortality* and *the question of final historical purpose* — open and vague. That openness is precisely what gives the Hebrew Bible its modern usability.

### The Vegetarian Aside — Genesis 1 and Isaiah's Peaceable Kingdom

Tabor inserts a personal aside that quietly anchors much of his broader work. His commitment to vegetarianism, while resting on other grounds, is reinforced by **the ideal picture in Genesis 1**, where humans and beasts are given only green herbs to eat. Meat is permitted only *after the flood*, when sin and violence had filled the earth. "Are we to represent to the world in this small way this way of peace from which we have fallen?"

The image is reinforced by Isaiah's peaceable kingdom — the wolf and the lamb lying together, a child leading the lion, infants playing at the nest of the scorpion. "They will not hurt nor destroy on all my holy mountain" (Isaiah 11:9). Yet we still face the stark reality of life on this planet — nature "red in tooth and claw." The images are powerful. But Tabor pauses to ask honestly: are they necessary? Are they the *most truthful* ones? Why are they so much of his life?

### The Personal Frame — From Evangelical Background to Hebraic Faith

The closing portion of the video is autobiographical. Tabor was raised in an evangelical Christian family with strong New Testament emphasis. **The Old Testament was treated as "mere background to the superseding revelation brought by Jesus and the Apostle Paul."** It was even common in his circles to speak of the old law as "done away." He had never taken the Jewishness of Jesus seriously in terms of its implications. He assumed — because Paul dominated evangelical Christianity — that **whatever Paul presented as his gospel was identical to that of Jesus**.

"That was then and this is now." His academic career on ancient Judaism and early Christianity led him to a different settled view: Jesus was a Jew of his time who put his faith in the God of Abraham, upheld the Torah and the Hebrew prophets, and lived and died for his ancestral Hebrew faith. The trilogy that came out of this work — *The Jesus Dynasty* (2006), *The Jesus Discovery* (2012), *Paul and Jesus* — plus the forthcoming *The Lost Mary* (2025) and the in-progress *Jesus Betrayed: How the Church Rejected the Message of Its Founder* — all sit downstream of this realization. *Restoring Abrahamic Faith* (4th edition) is the personal, non-specialist exposition of what Tabor calls "Hebraic faith" — the Bible of Jesus and his earliest followers.
