## Summary

This is an edited segment from Tabor's monthly Zoom with students in his "Quest for the Historical Paul" online course — a working seminar, not a polished lecture. Tabor walks verse-by-verse through Romans 9-10 with three texts open in parallel: Paul's Greek (RSV), the Masoretic Hebrew, and the Septuagint. The exercise is forensic: where does Paul's Greek line up with the Hebrew, where does it lean on the LXX, and — most importantly — does Paul honor the *original context* of the passages he quotes, or does he extract phrases for their wording while ignoring what the prophets were actually saying? This is the "serious students only" version of Tabor's standing argument: Paul builds his gospel "according to the scriptures," but the scriptures rarely say what Paul makes them say.

The shadow argument throughout is Jason Staples' regathering-of-Israel thesis (the long-form version is Tabor playlist #27 with Staples himself). Tabor announces up front that Staples has "revolutionized" his reading of Romans 9-11 — *90%* convinced — and that his 2012 book *Paul and the Jewish Roots of Christianity* is "out of date" because he wasn't aware of Staples' framework at the time. The pivot text is Paul's quotation of Hosea 1-2 in Romans 9:25-26. Tabor's old reading: Paul just liked the phrase "not my people / my people" and applied it allegorically to Gentile converts. Staples' reading, which Tabor now adopts: Paul means Hosea **literally** — the Northern Kingdom tribes scattered into the nations are returning, and Paul's Gentile mission is the eschatological mechanism by which Hosea's divorce prophecy gets reversed. Isaiah 11 is the messianic substructure: the Messiah first regathers the scattered tribes, *then* the nations come in response to the sign.

Tabor walks the other Romans 9-10 quotations through the same dual lens. The potter/clay passage from Isaiah 29 and 45 is decontextualized — Paul extracts the image to argue for divine fatalism without engaging Isaiah's actual setting. The "remnant" quotation from Isaiah 10 supports Paul's "not every Israelite, but Israel as a whole" reading of all-Israel-saved. Romans 10:5-8 fuses Leviticus, Deuteronomy 30, Isaiah 28, and Joel 2 into a cluster about confession-and-belief, but Joel 2's "call on the name of the Lord [YHWH]" gets quietly slid into "call on the name of the Lord [Jesus]" — raising the unresolved question of whether Paul thought Jesus *was* YHWH. The "how beautiful are the feet" line from Isaiah 52 is Paul commandeering a verse about monotheism for evangelism. Psalm 19 ("their voice has gone out to all the earth") — originally a poem about creation's natural revelation — gets repurposed as proof the gospel has gone to the nations. And the Song of Moses' "I will make you jealous of a foolish nation" lands much closer to its original meaning *if* Staples is right and Paul means the actual scattered tribes returning. Tabor closes by underscoring that this same playful-with-context exegetical style runs all through the Talmud — rabbis routinely build arguments on word-play that ignores context. But Paul isn't writing Talmud commentary. He's laying the foundation of a new religion, and the question is whether the foundation holds.

## Key Teachings

### Paul's "According to the Scriptures" — Revelation or Reading?

Tabor opens with a methodological observation. Paul claims his gospel comes from two sources: direct revelation from Jesus, and the Hebrew scriptures ("according to the scriptures"). But these two sources overlap in ways that are hard to disentangle. When Paul says it "pleased God to reveal his son to me," that could mean an audible vision — or it could mean Paul one day read Isaiah 53 and recognized Jesus of Nazareth in the suffering servant. Tabor compares this to a modern believer saying "the Lord showed me something in Genesis today" — the boundary between encountering scripture and receiving revelation is porous. This matters because if Paul's "revelation" is largely his *reading* of the Hebrew Bible, then the question of whether he is reading it faithfully becomes the question of whether his gospel rests on solid ground.

### The Potter and the Clay — Calvinist Theodicy

Romans 9:20-21 quotes Isaiah 29 and 45 on the potter and the clay. Tabor reads Paul's argument colloquially: God does whatever he wants, and if he decides to have mercy on one group and not on another, *it's none of your business*. This is the Calvinist answer to theodicy — "who are you, a man, to answer back to God?" — and it is the *opposite* of how most modern believers handle the problem of evil. Modern believers say "everything happens for a reason," meaning God causes everything for a purpose. Paul doesn't say that. Paul says there is no reason owed to you, and demanding one is presumption.

But what Paul does with Isaiah is take an image (potter/clay) and pull it out of Isaiah's situation entirely. Isaiah's potter passages address God's relationship with Israel; Paul applies the image to God's sovereign right to make vessels "for beauty" or "for menial use." The phrase travels; the original context stays behind.

### Hosea 1-2 — The Staples Pivot

Romans 9:25-26 quotes Hosea: *"Those who are not my people I will call my people... and in the place where it was said you are not my people, they will be called the sons of the living God."* These are unambiguously addresses to the *Northern* tribes of Israel — Ephraim — not to Judah. Hosea is talking about the 8th-century Assyrian captivity. The tribes were divorced by God ("they're not my people"), scattered, lost their identity, and became Gentiles. But Hosea 2 frames the return as a remarriage — God will betroth Israel back to himself ("I will betroth you to me in truth and justice and love"). The Jewish wedding ceremony still uses these vows today.

The old reading (Tabor's pre-Staples view): Paul just liked the catchy phrase "not my people / my people" and applied it allegorically to Gentile converts. The new reading (Staples'): Paul means this **literally**. The lost northern tribes assimilated into the Gentile nations, and Paul's mission to the Gentiles is the prophesied regathering — actual scattered Israel returning home through being absorbed back into the covenant via the Spirit. The "fullness of the Gentiles" in Romans 11:25 is the Hebrew *melo ha-goyim* from Genesis 48:19 — Jacob's blessing on Ephraim that his seed will become "the fullness of the nations."

Tabor's verdict: Staples has convinced him "90%." His own 2012 Paul book is now "out of date" on this point.

### Isaiah 11 Is the Messianic Substructure

Tabor flags Isaiah 11 as the chapter that holds Paul's framework together. Isaiah 11 is *the* Davidic messianic prophecy. What does the Messiah do? He **regathers the scattered tribes first**, then raises an ensign to the nations who come *in response* — drawn by the spectacle of the lost Israelites rejoining Judah. This is the structure: regathering first, ingathering second. Paul reads this and builds his mission on it. Jesus reads this and gathers twelve apostles for twelve thrones over twelve tribes (Matthew 19:28 / Luke 22:30). The Messiah's job — by Isaiah's definition — is to make Israel whole again.

The prodigal son parable suddenly looks like the same story at the national scale. One kid stays home with the father (Judah). The other goes off to the pigsty and loses his identity (the ten northern tribes). The father runs to receive him back. Hebrew Roots groups and others who suspect they may be the lost tribes are reading this same pattern — without necessarily having the philological scaffolding Staples provides.

### Romans 10:5-8 — The Heaven/Underworld Cluster

Romans 10:5-8 fuses Leviticus 18:5 ("the man who practices the righteousness which is based on law shall live by it"), Deuteronomy 30 ("do not say in your heart, who will ascend to heaven... the word is near you"), and the surrounding "confess with your mouth, believe in your heart" formula. Tabor walks through the original Deuteronomy 30 context: Moses is at Sinai/Horeb telling the Israelites *you don't need to go to heaven to fetch the Torah; you don't need to descend to the underworld to find it; I'm giving it to you right here.* It's about the immediate accessibility of Torah.

Paul takes this and routes it through a Christology of ascent and descent: you don't need to go to heaven (Christ already descended); you don't need to descend to the abyss (Christ already rose). The word is near you — but the word is now "Jesus is Lord, and God raised him from the dead." The Deuteronomy passage's claim that the Torah needs no cosmic retrieval becomes Paul's claim that *Christ* needs no cosmic retrieval. The original meaning (Torah is here, follow it) gets replaced by the new meaning (faith in Christ is sufficient).

This is the same passage where Paul quotes Isaiah 28 — and he goes with the LXX ("will not be put to shame") rather than the Masoretic ("will not act hastily"). For this verse, Paul's text is the Greek.

### Joel 2 and the "Lord = YHWH or Jesus?" Slide

Romans 10:13: *"Everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved."* Quoted from Joel 2:32. In Joel's original context, "the Lord" is YHWH, and the verse is set in the Day of the Lord — call on YHWH and be rescued from final judgment.

Paul lifts the phrase and uses it in a context where "Lord" (*kyrios* in Greek) clearly means **Jesus**. He has just said "if you confess with your lips that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved." Then immediately: *"Everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved."*

Tabor pauses on this: *unless Paul thinks Jesus is YHWH*, this is a category swap. Augustine would say yes, Jesus is YHWH, problem solved — they're "one substance." Many Christians would. But that's reading later Trinitarian doctrine back into Paul. Tabor sketches the early-church Christological options: full Trinitarianism (three persons, one substance, all eternal, "begotten not made") versus Arianism (the Logos is the first of creation, highly exalted but not eternal, ontologically subordinate). Where Paul lands is genuinely unclear. But the Joel 2 quotation is exactly the kind of move that would later get cited as scriptural proof of Jesus's identification with YHWH — even though Joel is talking about YHWH alone.

### Isaiah 52 — Beautiful Feet, Wrong Gospel

Romans 10:15: *"How beautiful are the feet of those who preach good news."* From Isaiah 52. Tabor's read: Paul uses this as a celebration of his apostolic ministry — his beautiful feet preaching the gospel of Jesus. But if you look up Isaiah 52, the "good news" is not the gospel of a crucified-and-risen Messiah. The good news of Isaiah 52 is that **there is one God, and the whole world should turn from idols to the one God** — radical monotheism. Paul has commandeered a phrase whose original meaning is foundationally Jewish (monotheism) and used it to celebrate a mission whose content (Christ crucified) is theologically distinct.

Tabor's recurring observation: Paul "just likes the phrase." He hears a beautiful line in Isaiah and grafts it onto his argument without engaging what Isaiah was actually saying. This works in rabbinic Talmud-style exegesis — open any tractate of the Gemara and you'll find rabbis doing the same thing — but the rabbis aren't building a new religion on their wordplay. Paul is.

### Isaiah 53 — Where the Servant Song Lands Right

Romans 10:16 quotes Isaiah 53:1 — "Lord, who has believed our report?" Tabor concedes this is where Paul's exegesis "comes home to roost." Isaiah 53 (with its preamble in 52:13ff) is the suffering servant: wounded for our transgressions, blood shed for redemption, all of us gone astray like sheep, lamb led to slaughter. Tabor notes the original Hebrew context is *not* primarily atonement-doctrine — it's a Hebrew rhetorical pattern ("who could believe this? the one marred and destroyed will be lifted up and become the highest in the earth") about the great reversal. But it lands close enough to Paul's actual subject (a crucified Messiah whose death is misunderstood) that the quotation works *contextually* rather than just verbally.

Of all the citations in Romans 9-10, Isaiah 53 is the one Paul reads most honestly. It's the exception that proves the pattern.

### Psalm 19 and the Song of Moses — Natural Revelation Repurposed

Romans 10:18 asks "have they not heard?" — and answers with Psalm 19:4: *"Their voice has gone out through all the earth."* But Psalm 19 is a celebration of **natural revelation** — the heavens declaring God's glory, the firmament showing his handiwork, the sun like a bridegroom emerging from his tent. It's poetic praise of Genesis 1. Paul uses it to claim that the gospel has gone to all the nations. The two have nothing to do with each other except that Paul liked the universality of the phrase.

Then Romans 10:19 reaches for the Song of Moses (Deuteronomy 32): *"I will make you jealous of those who are not a nation; with a foolish nation I will make you angry."* In Moses' original song, the "foolish nation" is a pagan power like Assyria or Babylon that God uses to provoke Israel during exile. Paul applies this to Gentile converts making Jewish believers jealous of God's favor — *unless* Staples is right. Then the "foolish nation" really is the scattered northern tribes who were foolish, fell into idolatry, and were divorced. The Song of Moses fits Paul's actual subject *better* on Staples' reading than on the standard one.

### Rabbinic Logic vs. Western Logic

Tabor closes this segment by observing that the exegetical style Paul uses — extracting phrases for their wording while ignoring their original context — is everywhere in the Talmud. Open any tractate of the Gemara and you'll find rabbis taking a verse about not muzzling an ox and building elaborate arguments about animal welfare, or taking "do not trip the blind" and developing a full body of disability law. They love single words. They love alliterations. Context routinely matters less than the wording's flexibility.

This is **not how Western logic works**. A Western argument that quotes Isaiah 52 on "beautiful feet" to prove the gospel has gone to the nations has not proven anything by Western standards — it has cited a phrase whose meaning doesn't apply. But by rabbinic standards, this is normal exegetical play. The question, then, is what kind of argument Paul thinks he is making. Tabor's implicit answer: Paul is doing rabbinic exegesis, but the *churches built on his letters* read him as if he were doing Western logical proof. The mismatch is the source of two thousand years of confused doctrine.

## Misconceptions Addressed

| Romans 9-11 is about the church replacing Israel | No — Paul is working from the Hebrew prophets' regathering-of-Israel framework. The Gentile mission *is* the prophesied return of scattered Israel, not a replacement of it |
| "All Israel will be saved" means every individual Israelite will eventually be saved | No — Tabor reads it as "Israel as a whole will be saved" / "Israel as a reconstituted nation," not as universal individual salvation. Romans 9:27 ("only a remnant") contextualizes 11:26 |

| Psalm 19's "their voice goes out through all the earth" supports Paul's gospel mission | Psalm 19 is about **natural revelation** — the heavens declaring God's glory through creation. Paul liked the universality of the phrase and applied it to evangelism |
