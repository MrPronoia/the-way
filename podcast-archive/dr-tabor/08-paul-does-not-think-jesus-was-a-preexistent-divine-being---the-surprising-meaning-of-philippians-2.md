## Summary

Dr. James Tabor takes on one of the load-bearing texts of orthodox Christology — Philippians 2:5-11, the so-called "Christ hymn" — and dismantles the preexistence reading that almost everyone (orthodox believers, mythicists, and most academics) agrees on. The standard reading: Jesus was a divine being in heaven who "emptied himself," put on flesh, died, and was exalted back to where he started. Tabor's claim: **Paul never says that, and the passage actually makes more sense without it.** Philippians 2 is not about a preexistent deity descending — it's about two Adams. The first Adam (Genesis 3) was told by the *nakash* he could "be like God"; he grasped for equality, ate the fruit, and went back to dust. Jesus is the second Adam — also a flesh-and-blood human "born of a woman" — who took the opposite path. He did not grasp at equality with God. He humbled himself unto death. And because of that, God highly exalted him.

This pivots the entire Pauline framework. Tabor reframes Paul's "big religious question" — it's not Luther's "how do I get saved?" but a cosmic one: **how can a flesh-and-blood man of dust be exalted into the highest heaven and become ruler of the cosmos, and what does that mean for the rest of us?** This is why 1 Corinthians 15 (Tabor again insists) should be called the *transformation* chapter, not the resurrection chapter. The driver is metamorphosis — caterpillar to butterfly, seed to oak, dust to a "life-giving spirit." Jesus is the firstborn of many brothers and sisters. The cosmos itself groans in Romans 8 to be set free from decay. The Hellenistic/Gnostic answer (escape this corrupt world) is rejected; Paul keeps the Hebrew premise that creation is good but subject to futility, and will be redeemed cosmically, not abandoned.

The interview closes with Tabor returning to a thesis from earlier videos: Paul is the only first-person witness to the resurrection, and his "resurrection" is not a resuscitated corpse. Using 2 Corinthians 5's twin analogies — old clothes vs. new clothes, an earthly tent vs. an eternal building "not made with hands" — Tabor argues Paul has zero interest in what happened to Jesus's physical body. The corpse went back to dust like every other human body. What Paul saw was the *transformed* Jesus, the new-creation Adam, the prototype of what every human is destined to become. The pre-existence reading, Tabor argues, gets pulled in from John's logos prologue and Colossians (which he treats as deutero-Pauline) and then projected backward onto Philippians 2, distorting Paul's actual theology beyond recognition.

## Key Teachings

### Philippians 2 Is About Two Adams, Not One Preexistent God

The hinge of the whole reading. The first Adam (Genesis 3) was told by the *nakash* (the "slick" or "shining one" — not yet Satan in Genesis's text) that if he ate the forbidden fruit he would "be like God." He grasped at that equality, ate, and was sentenced: "dust you are, to dust you shall return." Adam #1 is the cautionary pattern.

Jesus is Adam #2. Also flesh and blood. Also "born of a woman" — Galatians language meaning "human as opposed to angelic," not "incarnated from heaven." But Jesus *did not grasp* at equality with God. He took the opposite path: emptied himself, became a *doulos* (slave), humbled himself to the most despicable death — crucifixion. Therefore God exalted him. The passage is structured as parody/parallel: the first Adam tried to take and lost everything; the second Adam gave everything and was exalted.

This makes Philippians 2:6 readable on its own terms: "though he was in the form of God" maps directly to Genesis 1:26 — Adam was made *in the image of God*. The potential was already there. Adam #1 reached for it; Adam #2 didn't have to.

### Paul's Real Question Isn't Luther's

The Roman Catholic / Lutheran / Reformed tradition (back through Augustine) frames Paul around guilt: *how do I get saved?* Tabor says this is reading Paul through Luther's anxieties. Paul's actual driving question is different:

**How can a flesh-and-blood human being, born of a woman, get exalted to the highest heaven and become ruler of the cosmos — and what does that pattern mean for the rest of humanity?**

That's a cosmic question, not a juridical one. Paul wants to know how God saves the *cosmos*, not how individual souls get extracted from it. This is hebraic (creation is good but subject to futility) rather than Hellenistic/Gnostic (creation is a prison; escape it).

### The Resurrection Chapter Is the Transformation Chapter

Tabor returns to a theme from Video #01: 1 Corinthians 15 is mislabeled. It's not primarily about corpses coming back. It's about metamorphosis — the same Greek word behind a caterpillar becoming a butterfly. Paul addresses the dead *incidentally* because the Corinthians were worried their dead relatives would miss out when the parousia hit.

What Paul actually teaches: at the last trumpet, the living and the dead will be transformed "in the twinkling of an eye" — flesh becomes spirit, mortal becomes immortal, corruptible becomes incorruptible. Jesus is the *firstborn of many brothers and sisters* (Romans 8). He's not the unique god-man; he's the prototype.

### Paul Doesn't Care About Jesus's Corpse

This is the part that shocks orthodox readers. Tabor pushes it hard: Paul has zero interest in what happened to Jesus's physical body. Where did it go? Same place every corpse goes — back to dust. "Dust you are, and to dust you return."

Why isn't this a problem for Paul? Because resurrection in his framework is not the same body coming back. It's a *new* body — a "life-giving spirit" body. The old body is like old clothes you take off; the new body is the new clothing. Or: the old body is a tent you live in temporarily; the new body is a building "not made with hands" eternal in the heavens (2 Corinthians 5).

When Christians ask "but what about Jesus's wounds? What about eating fish on the beach?" — Tabor's answer is: that's John and Luke, not Paul. Paul's only first-person testimony is his own vision, and Paul explicitly says **he cannot describe** the resurrection body. If it had been a touchable resuscitated corpse, he could have described it just fine.

### "Born of a Woman" Doesn't Mean Incarnation

A linguistic correction. When Paul says Jesus was "born of a woman" (Galatians 4:4), modern Christians hear "incarnation" — God taking flesh. Tabor: that's importing John's logos theology where it doesn't belong. In Hebrew/Aramaic idiom, "born of a woman" is just the standard way of saying *human* as opposed to *angelic*.

Jesus uses the same phrase in the Q source: "Among those born of women, none has arisen greater than John." That's not a statement about reincarnation or incarnation — it just means "among humans." Paul means the same thing. Jesus was a real human being, with a real human mother, like John the Baptist.

### Paul's Mystical Sighting Was Not a Resuscitated Corpse

Tabor returns to his thesis that Paul is the only first-person witness. The standard apologetic move (Tabor names Liberty University's apologists, William Lane Craig, etc.) is to lean on Luke and John for the bodily-encounter stories — Thomas touching wounds, Jesus eating fish. But Luke explicitly says he's not an eyewitness. None of the gospels are first-person.

Paul is first-person. And Paul describes something visionary, glorious, unutterable — explicitly *not* a normal body. When Paul is pressed on "what kind of body?" his answer is essentially "I can't tell you, but it's powerful, glorious, and immortal."

This connects to Paul's ascent narrative in 2 Corinthians 12 — taken up to paradise, saw "things unutterable," didn't know if it was "in the body or out of the body." Paul is a mystic. His "seeing" was a visionary clairvoyance with a voice component, more like the apparitions at Fatima than a physical reunion with a friend.

### The Cosmos Itself Is Being Redeemed (Romans 8)

Paul's eschatology is *not* "the world is doomed, escape to heaven." That's Gnostic. Paul's frame is hebraic: the cosmos is good but "subjected to futility" — to decay, to death, to corruption. Romans 8 says the entire creation is groaning, in labor pains, waiting to be set free from its bondage to decay.

This is the opposite of the standard Christian hymn-line ("this world is not my home, I'm just a-passing through"). Paul's gospel is that *this world*, transformed, becomes the new creation. The body, transformed, becomes the spiritual body. Diamonds-in-the-mud Gnosticism would say: you don't redeem the mud, you extract the diamond. Paul says: God is redeeming the mud itself.

### Why People Misread Philippians 2

Tabor reconstructs the move. Readers come to Philippians 2 already carrying:
- **John 1** — the logos was with God in the beginning, became flesh
- **Colossians** — Christ as the firstborn of creation, agent of creation (a letter Tabor treats as not authentically Pauline)
- **Later creedal Christology** — Nicea, Chalcedon, the two-natures formula

With that baggage, "though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped" sounds obvious: a divine being chose not to cling to divinity. But if you read Paul on his own terms — without John, without Colossians, without the creeds — the Adamic parallel jumps out and the preexistence reading recedes. The phrase "form of God" maps onto Adam being in *the image of God*, and "grasped" maps onto Adam *grasping the fruit* to become like God.
