**Published:** March 10, 2024

## Summary

Dr. Tabor takes on one of the most central — and strangest — ideas in Christianity: that you are saved by being "washed in the blood" of Jesus. The old evangelical hymn captures the absurdity perfectly. "Are your garments white as snow? Are you washed in the blood of the Lamb?" Tabor: to anyone who's never heard of Christianity, blood doesn't make things white. So where did this idea come from? Did Jesus teach it? Did John the Baptist? Did James, who actually led the church for forty years after Jesus's death? Did Peter or John?

Tabor's answer: **none of them.** The blood-cleansing framework originates with Paul. Mark, our earliest gospel, is heavily Paul-influenced and carries the doctrine forward (Mark 10:45, "ransom for many"; the Last Supper "blood of the covenant poured out for many"). Hebrews extends it cosmically (Jesus enters the heavenly sanctuary with his own blood). 1 Peter and 1 John apply it to the original apostles' names — but Tabor argues these letters are pseudonymous, written by the Pauline school to retroactively put Paul's doctrine in Peter's and John's mouths. Even Revelation, in the standard manuscripts, repeats the formula ("washed their robes and made them white in the blood of the Lamb") — though the Byzantine textual tradition preserves an alternate reading ("blessed are those who keep the commandments"), and Tabor argues a pre-Christian version of Revelation can be recovered where the blood language was inserted later.

The historical Jesus, John the Baptist, and James the brother of Jesus taught the **Hebrew Bible's repentance framework**: turn to God, ask forgiveness, and you are forgiven. No blood required. The Lord's Prayer asks for forgiveness in exchange for forgiving others — no sacrifice. The parable of the Pharisee and tax collector (Luke 18) makes humble repentance, not blood payment, the means of justification. The book of James — written by Jesus's actual brother — mentions Jesus twice but says nothing about his death, blood, or sacrifice. Psalm 51 (David's repentance after the Bathsheba/Uriah affair): "wash me and I shall be whiter than snow" — and crucially, "you do not delight in sacrifice; the sacrifice acceptable to God is a broken spirit." Jeremiah 7 even has God say he never commanded sacrifices when he led Israel out of Egypt. The blood-atonement layer is Paul's invention, grafted onto a religion whose founders never taught it.

## Key Teachings

### The Idea Is Stranger Than It Sounds

Tabor opens with the old evangelical song "Are You Washed in the Blood of the Lamb" — "are your garments white as snow?" To someone hearing it cold, the imagery is incoherent. Blood doesn't make things white. The Hebrew Bible does have the image "though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow" (Isaiah 1:18) — but that passage has nothing to do with animal blood or Jesus's blood. God simply forgives. The "washed in blood" construction is a later splice of two different ideas.

### Read the New Testament Backwards

A core methodological move from Tabor's book *Paul and Jesus: How the Apostle Transformed Christianity*. The default assumption is that you go to the gospels to find Jesus and early Christianity, then read Paul as later commentary. Tabor flips it: **Paul writes earliest** (50s CE), the gospels come later (Mark ~70 CE, then Matthew and Luke building on Mark). If Mark is already Paul-influenced — and Tabor argues it heavily is — then by the time you get to the gospels you're reading Paul's framework retrofitted into Jesus's mouth, not Jesus's actual teaching.

### Mark Carries Paul's Blood Theology Into the Gospel Tradition

Two key Markan passages:
- **Mark 14 (Last Supper):** "This is my blood of the covenant, poured out for many." The phrase "for many" is technical language from Isaiah 53 — the suffering servant. Paul says in 1 Corinthians 11 that he "received from the Lord" this ritual — but Paul never met the earthly Jesus. He got it by revelation.
- **Mark 10:45:** "The Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many." Ransom = payment for release. Life = blood. This verse is climactic for the whole gospel and lays the substitutionary-atonement framework into Jesus's teaching.

Acts 20:28 (same author as Luke) seals it: "the church of God, which he purchased with the blood of his own son." Purchase, ransom, redeem — all economic transaction language for salvation.

### John's Gospel Pushes It Further — Into Mysticism

John has no Last Supper bread-and-wine institution. Instead, John 6 has Jesus saying earlier in his ministry: "Unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you have no life in you." Tabor reads this as Paul's eucharistic framework pushed into a participation-mysticism: by consuming the flesh/blood you are joined to Jesus's death and resurrection. Not symbol — participation.

### Paul's Blood Texts — The Core Passages

Tabor walks through the Pauline corpus:

- **Romans 3:25** — Jesus "whom God put forward as an expiation by his blood, to be received by faith." Expiation = cleansing, washing, atonement. You receive forgiveness by faith *in the blood*.
- **Romans 5:9** — "Justified by his blood, we shall be saved from the wrath of God." Without the blood, you're under wrath. This is core, not optional, in Paul.
- **1 Corinthians 10-11** — Communion as actual *koinonia* (participation) in Christ's blood. Not symbol — reenactment that unites you to the saving act.
- **Ephesians 2** (Pauline school, probably not Paul himself) — "You who were once far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ." Gentiles enter the covenant through blood.
- **Colossians 1:20** — God reconciles all things in the cosmos "by the blood of his cross."

The whole system runs on the same engine: no blood, no justification.

### Hebrews Expands It Into Cosmic Priesthood

Tabor doesn't think Paul wrote Hebrews, but it's "absolutely Pauline" in thought. The book reframes Jesus as high priest who enters the heavenly sanctuary not with the blood of goats and calves but with **his own blood** (Hebrews 9). It explicitly contrasts the Temple's animal sacrifices with Jesus's superior sacrifice. Hebrews 10:19 — "we have confidence to enter the sanctuary by the blood of Jesus." You don't even approach God without the blood as shield.

### 1 Peter and 1 John Are Pseudonymous — Paul's Doctrine Put in the Apostles' Names

Critical for Tabor's argument. Neither 1 Peter nor 1 John, in his reading, comes from the historical Peter or John. 1 Peter mentions Mark and Silvanus — Paul's companions — and reads as essentially Pauline. The opening of 1 Peter has believers "sprinkled with his blood" and "ransomed with the precious blood of Christ, like that of a lamb without blemish."

The mechanism: Pauline-school writers wanted to spread Paul's doctrine under apostolic authority, so they wrote letters in Peter's and John's names. The result is that when you open the New Testament it looks like Peter, John, Paul, and Jesus all agreed about the blood — but Tabor argues that picture is manufactured. Peter and John (the historical figures) never taught this.

### Revelation Has a Layer Problem

In the standard fourth-century manuscripts (Vaticanus, Sinaiticus, Alexandrinus), Revelation is saturated with blood-of-the-Lamb language:
- 1:5 — "freed us from our sins by his blood"
- 5:9 — "by your blood you ransomed men for God"
- 7:14 — "washed their robes and made them white in the blood of the Lamb" *(this is the source of the evangelical hymn)*
- 12:11 — "they conquered him by the blood of the Lamb"

But the Byzantine textual tradition preserves an alternate reading at 22:14: instead of "blessed are those who wash their robes," it reads **"blessed are those who keep the commandments"** — a thoroughly Jewish frame. Tabor has argued in articles on his blog that a pre-Christian (pre-Pauline-overlay) version of Revelation can be reconstructed in which the blood-of-the-Lamb language is removed and the text still flows cleanly. The Jewish remnant theology (Sabbath-keepers, commandment-keepers — Revelation 12:17, 14:12) survives in the manuscripts as a fossil of the earlier layer.

### What John the Baptist, Jesus, and James Actually Taught

The "core of the Jerusalem church" — what Jude calls "the faith once delivered to the saints" — looks completely different:

- **John the Baptist:** "a baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins." Turn to God, get baptized, be forgiven. No blood.
- **Jesus's parable of the Pharisee and the tax collector (Luke 18):** The tax collector won't lift his eyes, beats his breast, says "God be merciful to me, a sinner." Jesus: "this man went down to his house justified rather than the other." No blood. Just humble repentance.
- **The Lord's Prayer (Luke's version):** "Forgive us our sins, for we ourselves forgive everyone indebted to us." Forgiveness is conditioned on forgiving others — not on a blood payment.
- **The book of James:** Written by Jesus's actual brother. Calls Jesus "the Lord Jesus Christ" twice. Says **nothing** about his death, his blood, his sacrifice. James 5: prayer of faith heals the sick and forgives sins. No atonement mechanism. No cross.
- **Q source:** The double sayings shared by Matthew and Luke, often thought to be the earliest collection of Jesus's teachings. Contains the prophetic faith of the Hebrew Bible. Contains nothing of Paul's blood gospel.

This is the actual Jesus movement before Paul. A Jewish prophetic renewal of repentance, mercy, and turning to God.

### The Hebrew Bible's Own Forgiveness Theology

Tabor closes with the source material the Jesus movement was actually drawing from:

- **Psalm 103:** "The Lord is merciful and gracious, slow to anger, abounding in steadfast love... he does not deal with us according to our sins... as far as the east is from the west, so far does he remove our transgressions." No sacrifice required. God simply forgives those who turn to him.
- **Psalm 51 (David's repentance after Bathsheba/Uriah):** "Have mercy on me, O God, according to your steadfast love... blot out my transgressions. Wash me thoroughly from my iniquity... cleanse me from my sin. Wash me and I shall be whiter than snow." *And then* — "you do not delight in sacrifice... the sacrifice acceptable to God is a broken spirit; a broken and contrite heart, O God, you will not despise." The "washing" language is here in the Hebrew Bible, but it has nothing to do with blood. It's God forgiving the repentant heart.
- **Jeremiah 7:** "I did not command your fathers, when I brought them out of Egypt, concerning burnt offerings or sacrifices. But this I commanded them: Obey my voice, and I will be your God." Tabor: this freaks people out, because the Torah seems full of sacrificial commands. His reading: the sacrificial material comes from a Priestly editorial layer, and Jeremiah (and Jesus, and James) represent the **prophetic** stream that always relativized sacrifice in favor of obedience and mercy.

### Why Paul Made This Move

Tabor reserves his deepest argument for the next video in the series (on the suffering servant of Isaiah), but signals the direction: Paul read Isaiah 53's "lamb led to the slaughter" as a singular messianic prophecy of Jesus's substitutionary death. Tabor's view (set out in earlier videos): Isaiah's servant songs are actually about a **corporate group** of suffering servants whose faithfulness brings redemption to the world — not one individual paying a blood price. Paul also collapsed Passover (which was never a sin offering in the Hebrew Bible) into a sin-atonement framework: "Christ our Passover is sacrificed for us" (1 Corinthians 5). Paul fuses two unrelated sacrificial categories — Passover lamb and sin offering — into the Lamb of God who takes away the sins of the world.

Where did Paul actually get the framework? Tabor speculates: partly the Temple at Jerusalem (which Paul did know firsthand), but probably more. The full answer requires looking at Paul's broader background — Hellenistic mystery religions, Greco-Roman blood-purification rites, his own mystical experience of Christ. That's a topic for another video.
