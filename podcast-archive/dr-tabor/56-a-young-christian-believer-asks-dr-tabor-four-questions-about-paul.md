**Published:** May 28, 2025

## Summary

A young, sincere Christian believer named Jacob Giron — who has read Tabor's *Paul and Jesus* — sits down with Tabor and asks four questions a thoughtful Christian *has* to ask if they take the book seriously. The conversation is pedagogical and gentle. Tabor refuses to "trash" Paul, refuses to tell Jacob what to believe, and several times explicitly says, "I don't want to interfere with your personal faith." What he does instead is lay out, as a historian, what the documents say — and let Jacob feel the weight of the gap between what Jesus seems to have taught and what Paul built.

The four questions form a natural arc. First, *what is the centrality of Paul's message?* Tabor names it cleanly: Billy Graham's gospel — Jesus the eternal Son of God, born, crucified, buried, raised, ascended, returning in judgment, and unless you accept the free gift of grace you don't get eternal life. That's the package. Second, *how is Paul's message different from Jesus's?* Tabor pulls back to the **historical Jesus before the theological overlay** — the Q source, the Sermon on the Mount material, the prodigal son, the tax collector in the temple, Psalm 51. None of it requires blood atonement. None of it requires accepting Jesus. It's all *turn to God, be humble, repent, live justly* — the same message as Micah, Amos, and the prophets. Third, *why would Paul distort the message?* Tabor refuses the cynical answer. Paul is sincere — he believes he hears from Jesus directly (he says so in 2 Corinthians 12 and 1 Corinthians 11), he builds his framework on texts like Isaiah 53, and he suffers for it. He's not a Roman agent or a fraud; he's a mystic convinced he's the recipient of revelations.

Fourth — and this is where Jacob asks the question that lands hardest — *what can we know about a version of Jesus's faith that's different from Paul's?* Here Tabor delivers his core thesis. There **is** another version, and it has a primary witness: **James the brother of Jesus**. James led the Jerusalem movement from Jesus's death until 63 CE, when the same priestly family that killed Jesus killed him too. The Letter of James is in the New Testament; nobody reads it. Read it, Tabor says, and try to find a single line that reflects the Pauline message. You won't. What you'll find is Sermon-on-the-Mount material: don't swear, don't lie, don't cheat workers, watch your tongue. Combine James's letter with the **Q source** — the earliest extractable layer of Jesus's teachings, which contains *nothing* about crucifixion, resurrection, or the blood of Jesus — and you have a coherent alternative gospel that looks more like the prophets than like Paul. The Gospel of Thomas confirms it: "Go to my brother James the Just." If Jesus came back today, Tabor says, he'd be more comfortable in a synagogue reciting the Shema than in a church eating his own body and blood.

## Key Teachings

### Question 1 — What Is the Centrality of Paul's Message?

Tabor frames Paul's central message through Billy Graham, who "got it right" as a faithful summary of the Pauline gospel: God sent his eternal Son, incarnated, born of a woman, who died for the sins of the world. We are all sinners. Unless we accept him and the free gift of grace, we don't receive eternal life. It's John 3:16 expanded.

Tabor anchors this in Paul's own words: "I determined to know nothing among you except Jesus Christ and him crucified" (1 Cor 2:2). The full Pauline package: **Jesus the man, Christ the divine Son, crucified, dead, buried, raised on the third day, ascended to heaven, coming again in glory for final judgment.**

The implication that makes Jacob uncomfortable: a person who lives a good life, even believes in God, but doesn't accept Paul's specific doctrine, is — by Paul's logic — cast out of God's presence. "That's not how we would deal with any of our children," Tabor says. "Why would God be that way with human beings?"

### Paul Claims Direct Conversation with the Risen Jesus

A point easy to miss: Paul writes *before* the gospels. So when he references the Last Supper in 1 Corinthians 11 — "I received from the Lord what I'm now going to tell you of how on the night he was betrayed" — he's not referencing a gospel narrative. He's claiming **direct revelation from Jesus**.

Same for 2 Corinthians 12: Paul asked Jesus three times about his thorn in the flesh and got the same answer three times. He refers to an "abundance of revelations" — using compound Greek words meaning "super-abundance and greatness." For someone who loves Paul, this is amazing: Jesus left, ascended, but through Paul, you can keep hearing from him. For someone who reads it critically: Paul's package is grounded almost entirely on private, unverifiable revelations.

### Question 2 — How Is Paul's Message Different from Jesus's?

The methodological challenge: you can't just open Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John and "get Jesus." Tabor calls this **"reading the New Testament backwards"** — a chapter title in *Paul and Jesus*. The canonical gospels are heavily influenced by Paul (Tabor: "All of them. All of them. All of them."). To get to the historical Jesus, you have to strip back the Pauline overlay.

The method: find the **Q source** — material common to Matthew and Luke but absent from Mark. Mark wrote first; Matthew and Luke independently expanded Mark and independently drew from a shared sayings collection. Isolate that shared material and you get Jesus's teachings — the Sermon on the Mount material — *without* the crucifixion-resurrection theology.

**What Q contains:** ethical teaching, parables, the way to live, the way to relate to God. **What Q does not contain:** the death of Christ for sins, blood atonement, justification by faith, the necessity of accepting Jesus. Nothing about crucifixion or resurrection at all.

### Three Major Differences Tabor Highlights

**1. How do you receive God's favor and grace?** Paul: through accepting Christ's death, blood, baptism, communion, church membership. Jesus (from the Q-layer material): two men go to the temple — a religious Pharisee and a tax collector. The tax collector beats his breast: "God, be merciful to me, a sinner." Jesus says **that** man went home justified. Or the prodigal son — wastes everything, returns, says forgive me, is welcomed back. **No mention of blood, of cross, of accepting any savior.** Just turn to God sincerely.

**2. Social ethics.** Paul tells women to be silent in the assembly and ask questions of their husbands at home (1 Cor 14). Jesus, in the Mary/Martha story (Luke 10), affirms Mary's choice to sit with the men as a student of the rabbi — Tabor reads this as Jesus endorsing what we'd call **co-ed education** centuries before Harvard, Yale, and Princeton allowed women. Paul's "wives submit, slaves obey" is — Tabor argues — eschatologically motivated (the world is passing away, don't rock the boat) rather than principled. But the result is a Paul whose social ethics look very different from Jesus's.

**3. Taking up the cross.** Paul: the cross is what Jesus did *for you* — accept his blood and be saved. Jesus (Mark 8:34): "If anyone would follow me, let him deny himself and take up *a* cross." Same word, opposite meaning. Jesus is calling followers to the same way of life he's walking — speaking truth to power and probably getting smashed for it. Albert Schweitzer's famous line: "Jesus fell with all his might on the wheel of history. It lunged back and crushed him." Schweitzer's point: that's a call to *you*, not something someone did for you.

### Question 3 — Why Would Paul Distort the Message?

Tabor refuses the cynical answer. **Paul is sincere.** "I think this idea that he's a Roman agent or anything like that that you see on YouTube — no, Paul is very sincere." Paul goes through enormous suffering. He dedicates his life. He doesn't think he's distorting anything — he believes he's receiving revelation from Jesus and following texts of scripture (especially Isaiah 53, the Suffering Servant) that he can defend.

So how do we get from a sincere Paul to a distorted gospel? Tabor's answer: Paul **builds** his theology from specific texts read in specific ways. Isaiah 53 — the wounded-for-our-transgressions Suffering Servant — he identifies with Jesus. From there he constructs the framework: God needs to be appeased; the debt must be paid by his only Son.

The historical question, Tabor says, is whether *the prophets* or *Jesus* or *Moses* ever taught that framework. He cites **Micah:** God removes our sins from us as a father pities his children — by grace, *not* by sacrifice. In fact, Micah says God doesn't want sacrifices. The Pauline idea that God needs to be appeased through his Son's blood is the foundation of Christianity, but it's a **departure** from the underlying Abrahamic faith.

Tabor's framing: he's a historian. He's not telling you who's good and who's bad. He's saying — here are two different theological frameworks. One is Paul's. One is in the prophets and in the Q-layer Jesus.

### Question 4 — What Can We Know About a Version of Jesus's Faith Different from Paul's?

The most important answer in the conversation. Jacob asks if there's even *one* person from the early period who would have heard Paul preach and said "this guy is saying something different from what Jesus said."

Tabor: "We do have someone like that. Guess who it is? **James.**"

**The Letter of James:**
- Tucked in the back of the New Testament; nobody reads it
- Written by Jesus's brother, leader of the Jerusalem movement from 30 CE to 63 CE
- James was murdered by the same priestly family that killed Jesus — beaten and thrown from the wall of Jerusalem
- It's five chapters
- **Show me one line in the book of James that reflects the Pauline message.** You won't find one.

What you'll find: don't swear, don't lie, don't cheat your employees on wages, don't have an evil tongue. The standard prophetic-rabbinic teaching, the Torah, Jesus's Q-layer material. Same content. And James is writing to "the church scattered abroad" — i.e., to early Christian communities. **Why would he never mention the things Paul made central if those were the core of the faith?**

The Gospel of Thomas, saying #12: the disciples ask Jesus, "When you leave, who will be our teacher?" Jesus answers: "Go to **my brother James the Just**, for whose sake heaven and earth came into being."

In Acts 15 — the so-called Jerusalem Council — James gives the final ruling on the Gentile question. He's not even introduced. The author of Acts doesn't tell readers who James *is*. Paul does, in Galatians: "James, the Lord's brother." But Acts buries the fact. Tabor: a whole wing of early Christianity, led by the brother of Jesus, was completely written out of the great-church narrative in favor of Paul.

### Would Jesus Be More Comfortable in a Synagogue or a Church?

Jacob asks: would the historical Jesus look at Paul's preaching — Jesus Christ is Lord and Savior, repent and believe or face hell — and call it heresy or agree with it?

Tabor's answer: Jesus "would be sort of amazed that that's the turn it took." If Jesus came back today:
- A church meeting on Sunday (he kept Sabbath)
- Eating and drinking the body and blood of his God (a horrifying image for a Torah-observant Jew)
- Being told you go to hell unless you accept Paul's blood-atonement framework
- Compare to a synagogue: quoting the Shema (which Jesus quoted), "Love your neighbor as yourself" (which Jesus taught), upholding the prophets

**He'd be more comfortable in the synagogue.** Easter, Christmas, the Eucharist — all of it would be foreign. What Jesus condemned was **hypocritical religion**, not Torah or the prophets. He said the Sabbath was made for humans, not humans for the Sabbath — affirming the gift of Sabbath, not abolishing it.

### Why Did Paul's Version Win?

Jacob's natural follow-up: why did Paul's gospel take over the world if it was a departure?

Tabor: it's a **package deal**. "Believe on the Lord Jesus and you will be saved" is enormously attractive. It's portable, it's universal, it doesn't require Torah observance. It's also exclusive — like Islam, salvation is gated through one specific revelation. Hebrew Bible religion is unconditional in a way Paul's gospel is not: anyone, anywhere, can turn to the creator God, do Psalm 51 sincerely, and be in right relationship. Paul makes that insufficient — you can do Psalm 51 all you want, but without the blood of Jesus, no relationship.

That conditional structure is **what Christianity is**, Tabor says — and it's a real departure from what's in the Hebrew Bible, the Q-layer Jesus, and the letter of James.

### Tabor's Closing Move — Not Telling Jacob What to Believe

The pastoral note throughout: "I don't want to interfere with your personal faith." Tabor positions himself as a professor doing what a professor would do — laying out the historical evidence, the sources, the layers, the dating. The decision is Jacob's.

The hinge question Jacob lands on: "Did Jesus himself have exclusive claims? Did he himself consider himself Lord and Savior?" If yes, then Pauline Christianity might be on solid ground. If no — if those claims come only from Paul or from John, which Tabor doesn't think is historical — then the burden of proof shifts.

Tabor's answer: as a rabbi, Jesus said "come to me, listen to my word" the way any confident teacher does. But the specific package — accept me, blood, eternal life, the only way to the Father — Tabor thinks Jesus **turned people toward God**, not toward himself. He might see himself as a way *pointing* to God, not as the way. The John 14:6 "I am the way" — Tabor doesn't think that's historical.

## Misconceptions Addressed

| Misconception | Tabor's Response |
|---------------|------------------|
| "Paul knew Jesus and his teaching firsthand" | Paul never met the earthly Jesus. He claims direct revelation from the risen Christ — "I received from the Lord" — and these revelations include things like the Last Supper liturgy (1 Cor 11), which Paul claims as direct revelation, not gospel tradition. His framework is built on private mystical experiences, not on knowing what Jesus historically taught. |
| "Just read Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John to find Jesus" | The canonical gospels are heavily Pauline. To get the historical Jesus you have to identify the earliest strata — Mark as foundation, the Q source as Jesus's teachings — beneath the theological overlay. |
| "Paul was a fraud or a Roman agent" | Tabor explicitly rejects this. Paul is sincere. He suffers, he dedicates his life, he believes he's hearing from Jesus. The problem isn't his motives; the problem is his framework departs from Jesus's. |
| "The early church was unified around Paul's gospel" | False. Until 63 CE the Jerusalem movement was led by James the brother of Jesus, who taught Sermon-on-the-Mount-style ethics with no Pauline blood-atonement framework. Two streams existed; only one survived in the canon. |
| "The letter of James is just practical advice — not theology" | Tabor's challenge: read it as theology. It's five chapters from the leader of the original Jesus movement, written to scattered believers, and it contains zero Pauline content. That's a *massive* theological statement by omission. |
| "Jesus said 'I am the way' — exclusive Christianity is from his own lips" | "I don't even think John 14:6 is historical." The exclusive-Christ claim doesn't appear in the Q-layer Jesus material. It's in the latest gospel (John), written ~90-100 CE, decades after Paul. |
| "The historical Jesus is just a modern liberal invention" | The Quest for the Historical Jesus is a 200+ year scholarly enterprise. Methodologically: isolate the earliest layers (Q, the sayings material), compare with James, compare with the prophets. The convergence is consistent. |
| "Hebrew Bible religion was legalistic; Jesus brought grace" | Tabor pushes back: Psalm 51, Micah 7, Isaiah 66, Psalm 145 — the Hebrew Bible is **drenched** in grace. God runs to forgive. The "fossilized legalistic Judaism" trope is a Christian caricature that Paul's framework requires but the Hebrew Bible doesn't support. |
