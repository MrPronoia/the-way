## Summary

This wide-ranging two-hour conversation is Tabor's most comprehensive single-session treatment of Paul's biography, sources, and methodology. He begins with his own scholarly credentials and a long excursus on the 1993 Waco siege (where he worked with the FBI to interpret the Branch Davidian worldview) — a reminder that apocalyptic communities need to be understood from inside their framework, not dismissed from outside. This becomes the methodological key to reading Paul: **Paul was an apocalyptic Jew who believed the parousia would arrive within his own lifetime**. Every social ethic, sexual instruction, slavery directive, and church-order recommendation he gave was calibrated to a 5-10 year horizon. When that horizon failed, the church inherited the instructions but lost the framework.

Tabor lays out his **source-hierarchy method** for Pauline studies — the single most important methodological contribution in the conversation. "Primary Paul" is the seven undisputed letters (Romans, 1-2 Corinthians, Galatians, 1 Thessalonians, Philippians, Philemon). "Secondary Paul" is the disputed corpus (Ephesians, Colossians, 2 Thessalonians). The Pastorals (1-2 Timothy, Titus) come next, then Acts (Luke as a sympathetic but theologically distinct source), then later traditions like Jerome. Tabor only uses Primary Paul to establish Paul's actual views; everything else is corroborating or supplementary. This method is what lets him read Paul without harmonizing him into the orthodox synthesis the church later built.

Then comes the biographical reconstruction: Paul's birth in Galilee (Jerome's tradition places it in Gish, a small village in the north, exiled to Tarsus around 4 BCE after the post-Herodian "year of the three Messiahs" revolt that the Romans crushed brutally), his Pharisaic training under Gamaliel in Jerusalem, his self-identification with Isaiah 49 ("called from my mother's womb"), and his three-stage cosmic eschatology (Christ first, those who are Christ's at his parousia, then "God all in all" — possibly universalist). The conversation also unpacks why Tabor reads **Mark's Gospel as substantially Pauline** (close to Paul's theology on almost every point — "the Godzilla Mark fits in as Paul's view of the Jesus story"), the Antioch incident with Peter, the famous misreading of Mark 7 ("thus he declared all foods clean" is not in the Greek), the meat-sacrificed-to-idols question, and Paul's vegan-friendly ethic that Tabor himself shares for ethical-not-religious reasons.

Closing themes: the disastrous habit of harmonizing the gospels ("you will destroy all of them"), Tabor's rejection of "Paul-is-perfect" fundamentalism as historically incoherent, John Dominic Crossan's progressive Pauline ethics, and a final reflection — what would Paul say if he came back today and learned the church became Constantinian? "His eyes would be as big as saucers."

## Key Teachings

### Why Apocalyptic Framing Is the Master Key to Paul

The Waco excursus that opens the interview is not a detour — it's the methodological frame. Tabor and Philip Arnold worked the FBI for 51 days in 1993 trying to communicate with David Koresh by entering his apocalyptic worldview rather than dismissing it. **The same principle applies to Paul.** You cannot understand Paul's directives about marriage, slavery, gender, sexuality, or church discipline without recognizing he expected the end within 5-10 years. The "appointed time has grown very short" (1 Cor 7:29) isn't rhetorical flourish — it's the operating system underneath every recommendation.

This is also why importing Paul's social instructions into the modern era produces disasters. Slave owners in the 1850s American South cited Paul's "if you're a slave, stay a slave" (1 Cor 7) as scriptural authority. Paul never meant that as a permanent ethic — he meant it for the brief interval before the parousia. But once you cut out the apocalyptic urgency, you're left with the instruction as if it were eternal.

### The Source-Hierarchy Method for Pauline Studies

Tabor's most portable methodological contribution. He uses these tiers (and only Tier 1 to establish Paul's actual positions):

1. **Primary Paul** — the seven undisputed letters: Romans, 1 Corinthians, 2 Corinthians, Galatians, 1 Thessalonians, Philippians, Philemon
2. **Secondary Paul** — disputed but possibly Pauline: Ephesians, Colossians, 2 Thessalonians
3. **The Pastorals** — almost certainly pseudonymous: 1 Timothy, 2 Timothy, Titus
4. **Acts** — sympathetic to Paul but theologically distinct; usable for biographical detail when it harmonizes with Primary Paul
5. **Later traditions** — Jerome, Eusebius, church-father references

The discipline of this method is that Tabor can be challenged to show his work: every claim about Paul's actual views must be grounded in Primary Paul. Secondary Paul shouldn't contradict Primary Paul. Acts can supplement biography but not override theology. The Pastorals are not Paul.

Most pop-Christian apologetics never makes this distinction — they treat all 13 (or 14 with Hebrews) "Pauline" letters as equally Paul's words. The result is a hopelessly muddled Paul. Tabor's method is the first step in actually hearing what Paul said.

### Paul's Biographical Reconstruction

Working from Primary Paul plus a Jerome tradition Tabor finds plausible:

- **Born ~4 BCE** (possibly the same age as Jesus, given the standard 5 BCE Jesus date)
- **Birthplace:** Gish (Jewish village in northern Galilee — modern Hebrew Jish/Gish, ج/J or G/J depending on Arabic-Hebrew-English transliteration). Jerome preserves this tradition; the village still has restaurants memorializing Paul as a native son.
- **Exiled to Tarsus** as an infant during the Roman crushing of the post-Herodian revolt. The "year of the three Messiahs" (Judas the Galilean, Simon of Perea, the Shepherd King) ended with Roman legions from Syria putting down the revolt by force — Josephus reports 2,000 crucifixions. The Galilean population was exiled in large numbers.
- **Roman citizen by birth** — fits the exile-from-Israel scenario; the Herodians had perpetual Roman citizenship by Julius Caesar's grant.
- **Trade:** leather worker / "tent maker" (Greek *skenopoios*) — probably learned from his father, as Jewish boys were taught their father's trade. This is hide-and-leather work for Roman army tents, not high-status, but supportable on travel.
- **Pharisaic training in Jerusalem** — "studied at the feet of Gamaliel" per Acts; corroborates Paul's own claim in Galatians 1 that "I advanced beyond all my contemporaries in my own people."
- **Self-identification with Isaiah 49** — "called from my mother's womb"; Paul reads this Suffering Servant hymn as prophesying his own apostolic mission to the Gentiles.

### Paul as the Most Influential Person in Western History

Tabor's striking claim — more influential than Jesus, Moses, or anyone else. The argument: most people receive Jesus *through* Paul. The doctrines that shaped Western Christianity (justification by faith, substitutionary atonement, the resurrection-as-foundation, the moral architecture of sin/grace, the Gentile mission as God's plan, the eschatological framework) are Pauline first, only later read back into the gospels.

If you grow up Protestant, you've been catechized by Paul whether you know it or not. Even Catholics inherit a Pauline framework via Augustine. The historical Jesus of Galilee — Torah-observant, anti-Temple, vegetarian-ish, family-centered around James — is barely visible behind the Pauline overlay.

### Mark as a Pauline Gospel

A claim Tabor returns to repeatedly. The traditional patristic story (Mark as Peter's interpreter, written in Rome) is suspect. Tabor argues Mark's theology is close to Paul "on almost every point":
- No birth narrative — fits Paul's silence on Jesus's pre-passion life
- Empty tomb without appearances (in the original ending) — fits Paul's resurrection-as-transformation
- Anti-family, anti-Temple, anti-disciples tone — fits Paul's lone-wolf apostolic self-presentation
- The Eucharistic words at the Last Supper — derived from 1 Corinthians 11

**"Mark fits in as a way of casting the story of Jesus probably as Paul would largely agree with it."** This isn't a tangent — it implies the Pauline gospel was first, the synoptics second, and the historical Jesus has to be reconstructed by reading *against* the Pauline overlay.

### "Apocalyptic Celibacy" and 1 Corinthians 7

The famous Pauline advice ("better to remain single") is not asceticism for its own sake. Tabor's term: **apocalyptic celibacy**. The reasoning is in the text itself:

- 1 Cor 7:29 — "The appointed time has grown very short"
- 1 Cor 7:26 — "In view of the present distress"
- The "those who have wives be as those who have none" instruction

Paul thinks the parousia is so imminent that marriage, family-building, and slave-emancipation are all moot — they're rearranging deck chairs before a great transformation. Once the framework collapses (and 2000 years pass), what's left is just "celibacy is better" as a freestanding doctrine — a transformation Paul never intended.

### Galatians 3:28 in Eschatological Frame

"There is neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male nor female" reads as a progressive social ethic to modern eyes. Tabor reads it eschatologically. In the age to come — what Paul thought was about to break in — gender, social status, and ethnicity will no longer exist as categories. Believers already participate proleptically in this dissolution.

This also fits Jesus's saying about no marriage in the resurrection (Mark 12:25). And it explains why the same Paul who writes Gal 3:28 also says women should be silent (1 Cor 14), heads covered (1 Cor 11), and slaves should stay slaves (1 Cor 7) — those instructions apply to the brief interval *before* the new age fully arrives.

### The Antioch Incident (Galatians 2) — Not About Ham Sandwiches

The classic Paul-vs-Peter confrontation. Tabor's reading: it's not that Peter was eating non-kosher with Gentiles and got caught. Peter likely kept the Torah throughout. The dispute is about **table fellowship etiquette** — Peter being willing to share table with Gentiles when no one's watching, then physically separating when James's people arrive from Jerusalem.

The cultural reality Tabor sketches: it's a cafeteria scene. Peter takes his tray and moves to the Jewish side when the conservatives show up. He's not breaking Torah; he's signaling solidarity with the stricter wing at the moment it matters. Paul sees this as hypocrisy and calls him out.

### The Mark 7 Misreading

A specific scholarly correction worth marking: **"Thus he declared all foods clean" is not in the Greek text of Mark 7.** That phrase is a translator's gloss, not a quotation. Mark 7 is about *ritual* purity (hand-washing before meals — the Pharisaic minhag), not *dietary* purity (which foods are kosher).

Jesus's actual point in Mark 7: if you ate something that picked up Gentile defilement, it goes into the toilet and is flushed away. The body excretes physical impurity. The real impurity is what comes out of the heart — and Jesus lists 13 internal vices.

The misreading "thus he declared all foods clean" has been used for centuries to claim Jesus abolished kosher law. He didn't. Mark 7 doesn't say that. Both the historical Jesus (Tabor's view) and James the brother of Jesus continued to keep kosher.

### Paul's Vegetarian Cousins

Tabor's own ethical veganism (not religious — health and animal welfare) leads to a quiet observation in this video: James the brother of Jesus was vegetarian, per the Hegesippus tradition preserved in Eusebius. The Ebionites and Nazarenes after them were vegetarian. The Essene tradition (per Tabor's earlier work) trends vegetarian and anti-sacrifice. So Tabor's veganism puts him aesthetically closer to the Jewish-Christian tradition than the Pauline one — though he frames it as ethics, not religion.

### Mary's Age and the Galilean Setting

A telling reconstruction Tabor mentions: if Mary was betrothed to Joseph at the standard Jewish marriage age of 14-15 and gave birth to Jesus around 5 BCE, she would have been very young in the year of the three Messiahs (4 BCE). The Galilee was in upheaval. Paul's family — if exiled to Tarsus — would have been part of that displacement. The geography puts Jesus and Paul on overlapping cultural ground in ways the standard Acts narrative obscures.

This connects to Tabor's forthcoming Mary book, which has a chapter on the "year of the three Messiahs" framing.

### Don't Harmonize the Gospels

A methodological cri de coeur. Christians often try to make Matthew + Mark + Luke + John into one composite story. Tabor: "If you want to harmonize the gospels, you will destroy all the gospels."

Each gospel has a distinct voice, a distinct theology, a distinct social setting. Matthew rewrites Mark in specific directions (e.g., the picking-grain-on-Sabbath story is dramatically different in Mark vs. Matthew). Luke softens Mark's anti-disciple tone. John is a different theological universe altogether.

The harmonizing impulse is well-intentioned but creates a fourth gospel — the "composite" — that no one actually wrote and no one historically read. Better: read each gospel as its own voice and let the differences stand.

### The Critique of "Paul-Is-Perfect" Fundamentalism

In response to viewer questions, Tabor calls the "Paul is perfect / Paul is infallible scripture" position "kind of ludicrous when you think about it." His grounds: Paul is of his own time and place, his predictions about the parousia were wrong, his social ethics were calibrated to that error, and treating his every word as eternal doctrine has produced catastrophic results across 2000 years.

Tabor's redefinition of liberal/conservative: he calls himself "conservative" in the sense of being careful with each text in its own context, refusing to harmonize, and trusting that the texts can speak for themselves without being forced into a single doctrinal synthesis. The fundamentalists who insist on "Paul perfectly inspired" are, by Tabor's reckoning, the actually-liberal ones — they take massive interpretive liberties to force the text into a system.

### What Would Paul Say Today?

A speculative coda. If Paul came back today and learned that the parousia didn't arrive, that Constantine Christianized the Empire, that the church became persecutor instead of persecuted, that crusades and inquisitions and witch-hunts and slave-trade theology were all done in Jesus's name — Tabor: "His eyes would be as big as saucers."

The implication: Paul's actual gospel was time-bound and Jewish-eschatological. Almost everything Western Christianity made of him would be alien to him. The work of recovering "the historical Paul" is also the work of recognizing how far the church traveled from him while claiming his authority.

## Misconceptions Addressed

| Misconception | Tabor's Response |
|---------------|------------------|
| "Paul was born in Tarsus" | Acts says so, but Jerome preserves an earlier tradition that Paul was born in Gish (Galilee) and exiled to Tarsus as an infant during the 4 BCE revolt. Both can be true — born in Gish, exile-completed birth in Tarsus, depending on how you read "born." |
| "Paul is the same Paul throughout the New Testament" | No. Use the source-hierarchy method: Primary Paul (7 letters) is who we can confidently access. Secondary Paul, Pastorals, Acts, and Jerome get progressively less authoritative. Most popular Christian apologetics conflates all of them. |
| "Mark 7 says Jesus declared all foods clean" | "Thus he declared all foods clean" is not in the Greek. Mark 7 is about ritual handwashing (Pharisaic tradition), not dietary law. The historical Jesus and James the brother of Jesus both kept kosher. |
| "Paul and Peter agreed on everything" | Galatians 2's Antioch incident shows direct confrontation. Paul calls Peter out by name for "hypocrisy" over table fellowship. The Jerusalem-Antioch tension is real. |
| "Paul opposed the Torah for everyone" | Paul opposed *Gentile* conversion to Judaism with full Torah obligation. He accepted Torah observance for Jewish believers and operated within a Noahide-laws-plus-ethics framework for Gentiles. His "I became as a Jew to the Jews" suggests he himself stayed Torah-observant when among Jews. |
| "Paul was infallible / 'inerrant scripture'" | "It's just kind of ludicrous when you think about it." Paul predicted the parousia in his lifetime. It didn't happen. His apocalyptic-calibrated social ethics break when imported across 2000 years. |
| "Paul founded Christianity" | A more careful claim: Paul founded a *form* of Jewish messianism — a Gentile-friendly, blood-atonement-centered, justification-by-faith framework — that the Constantinian church later adopted as orthodoxy. The original Jerusalem community under James went a different direction (Nazarenes, Ebionites). |
