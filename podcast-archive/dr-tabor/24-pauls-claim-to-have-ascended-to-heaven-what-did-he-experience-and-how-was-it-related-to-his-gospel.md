## Summary

This is Tabor's home turf. *Paul's Ascent to Paradise* was his University of Chicago dissertation and remains, in his own words, his **most important book on Paul**. The interview walks through the book's central thesis: that Paul's claim in 2 Corinthians 12 to have been "caught up to the third heaven" and "into paradise" — where he heard "things unutterable, not lawful for a man to speak" — is not a curious footnote in Paul's biography. It is the **structural foundation of his entire apostolic authority and message**. Yet most Paul scholars barely mention it. Tabor's argument: you cannot understand Paul without understanding the ascent.

Tabor opens by parsing the Greek doublet in 2 Cor 12:2-4 — a carefully constructed literary parallel where Paul says "I knew a man... whether in body I do not know, whether out of the body I do not know... but God knows." He notes the rhetorical third-person device is well-attested in Hellenistic Greek literature: it's a roundabout form of boasting, and by the end of the passage Paul makes clear he's describing himself ("lest I be too elated by the surpassing greatness of the revelations, a thorn was given me in the flesh"). The Greek phrase Paul uses for the revelations is a compounded double verb meaning roughly *super-abundant beyond-revelation revelations* — Paul is essentially saying *I am getting more than anyone else*. This is the highest possible claim a first-century Jewish mystic could make.

Tabor then situates Paul's account in the broader Hellenistic-Jewish ascent literature: the Life of Adam and Eve, 2 Enoch (Slavonic), 1 Enoch (Ethiopic), the *Poimandres*, the *Mithras Liturgy*, Plato's myth of Er, the magical papyri, and Apuleius's first-person initiation account in *The Golden Ass* (the **only other personal ascent narrative we have from the ancient world**). Tabor categorizes ascent literature into four types: (1) ascent as forbidden invasion of heaven, (2) ascent for revelation, (3) the final permanent ascent at the end of the age, and (4) the **proleptic ascent** — an anticipatory taste of the final glorification before the end. Paul's experience is type 4: he was permitted to experience now what believers will experience at the parousia. This is what makes him uniquely qualified — in his own mind — to be the apostle who culminates God's plan for the cosmos.

Finally Tabor reveals what he thinks Paul actually experienced. His best guess: Paul was told he would **skip the intermediate sleep of Sheol** that other dead believers must wait through, and would experience direct glorification at death — the same metamorphosis Jesus underwent. This is what made him the proleptic anticipation of the new humanity, the "second Christ" (small c), the figure prophesied in Isaiah 49 commissioned to go to the nations and gather them in so that the *parousia* could finally arrive. Paul's gospel, in Tabor's reading, is not "Christ died for your sins so you can go to heaven." It is: **God is creating a new genus of being through Jesus and through me — and you are invited into that family, that glorification, that cosmic rule with Christ.** The ascent is the proof, the authority, and the message all at once.

## Key Teachings

### Paul's Ascent Is the Structural Foundation of His Apostolic Authority

Most Paul scholars treat 2 Corinthians 12 as a curiosity or autobiographical aside. Tabor's dissertation argues the opposite: **the ascent to paradise is the keystone of Paul's entire apostolic claim**. Paul claims direct, ongoing, conversational revelation from the exalted Jesus — "I received from the Lord," "I asked the Lord three times" — and the ascent to the third heaven and paradise is the supreme instance of that direct line. His authority does not derive from human tradition (Galatians 1: "not from men nor through man") and not from the Jerusalem pillars. It derives from the super-abundant revelations he alone received. Few other New Testament writers make first-person claims of this kind; Paul's whole self-understanding rests on it.

### The Greek Doublet: "In the Body or Out of the Body, I Do Not Know"

Tabor walks through the Greek text of 2 Cor 12:2-4 as a carefully crafted literary doublet — two parallel stanzas plus a conclusion. Stanza 1: "I knew a man in Christ more than fourteen years ago — whether in the body I do not know, whether out of the body I do not know, God knows — such a one caught up to the third heaven." Stanza 2: "And I know that this man — whether in the body or without the body, I do not know, God knows — was caught up into paradise." Conclusion: "and heard unutterable words (*arrēta*) which it is not lawful for a man to speak." The third-person framing ("I knew a man") is a known Hellenistic rhetorical device — a kind of indirect boasting. The thorn-in-the-flesh verses that follow make clear Paul is describing himself.

### Three Possible Meanings of "Unutterable Words"

The Greek *arrēta* in plural carries three possible senses, and Tabor thinks Paul may intend all of them at once:
1. **Indescribable** — there is no way to tell you what I saw, any more than you could describe color to someone born blind.
2. **Unutterable / forbidden** — secrets that must not be revealed; the standard motif in Hellenistic mystery literature.
3. **Both** — the experience is so beyond articulation *and* the revelation contains material that initiates are prohibited from disclosing.

This places Paul squarely within the mystery-religion tradition of his Hellenistic milieu, where the deepest initiations could not be spoken of by those who received them.

### Paul as Shaman / Mystical Conversationalist with Jesus

Tabor frames Paul in history-of-religions terms as a **shamanistic figure** negotiating revelations back and forth with the highest cosmic power next to God the Father. The intimacy is striking and concrete: "I asked the Lord three times" about the thorn, and three times got the same answer. "Satan hindered me" from making a trip. He receives words from the Lord about timing, travel, instructions for the assemblies. This is not vague mystical impression — it is conversation. When Paul says he received the Last Supper tradition "from the Lord" (1 Cor 11:23), Tabor takes him literally: he is claiming Jesus told him directly, not that he received it through the chain of tradition from Peter.

### Four Categories of Ascent in Antiquity

Tabor's typology of ancient ascent literature, which structures chapter 2 of *Paul's Ascent to Paradise*:

1. **Ascent as forbidden invasion** — mortals attempt heaven and are rebuked or expelled.
2. **Ascent for revelation** — Isaiah's heavenly throne vision, Enoch shown the secrets, the seer returns with knowledge.
3. **Final permanent ascent** — the eschatological transformation; 1 Thess 4's "caught up to meet the Lord in the air."
4. **Proleptic ascent** — the rare anticipatory taste. The mystic experiences now what is reserved for the end of days. Paul's experience is this fourth type.

### Paul's Ascent Is the Only First-Person Proleptic Ascent We Have

Tabor underscores a point he says he didn't emphasize enough in the book itself: **2 Corinthians 12 is the only first-person account of a proleptic ascent to heaven from the entire ancient world**. We have many literary, poetic, and mythic accounts — Enoch, the *Poimandres*, the *Mithras Liturgy*, Plato's myth of Er, the magical papyri — but they are framed as third-person reports, fictionalized narratives, or attributed pseudepigraphically to ancient figures. Paul, by contrast, is a known historical figure writing a real letter to a real community describing his own experience. The closest parallel is Apuleius's *Golden Ass* (the Isis initiation in book 11), but that is a work of fiction — Apuleius novelizing his initiation. Paul's account is unique.

### What Paul Actually Experienced (Tabor's Speculation)

After teasing his answer through the interview, Tabor lays out his best guess. Paul normally believed the dead go to Sheol/Hades — "paradise" in the sense of the place where the blessed dead rest awaiting resurrection (Luke 16's rich man and Lazarus; Jesus to the thief: "today you shall be with me in paradise"). The standard pattern: die, sleep, wait for resurrection at the *parousia*, then be transformed. Tabor's hypothesis: **Paul was told he would skip the sleeping stage**. At his death he would experience direct, immediate glorification — the same metamorphosis Jesus underwent — without having to wait. This would parallel the Apuleius account where the initiate descends to the underworld, ascends through the heavens, sees the gods above and below face to face, and returns transformed. The parallel passage is Philippians 1:23, where Paul says he desires "to depart and be with Christ, which is far better" — language that breaks from his earlier expectation of being alive at the parousia.

### The Thorn in the Flesh: Likely a Sexual Temptation

The thorn is given, Paul says, "to keep me from being too elated by the surpassing greatness of the revelations." Over 50 scholarly proposals exist: epilepsy, extreme nearsightedness ("Mr. Magoo"), a disfiguring facial affliction (Galatians 4:14, "you did not despise my bodily ailment"), migraine headaches. Tabor thinks the most plausible candidate, given Paul's language elsewhere about the flesh warring against the spirit (Romans 7), and given the parallel in later monastic literature about demonic sexual temptations of celibate ascetics, is **persistent sexual temptation**. Paul calls the thorn "an angel of Satan" (*angelos satana*) — a demonic messenger. The pattern of dream-Temptations harassing celibate monks is well-attested in later Christian sources, and Paul's struggle in Romans 7 ("the good I would, I do not... O wretched man that I am") fits that frame.

### Paul Believed He Was Prophesied in Isaiah 49

One of the boldest claims in the book: Paul reads the suffering-servant material of Isaiah (chapters 42, 49, 51, 53) as describing not one figure but **two** — one sent to Israel ("the circumcision," fulfilled by Jesus) and one sent to the nations ("the uncircumcision," fulfilled by Paul). Isaiah 49 in particular says the servant is "formed from the womb" to be a light to the nations — and Paul in Galatians 1:15 echoes this exactly: "He who set me apart before I was born and called me through his grace..." This is, Tabor says bluntly, **a very high view of the self**. Paul believed he was personally prophesied in scripture, set apart from the womb, given a unique cosmic role that would culminate the final events of history. A modern psychologist would have something to say about this — but in the ancient apocalyptic worldview it was a coherent claim.

### Paul as "Second Christ" (Small C)

Tabor is willing to put it baldly: in Paul's own self-understanding, **he is the second Christ**. Not in the sense that he is divine or messianic in the way Jesus is — Jesus is the firstborn, the older brother, the one already glorified. But Paul sees himself as the proleptic anticipation of the next stage: the figure who completes what Jesus began. Jesus went to Israel; Paul goes to the nations. Jesus suffered the servant's sufferings; Paul bears in his body "the marks (*stigmata*) of the Lord Jesus." Jesus was the firstfruits; Paul is the one whose mission must be completed before the harvest comes in. Romans 11: "until the fullness of the Gentiles come in" — and *Paul is the one bringing them in*. The parousia itself is being held back, in Paul's mind, until he finishes his work.

### Paul's Gospel Is Not "Christ Died for Your Sins"

Tabor draws a sharp line between standard early Christian preaching and what is **uniquely Pauline**. The atoning death, the call to repentance, the resurrection hope — Peter preached all of this; James preached ethical teaching grounded in Jesus; the Book of James reads like Jewish wisdom literature with Christian framing. None of that is the new revelation Paul claims. **Paul's unique gospel is the mystery now revealed: God is creating a new genus of beings through Jesus.** Children of God. Offspring of God. Immortalized, glorified, given the same status and power as Jesus himself. "Christ in you, the hope of glory" (Col 1:27 — though disputed Pauline). The believer's destiny is not "go to heaven when you die" but **be incorporated into a new family of glorified cosmic beings who rule above the angels with Christ as their elder brother**. This is what the ascent revealed. This is what makes Paul's gospel different from anyone else's.

### The 1 Corinthians 5 "Hand Over to Satan" Passage as Reverse Baptism

In a long aside Tabor unpacks 1 Corinthians 5:3-5, where Paul says of the man living with his father's wife: "I have already pronounced judgment... when you are assembled, and my spirit is present, with the power of our Lord Jesus, you are to deliver this man to Satan for the destruction of the flesh." Tabor reads this as a formal ceremony — almost a séance-like assembly where Paul projects his spirit, the spirit of Christ is invoked, and the offender is **officially ex-baptized**: pushed out of the protective sphere of the body of Christ and back into the unprotected world where Satan's powers operate freely. The hope is that the affliction will bring the man to repentance before the day of the Lord, which Paul thinks is imminent — months or years away, not decades, certainly not millennia.

### Paul's Apocalyptic Timeline Was Short

Tabor returns repeatedly to a point easy to forget: Paul was a thoroughgoing apocalypticist. When he writes about celibacy in 1 Corinthians 7, the *apocalyptic celibacy* is conditional on the imminent end ("the appointed time has grown very short"). It parallels Jesus's "woe to those who are pregnant or nursing in those days" (Mark 13) — not anti-family, but practical advice for the final crisis. Paul never imagined the parousia stretching out for thousands of years. He thought he would live to see it. Only at the very end, in Philippians, does he begin to allow that he might die first. This compressed timeline shapes everything Paul writes — the urgency, the radical demands, the willingness to set aside marriage and possessions and normal life. He believed it was almost over.
