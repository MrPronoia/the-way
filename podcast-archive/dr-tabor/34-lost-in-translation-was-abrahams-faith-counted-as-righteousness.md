## Summary

This is the second installment in Tabor's "Lost in Translation" series — a focused, surgical treatment of **Genesis 15:6**, the second of the two pillar texts on which Paul builds his gospel of justification by faith. (The first, Habakkuk 2:4, was covered in the previous video.) Paul quotes Genesis 15:6 in Romans 4:3 as: "Abraham believed God and it was reckoned to him as righteousness" — and reads it to mean that Abraham, a sinner like everyone else, became righteous *by* his act of faith. From this, Paul builds the foundational claim of Evangelical-Orthodox Christianity: nobody is righteous, everyone is damned, and the only path to righteousness is faith in the shed blood of Jesus.

Tabor's argument is sharp: **the Hebrew of Genesis 15:6 doesn't say what Paul thinks it says.** Read in its original context — Abraham is 99, childless, being shown the stars and promised descendants beyond counting — the verse is not about how a sinful Abraham gets justified. Abraham isn't in a state of sin. He's being asked whether he trusts a promise he cannot see how God will keep. Tabor's better reading: "He put his faith in the Lord, and he counted *Him* [God] to be righteous" — meaning Abraham reckoned God as trustworthy, faithful to fulfill the promise of a son. The grammar supports it. The context demands it. And it perfectly parallels Habakkuk 2:4 — both verses describe how *a righteous person lives* (by trusting God's promises through the delay), not how an unrighteous person *becomes* righteous. This is companion material to Video #02, which laid out four such Pauline misappropriations; this video drills into one of them with the patience a single text deserves.

## Key Teachings

### Paul's Reading of Genesis 15:6 in Romans 4

Paul's argument in Romans 4 runs: "If Abraham was justified by works, he has something to boast about, but not before God. For what does the scripture say? *Abraham believed God and it was reckoned to him as righteousness.*" Then Paul generalizes: "to one who does not work but trusts him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is reckoned as righteousness." The reading Paul wants: Abraham was unrighteous, he had faith, God credited that faith as righteousness — therefore faith (not works, not Torah, not circumcision) is the mechanism by which sinners are justified. This is the engine of the entire Pauline gospel, and it depends on Genesis 15:6 saying what Paul claims it says.

### What Genesis 15 Is Actually About

Read verses 3-5 before verse 6. Abraham complains: "you haven't given me any offspring; a slave born in my house will be my heir." God responds: this man shall not be your heir — your own son shall be. Then God brings Abraham outside the tent, has him look up at the stars, and says "so shall your descendants be." **Then comes verse 6.** Abraham is 99 years old. Sarah is around 90. The promise is biologically impossible from where Abraham is standing. The verse is about whether Abraham trusts a God who has promised the unseeable. Abraham is not a sinner being justified — he is a righteous patriarch being asked to trust.

### The Hebrew Bible Definition of "Righteous"

Tabor presses a point that gets erased in Pauline theology: in the Hebrew Bible, a "righteous person" is not someone who has never sinned or achieved perfection. It is someone who **orients toward God, trusts God, walks humbly, and repents when they fail.** Noah is called righteous. David — adulterer, murderer — prays Psalm 51 and is forgiven without any blood-of-Jesus framework: "Have mercy on me O God according to your grace and abundant mercy, blot out my transgressions." Even James in the New Testament knows this — "the prayer of a righteous person avails much." Righteousness is relational, not forensic.

### The Better Translation — Abraham Counted God as Righteous

The Hebrew of Genesis 15:6 can grammatically be read: *"He [Abraham] put his faith in the Lord, and he [Abraham] thought him [the Lord] to be righteous."* The verb (reckon / consider / think) takes Abraham as subject and God as object. Tabor's gloss: "I'm putting my faith in you, that you will do what you promised, even though I can't see how it's going to happen." Abraham counts God as trustworthy. The verse is not about Abraham acquiring righteousness — it is about Abraham acknowledging God's righteousness (faithfulness to promise).

This reading lines up perfectly with the Habakkuk parallel from the previous video. Habakkuk 2:4 in context: "the vision tarries; wait for it; the righteous one will live by faith [in the vision]." Genesis 15:6 in context: Abraham, awaiting an impossible promise, trusts God's faithfulness. **Both verses describe how a righteous person lives — neither describes how an unrighteous person becomes righteous.** Paul has flipped both.

### Why This Misreading Matters — Two Verses, an Entire Gospel

Tabor's point is sharp: this isn't a stray rabbinic allusion like Matthew's "out of Egypt I called my son" (which everyone knows is a typological flourish, not a doctrinal cornerstone). Paul **explicitly bases his gospel on these two verses.** Romans 1:17 (Habakkuk) and Romans 4:3 (Genesis 15:6) are the textual foundation Paul cites when he says "my gospel which I preach is based upon this." Misread one allusion and you have a literary flourish gone awry. Misread the two verses your entire soteriology rests on, and you have built a different religion — one in which humans are hopelessly wicked, faith in shed blood is the only path, and the Hebrew Bible's abundant grace gets retroactively recategorized as "law" that no one could keep.

Compare Luke 18 — the Pharisee and the tax collector. Both pray in the temple. No sacrifice, no blood, no faith-credit-mechanism. The tax collector says "God be merciful to me, a sinner" and goes home justified. That is the Hebrew Bible's actual theology of forgiveness. Paul's faith-in-the-blood construct is the novelty.
