## Summary

This is the **twelfth and final installment** of Tabor's Death & Afterlife series — and the one where he names the contradiction Christianity has never resolved and, in his judgment, cannot resolve. The earliest Christian witness to resurrection (Paul, 1 Cor 15) describes a **transformation into a glorious spirit-body** that he cannot even describe — a "life-giving spirit," not flesh and bone. But the later gospel accounts (Matthew, Luke, John) depict the risen Jesus as a **flesh-and-bone physical body** that eats fish, shows his wounds, and explicitly tells the disciples "a spirit does not have flesh and bones as you see I have." Then the church doubled down. The early creeds — Apostles', Nicene, the Council of Lyon in 1274 — confess belief in "the resurrection of *the flesh*" (Greek *sarx*, Latin *carnes*). Not "the body" generically. The flesh. The literal meat that hangs on our bones. "This flesh that we now possess will rise."

Tabor's verdict: that doctrine is exegetically forced, scientifically incoherent, and theologically at war with Paul. The bulk of the lecture is a walk through Augustine's *City of God*, Book 22 — where Augustine, working in the early fifth century, takes the resurrection-of-the-flesh dogma to its logical conclusions and has to answer ridiculous questions raised by his Hellenistic critics. What about abortions? They get raised. What about infants? They'll be raised at adult size. What about cannibalism — if person A eats person B, who gets the flesh back? God reassembles the very atoms. What about deformities? Symmetrical and perfected. Everyone resurrected at age 33 (Jesus's age), still male and female, still with eyes, nose, ears, fingernails — but with *incorruptible* flesh. Tabor leans heavily on Paula Fredriksen's article **"Vile Bodies: Paul and Augustine on the Resurrection of the Flesh"** to show that Augustine arrived at this position by *misreading Paul*, especially the load-bearing line in 1 Corinthians 15:50 — **"flesh and blood will not inherit the kingdom of God."** Paul's view of resurrection has nothing to do with flesh and blood. Augustine's view requires it.

The closer is the cost. **Christian theologians had to choose**, and they chose the gospel narratives over Paul. They had to, because Luke 24's Jesus says "handle me and see — a spirit does not have flesh and bones." If that's the prototype for everyone's resurrection, then everyone gets raised in their literal flesh forever. Origen tried to argue Paul's transformation framework in the third century and was condemned for denying the resurrection of the flesh. Augustine's flesh-resurrection won and became Catholic and Protestant orthodoxy. But 99% of Christians today have quietly drifted back to a *Platonic* soul-flight framework anyway — at the casket they say "Grandpa is in heaven now," which is neither Paul *nor* the Catechism. The result: most of Christianity lives inside a contradiction it doesn't know it's holding. Soul-flight at funerals, flesh-resurrection in the creed, transformation in Paul. Three different eschatologies stitched together with theological duct tape. That's the unsolvable dilemma.

## Key Teachings

### Christianity's Unsolvable Dilemma — Three Incompatible Eschatologies

The headline framing of the whole video. Christianity inherited three frameworks that don't fit:

1. **Pauline transformation** (1 Cor 15) — the perishable dust-body is *not* what gets raised; God gives a new spiritual body, a "life-giving spirit" mode of existence that Paul cannot even describe. Flesh and blood do not inherit.
2. **Gospel flesh-resurrection** (Luke 24, John 20-21) — the risen Jesus is explicitly a flesh-and-bone physical body. He shows wounds. He eats broiled fish. He invites Thomas to touch.
3. **Platonic immortal-soul-flight** (popular Christianity from Augustine onward) — at death the soul leaves the body and goes to be with Christ in heaven; the body becomes irrelevant.

The official patristic and medieval church chose framework #2 and called it "the resurrection of the flesh" in the creeds. Popular Christianity quietly defected to framework #3. Framework #1 — what Paul actually said — got buried by both.

### The Apostles' Creed Says "Resurrection of the FLESH" — Not the Body

Tabor walks through what most modern Christians don't know they're supposed to believe. The Apostles' Creed, the Nicene Creed, the Chalcedonian Creed, and the **Council of Lyon (1274 CE)** all confess: *credo in carnis resurrectionem* — "I believe in the resurrection of the flesh." Not the *body* (which could be hedged). The **flesh** — Greek *sarx*, Latin *carnes / carnem*. The meat that hangs on the bones.

The Council of Lyon stated it as bluntly as any council ever stated anything: **"We believe in the true resurrection of this flesh that we now possess."** This flesh. Not new flesh. Not generic body. *This* flesh. The same body Mary gave birth to. The same cells (Augustine doesn't know about cells, but the principle is the same atoms). Tabor flags the Catholic catechism quote: "the flesh is the hinge of salvation... we believe in God who is creator of the flesh, in the Word made flesh, in the resurrection of the flesh."

99% of Christians have never heard this and would not recognize it as their faith. That's the gap.

### Augustine's City of God, Book 22 — Working Out the Absurdities

The longest stretch of the lecture is a tour of Augustine's *City of God*, Book 22 — written ~AD 428 — where Augustine, having committed to flesh-resurrection, has to answer the ridicule of his Hellenistic neoplatonist critics. The questions are not strawmen; they are the questions any thinking person would ask if you insist that *this very flesh* rises eternally.

- **Abortions:** Are aborted/miscarried fetuses raised? Augustine: yes, they attain resurrection.
- **Infants:** Are children raised at the size they died? Augustine: no, all raised at adult stature (age 33, like the Lord).
- **Male and female:** Are women raised female? Augustine: yes, because Jesus was raised male and the resurrected don't lose sex distinction. But — citing Jesus's "they neither marry nor are given in marriage" — they won't have carnal/sexual desires.
- **Bodily blemishes and deformities:** Erased. Everyone "as beautiful as possible," symmetrical, perfected.
- **Cannibalism:** If person A is eaten by person B (or by fish, or by another human), who gets the flesh? Augustine: God reassembles the very atoms. Whatever was *yours* comes back to you.
- **Baldness, fatness, leanness:** All corrected in the resurrection.
- **The spiritual body:** Forced by Paul's *soma pneumatikon* language, Augustine concludes the spiritual body is *spiritual flesh* — incorruptible flesh, like Adam and Eve would have had if they'd eaten from the tree of life. Still flesh. Just immortal.

Tabor's tone here is "this is almost funny — I mean, it is funny." But the doctrine is dead serious. This is the official Christian position for 1700 years.

### Paula Fredriksen — "Vile Bodies: Paul and Augustine on the Resurrection of the Flesh"

The scholarly anchor of the whole lecture. Fredriksen's article (in the 1991 Festschrift for Karl-Friedrich, edited by Mark Burrows and Paul Rorem, published by Eerdmans) cuts to the core: Augustine's position is **exegetically untenable** because he has to derive it from Paul, and Paul says the opposite.

Fredriksen's opening sentence is the killer: *"The body that you gave breakfast to this morning, the body that helped you navigate your automobile, the body with which you at this moment occupy your chair, is, according to Augustine, this very same body that will dwell in the heavens and see God. This is an extraordinary claim. It was scarcely coherent, and it was certainly unscientific, when he presented it in AD 428."*

And then the kicker: **Augustine "was scarcely helped in formulating it by having to base his position on an exegesis of Paul, mainly 1 Corinthians 15:50 — 'flesh and blood will not inherit the kingdom of God.'"**

Paul says flesh and blood will not inherit. Augustine says the flesh will inherit. Augustine wins because the gospels say Jesus was raised in the flesh. But Augustine had to redefine the word "flesh" to make it work.

### 1 Corinthians 15:50 — Paul's Veto on the Whole Dogma

The single most important verse in this lecture. Paul: **"Flesh and blood will not inherit the kingdom of God; nor does the perishable inherit the imperishable."**

Tabor: if you take that verse seriously, the entire "resurrection of the flesh" doctrine collapses. Paul is explicit. The dust-body is not what gets raised. What gets raised is a *spiritual body* — *soma pneumatikon*, a wind-body, a spirit mode of existence. It is sown a physical body, it is raised a spiritual body. Not the same body with a tune-up. A new mode of existence.

Augustine's solution — that the spiritual body is *spiritual flesh*, incorruptible flesh — is a contradiction in terms read backward into Paul. Paul's whole point in 1 Cor 15 is that the seed is not the plant. The caterpillar is not the butterfly. The dust-body is not the resurrection body.

### Why the Church Chose Augustine Over Origen

The third-century theologian **Origen of Alexandria** argued — closer to Paul — that the resurrection body would be fundamentally transformed, not flesh. He acknowledged some continuity (the same person is raised) but the substance is changed into something like Paul's "spiritual body." For this and other positions Origen was **posthumously condemned** by later church councils, and one of the explicit charges against him was that he *denied the resurrection of the flesh*.

Augustine's flesh-resurrection won. It became Catholic and Protestant orthodoxy. The Pauline transformation framework — the *earliest* Christian view, and the only first-person witness — got declared heresy.

Tabor's read: this happened because the gospels demanded it. If Jesus came out of the tomb in his flesh-and-bone body (and Luke 24 says he did, explicitly contradicting "a spirit"), and if Jesus is the prototype of resurrection, then everybody's resurrection has to be flesh-and-bone. Augustine's logic is internally consistent given the gospels. It just can't be reconciled with Paul.

### The Funeral Defection — Popular Christianity Quietly Went Platonic

The contradiction Tabor flagged in video #14 returns here. Even though the *official* Christian doctrine (creed-level, council-level, catechism-level) is the resurrection of the flesh, **99% of Christians at funerals and graveside services talk like Platonists**: "Dear George has departed. Don't mourn for him. He's with Christ. The body is just the shell."

That is neither Paul *nor* Augustine. That's Socrates. The body as inferior, corrupted, weak. The spirit as eternal, immortal, going back to God. Tabor: "none of that is proper Christian doctrine." The flock has quietly drifted to a framework the creeds explicitly reject — and the pastors who lead funerals usually don't notice.

The result: Christianity now holds three eschatologies simultaneously and most Christians don't see the contradiction.
- At the casket: Platonic soul-flight.
- In the creed: Augustinian flesh-resurrection.
- In Paul: spiritual-body transformation.

These are three different religions sharing one liturgy.

### Tabor's Historian's Verdict — Paul's View Is the Earliest and the Only First-Person Witness

The closing move. Tabor brings the historian's discipline to bear: **the earliest extant Christian text on resurrection is 1 Corinthians 15 (mid-50s CE). The earliest first-person witness to having seen the risen Jesus is Paul.** Mark (~70 CE) has no resurrection appearance at all — just an empty tomb. The fleshly-Jesus appearances (Matthew 28, Luke 24, John 20-21) are later (~80-95 CE).

When Paul, the only first-person witness, is asked "what kind of body?" he says he cannot describe it. Not flesh and blood. Not a meal-eating, wound-touching corpse. A *life-giving spirit*. A glorious, indescribable mode of existence appropriate to the new heavenly reality.

If Paul is the earliest and the only first-person witness, then **the fleshly-resurrection framework is not original Christianity**. It is a later narrative development. The creeds doubled down on the later development. Augustine systematized the later development. The whole edifice rests on Luke 24 over against 1 Corinthians 15.

Tabor doesn't claim to "solve" the dilemma. He claims it's *unsolvable* — which is the title and the thesis. The best one can do is the historian's work: recognize that Paul's view is earlier and the gospels' view is later, and let people decide what to do with that.
