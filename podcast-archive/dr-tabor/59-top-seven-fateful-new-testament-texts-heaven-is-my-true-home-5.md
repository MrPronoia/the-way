## Summary

Installment five of Tabor's "Seven Fateful New Testament Texts" series zeroes in on **2 Corinthians 4:18** — *"We look not to the things that are seen but to the things that are unseen; for the things that are seen are transient, but the things that are unseen are eternal."* Read in isolation, this single Pauline sentence becomes the doctrinal seedbed of what Tabor calls **"the bad idea that took over the world"** — the conviction that the visible material earth is a shadow, the real world is elsewhere, and the proper Christian orientation is *out of here and up there*. Tabor argues this reading is more **Plato than Paul**. Lifted from its apocalyptic context (where Paul means the *coming* cosmic transformation of this present world), the verse fueled two thousand years of world-denial: indifference to suffering ("there's justice in the world to come"), tolerance of slavery and mistreatment ("everything will be made right later"), and the still-living evangelistic cliche "if you died tonight, do you know you'd go to heaven?"

The video's load-bearing move is to contrast this Platonic frame with the **Hebraic creation theology** of Genesis 1-3. The earth is called *good* seven times. Humans are given *responsibility* (King James "dominion" — Tabor: better translated as management) to dress and keep the garden. The Hebrew Bible is relentlessly focused on **living rightly here and now** — not escaping. Tabor traces the bad idea's archaeological origin to the **Orphic-Pythagorean golden tablets** (4th c. BCE, found in Mediterranean tombs), which inscribed prayer formulas for the dying soul to recite while descending into Hades: *"I am a child of Earth and Starry Heaven, but my race is of Heaven alone."* The tablets coach the dead to avoid the Spring of Lethe (forgetfulness, which traps you in reincarnation) and find the Lake of Memory — gnostic in the original sense: *you must know you don't really belong here*. Tabor's verdict: this is the cosmology Plato systematized, the Hellenistic age absorbed, the New Testament got read through, and Christian hymnody canonized ("This world is not my home, I'm just a pilgrim passing through"). Paul actually meant the opposite. The corrective text Tabor reaches for is Robert Frost's *Birches*: "Earth's the right place for love. I don't know where it's likely to go better." That, Tabor says, is closer to the Hebrew Bible than 90% of Christian preaching.

## Key Teachings

### What Paul Actually Meant by "Things Unseen"

The fateful verse is 2 Corinthians 4:18 — but Tabor stresses that in **context**, Paul is talking about the *future*, when Jesus the Messiah returns in the clouds of heaven and transforms mortal bodies into immortality and creates **this new world**. The "unseen things" are not a parallel non-material realm where disembodied souls fly to. They are the not-yet-visible cosmic transformation of the present earth at the parousia. Paul's framework is **eschatological**, not metaphysical. The shadow-vs-real-world dualism comes from somewhere else. Pull the verse out of its apocalyptic frame and you've handed Plato a Pauline pull-quote he never owned.

### The Hebraic Frame — Earth Is the Good Place

Genesis 1-3 sets down the foundational picture. The earth is called *good* seven times — and after humans are made, *very good*. Adam and Eve are placed in the garden and given "dominion" (Hebrew: better translated as *responsibility* or *management*) — to dress and keep it. The Hebrew Bible is consistently this-worldly: laws given for ethical life in this world (the Ten Commandments), care for animals (the Torah's humane treatment laws Paul stretches in 1 Cor 9), procreation as the first command. Tabor's compact formulation: *"The secret things, the unseen things, belong to God; the things that are revealed or seen are what belong to us"* (Deut 29:29). **One world at a time, please.** Fixing this world and making it as it could potentially be — peace and justice upon the earth — is the human task. Not escape.

### Tracing the Bad Idea — The Orphic Golden Tablets

The earliest archaeological evidence Tabor finds for "Heaven is my true home" theology is the **golden plates** (or *lamellae*) — thin gold-foil prayer sheets buried with the dead, found throughout the Mediterranean from the 4th century BCE onward (roughly contemporaneous with Plato). Likely **Orphic or Pythagorean** in origin. They are prayer formulas the dead soul recites while descending into Hades. The instructions: do not drink from the Spring of Lethe (forgetfulness — drink it and you reincarnate, repeating the cycle of birth and rebirth). Find instead the Lake of Memory, guarded by powers who will not give you water unless you can declare your true identity:

> *"I am a child of Earth and Starry Heaven, but my race is of Heaven alone. This you know yourselves. Parched with thirst I perish. Give me quickly the cold water flowing from the Lake of Memory."*

You drink because you are **gnostic** — you *know* you don't actually belong on earth. Tabor flags this as the seedbed: the bad idea is not Christian in origin. It is pre-Platonic mystery cult, systematized by Plato (cave allegory, *Phaedo*, *Timaeus*), absorbed by the Hellenistic world, and then read back into the New Testament centuries later when Christian theologians fused Plato with Paul.

### How the Bad Idea Caused Real Harm

Tabor is blunt about the consequences. Once you orient your life toward an unseen world *over against* this world, you license tolerance for evil in this world. *Humans could be enslaved, humans could be mistreated — because after all, there's justice in the world to come and everything will be made right.* This is not abstract. It is the engine behind centuries of Christian quietism about social injustice, the otherworldly resignation of "your reward is in heaven," and the famous Marxist diagnosis of religion as opiate. Tabor's college-campus anecdote brings it home: the *Four Spiritual Laws* booklet, the "if you died tonight, do you absolutely know you'd go to heaven?" opener — the entire framework trades on the bad idea that the point of this life is to secure passage to the next. Answer the question with "I'm not focusing on the world to come — I'm focusing on the world we've been given," and the evangelistic script breaks.

### Robert Frost's *Birches* — The Hebraic Corrective in a Modern Voice

Tabor reads from Frost's *Birches* — the boy who climbs birch trees and lets his weight bend them slowly back down. The lines Tabor draws out:

> *May no fate willfully misunderstand me / and half grant what I wish and snatch me away / not to return. Earth's the right place for love. / I don't know where it's likely to go better.*

The poem is the Hebraic instinct in modern voice. We can look at the stars, have our head in the heavens, imagine unseen things — but **the earth is the human place**. Tabor pairs the Frost reading with a recommendation of Nicholas Cage's *City of Angels* (1998), where the angel chooses embodied earthly love over disembodied heavenly existence. Same point. The earth is the place to be. You climb up — and you come back.

### Paul's Genuine Eschatology vs. the Platonized Misreading

Tabor doesn't soften Paul. Paul absolutely does say "look to the things unseen." Paul absolutely does have an apocalyptic-cosmic frame in which the present age is "passing away." But Paul's *unseen* is the **coming new creation of this earth** — the cosmic transformation when Christ returns. Romans 8: the whole creation groaning in labor pains, awaiting its release into a new earth. Revelation 21: new heavens and new earth, God dwelling with humans on the earth. The Pauline hope is **earth renewed**, not **earth escaped**. The misreading turns "the new earth" into "heaven somewhere else" — and once that misreading is locked in, Christian hymnody, funeral liturgy, and evangelistic theology follow it for two millennia.

## Misconceptions Addressed

| Misconception | Tabor's Response |
|---------------|------------------|
| "Christianity teaches that heaven is your true home and earth is just a temporary stopover" | This is Orphic-Platonic mystery-cult theology, not Christian or Hebraic. The Hebrew Bible says God made the earth and called it *very good*; humans were placed in the garden as stewards, given the first command to "be fruitful and multiply and fill the earth." Paul's actual eschatology is *new earth*, not *escape from earth*. |
| "When Paul says 'look to the unseen,' he means a parallel non-material realm where souls go after death" | In context (2 Cor 4-5, 1 Cor 15, Romans 8), Paul's "unseen" is the **coming cosmic transformation** of this present world at the parousia. It is temporally future, not spatially elsewhere. Reading it as Platonic two-worlds metaphysics is exactly the misreading Tabor calls fateful. |
| "If you died tonight, do you know for certain you'd go to heaven?" — the standard evangelism opener | The question is built on the bad idea. It assumes the point of life is to secure passage to a disembodied heavenly home. Answer it with "I'm not focused on the world to come, because this is the world we've been given by the ultimate forces of the universe — and this is the world before us, with our choices of choosing life and good rather than death" — and the entire evangelistic script collapses. |
| "Christian hymns like 'This World Is Not My Home' express authentic biblical teaching" | The hymn quotes the Orphic golden tablet almost verbatim. It is gnostic-Platonic theology dressed in Christian vocabulary. The Hebrew Bible and the historical Jesus and Paul teach the opposite: the earth *is* the human home, and the eschatological hope is its renewal. |
| "Other-worldly orientation is the spiritual high ground" | It has historically licensed indifference to slavery, suffering, environmental destruction, and social injustice. "There's justice in the world to come" has been the church's quietest excuse for a thousand failures to act for justice now. The Hebrew prophets — and Jesus — were relentlessly *this-worldly* in their ethical demand. |
| "Plato and Paul are saying the same thing in different vocabulary" | They are saying *opposite* things. Plato wants out of the body and away from earth. Paul wants the cosmos transformed and the body re-clothed with immortality on a renewed earth. The fusion was forged by later church theologians and it dominates Christian funerals and hymnody to this day. |
| "Matter is unspiritual" | Even Einstein defined atoms as manifestations of energy. The material world has dimensions of consciousness and spirituality. The body/spirit dualism Christianity inherited from Hellenism is not a feature of the Hebrew Bible. |
