## Summary

Tabor opens with a thought experiment that cuts through almost every popular debate about the resurrection: if archaeologists found Jesus's bones in a first-century Jerusalem tomb, and the identification was airtight, would that overthrow the earliest Christian faith in resurrection? Millions of modern Christians assume yes — "find the bones, no resurrection." But Tabor argues, with Crossan in agreement, that **the answer is no**, because the earliest Pauline framework was never about a revived corpse. Paul says flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God. The body is shed off "like an old garment." If you walked up to Paul and said, "We found the Talpiot tomb, the bones are Jesus's," Crossan jokes Paul would tell you to "get a life — the body molders into dust, the spirit gets its own new body." It would not bother Paul. **It would bother Luke** — because Luke is the one who has Jesus eating fish after the resurrection.

The bulk of the video is Tabor's textual case for why the earliest resurrection accounts are visionary and post-burial, not corpse-revival. He walks through three texts that scholars and apologists routinely skip: the original ending of Mark (16:1-8), the first ten verses of John 20, and the Gospel of Peter (discovered 1886 in Egypt). All three describe the same scenario: women come to the tomb early Sunday, the stone is already moved, the body is gone, a young man (not an angel) says he has been "lifted up" — and the disciples go home in grief. The Gospel of Peter explicitly says they wept and mourned for seven days, then Peter said, "I'm going fishing." That is not the behavior of people who just had breakfast with a resuscitated friend.

Tabor's reconstruction: Joseph of Arimathea performs an emergency Passover-eve burial in a nearby unused tomb at Golgotha. Saturday night after Sabbath ends, his burial party moves the body to a permanent grave — most likely on his own family property. Sunday morning the women arrive to finish anointing and find the temporary tomb empty. The disciples flee to Galilee in despair, and only **there** do the visionary sightings of the glorified Christ begin — the same kind of vision Paul later experiences and equates with theirs in 1 Corinthians 15. Matthew, Luke, and John then progressively embellish the story with earthquakes, dazzling angels, road encounters, and wound-touching — apologetic material that doesn't fit the earliest three texts at all.

## Key Teachings

### Pauline Resurrection Is Cosmic Transformation, Not Corpse Revival

The whole question of "what if we found the bones?" presupposes that resurrection means a revived corpse. For Paul, it doesn't. Paul calls the resurrection body a "life-giving spirit" and explicitly states "flesh and blood cannot enter the kingdom of God." The dust-body is shed like old clothing; the new body is a glorified, immortal Spirit being. **Finding bones leaves this framework completely undisturbed.** Crossan's line: "The body molders into dust, the spirit gets its own new body. Of course."

This is why the Talpiot tomb question is not the threat to early Christianity that modern apologists assume. The threat is real for *Luke's* resurrection — the eating-fish, touch-my-wounds Jesus. It is not a threat for Paul's resurrection, which is the earlier framework.

### Why Luke (Not Paul) Would Be Tearing His Hair Out

Crossan's vivid framing: ask Paul whether bones in a tomb falsify resurrection and Paul shrugs. Ask Luke and he panics. Why? Because Luke specifically wrote Jesus eating broiled fish (Luke 24) and the post-resurrection meals to *prove* physicality against second-century skeptics like Celsus. Luke's whole rhetorical move is, "Look, he's not a ghost — he's flesh." A bone find collapses Luke's apologetic. **It doesn't touch Paul's theology.** The popular conflation of Luke's resuscitated-corpse with Paul's glorified-spirit is exactly the mistake Tabor wants to expose.

### Three Texts the Apologists Skip

Tabor's heart of the case is three accounts of Easter morning that share a remarkably consistent scenario — and that none of them feature a single sighting of Jesus at the tomb:

1. **Mark 16:1-8 (the original ending)** — Women come, stone already rolled away, a "young man" in a white robe (not an angel) says he has been "lifted up" and is not here. The women flee in terror and "say nothing to anyone." That's it. Mark ends there. The other three endings of Mark are forgeries appended in the 4th-6th centuries.
2. **John 20:1-10** — Mary Magdalene alone, still dark, stone already moved. She concludes "they have taken the Lord" — burial party plural — "and we do not know where they have laid him." Peter and the beloved disciple investigate, see the burial cloths arranged, "and they went back to their own homes" (literally: to their own places — i.e., Galilee). No appearance.
3. **Gospel of Peter** (discovered 1886, Egypt) — Mary and women, tomb open, young man inside, body gone. Then the unique detail: **the final day of unleavened bread (a week after Passover), the twelve disciples were still "weeping and sorrowful" and went home, and Peter said, "I am going fishing."** Fragment breaks off.

If these three texts are independent witnesses to a common early tradition — and Tabor argues they are — then **the original Easter narrative had no appearances at the tomb at all.** Just an empty tomb and grief.

### The Joseph of Arimathea Reburial Reconstruction

Tabor argues the empty tomb has a perfectly mundane explanation embedded right in the gospel texts themselves — most readers just miss it. Mark 15 says Joseph laid Jesus "in a tomb which had been hewn out of the rock" — note: not *his* tomb. John 19 says it bluntly: "Because of the Jewish day of preparation, since the tomb was close at hand, they laid Jesus there." **This was an emergency Passover-eve burial in a nearby unused tomb at Golgotha.** Joseph, as a wealthy Sanhedrin sympathizer, would have had his family tomb on his own property, not at the execution site.

Reconstruction:
- **Friday ~3 PM:** Jesus removed from cross. Passover and Sabbath begin at sunset. No time for proper burial.
- **Friday before sunset:** Joseph of Arimathea wraps the body in a shroud and places it in the nearest available tomb. Temporary.
- **Saturday after sunset (Sabbath ends):** The burial party returns and moves the body to permanent burial — most likely Joseph's family property.
- **Sunday before dawn:** Mary and the women arrive with spices to finish the anointing rites. They find the temporary tomb empty. They conclude "they have taken him" — and they're right. The burial party has.

This is what the texts actually describe if you stop reading them through Luke's later apologetic lens.

### The Visionary Galilee Sightings Came Later

The empty tomb didn't generate resurrection faith. **The Galilee visions did.** The disciples flee home in despair (seven days of weeping in Gospel of Peter). Then Peter, James, and the others begin to have overwhelming visionary experiences of the glorified Christ exalted to heaven. Paul, years later, has his own version of the same kind of vision — and equates his with theirs in 1 Corinthians 15. Acts 7 preserves another sample: Stephen looks up as he's being stoned and sees "the Son of Man standing at the right hand of God." **That is the early-Christian resurrection experience** — a glorified Heavenly Christ, not a corpse walking around Jerusalem.

### The Embellishment Trajectory

Compare the original three texts to what Matthew, Luke, and John (post-verse-10) add:

- **Matthew 28:** Earthquake, descending angel, sealed tomb, fainting Roman guards, Jesus meeting the women on the road. Mountain encounter in Galilee — but "some doubted."
- **Luke 24:** Two men in "dazzling apparel," Emmaus road encounter, Jesus appearing to apostles in Jerusalem the same Sunday, eating broiled fish to prove he's not a ghost, instructions to stay in Jerusalem (contradicting the Galilee tradition).
- **John 20:11+ / John 21:** Mary recognizing Jesus as the gardener, Thomas touching wounds, breakfast on the beach in Galilee.

Tabor's verdict: **"It just doesn't hold up for historical critical studies."** Each successive layer ratchets up the supernatural and the physical. The earliest texts have neither.

### Paul Is Our Earliest Resurrection Witness — and He Saw a Glorified Spirit

1 Corinthians 15 is earlier than any gospel resurrection narrative. Paul lists Peter first, then the Twelve, then 500 brethren, James, the apostles, and "last of all, as to one untimely born, he appeared also to me." He equates his vision with theirs. **What did Paul see?** A glorified Heavenly Christ exalted to God's right hand. A "life-giving spirit." Not a body with lungs. Not a man eating fish. This is the earliest literary witness we have to the resurrection — and it has nothing in common with Luke's corpse-meal.

## Misconceptions Addressed

| Misconception | Tabor's Response |
|---------------|------------------|
| "If the bones of Jesus were found, Christianity would collapse" | Only the *Lukan* corpse-revival framework would collapse. Paul's framework — cosmic transformation into a life-giving spirit body — is untouched by physical remains. Crossan: "Paul would say get a life." |
| "The resurrection accounts in the four gospels are basically consistent" | Mark has no appearances. John 20:1-10 has no appearances. Gospel of Peter has no appearances and seven days of weeping. Matthew, Luke, and post-verse-10 John add progressively elaborate supernatural scenes. The earliest layer and the latest layer are completely different stories. |
| "Joseph of Arimathea buried Jesus in his own family tomb" | Mark says "*a* tomb," not "*his* tomb." John 19 explicitly says it was used because it was "close at hand" due to the day of preparation. This was an emergency burial; the permanent reburial happened after Sabbath ended. |
| "The empty tomb proves the resurrection" | The empty tomb only proves the body was moved. The earliest texts describe exactly that — a burial party reburial. Resurrection faith arose later, in Galilee, through visionary experiences of a glorified Christ. |
| "The longer ending of Mark (16:9-20) is original" | It's a 4th-6th century forgery, along with two other appended endings. Scribes weren't satisfied with the abrupt original conclusion and added the appearances they expected from the later gospels. |
| "Luke's account (broiled fish, touch me) is what early Christians believed" | Luke is one of the *latest* accounts. It is written for second-century apologetic purposes — to defeat skeptics who said "you only had visions." The earliest believers did not picture a corpse eating fish. |
