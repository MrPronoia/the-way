## Summary

This is a free-form, call-in-style Q&A with the Canadian Catholic YouTube channel. The host frames it as "no holds barred" — viewers phone in, the host throws scattered questions, and Tabor answers in a deliberately historian's-hat-on mode rather than a confessional mode. The conversation skips across Matthew 16 (Peter, keys, rock), Jesus's possible marriage to Mary Magdalene, the gates-of-Hades-and-Mount-Hermon reading, the Tribe of Dan question, the authorship of John, the Talpiot tomb, the topology of the empty tomb, the post-crucifixion reburial scenario, ancient Jewish belief in a pre-existent Messiah, the relationship of the Ebionites, Nazarenes, and Mandaeans, and a quick Hebrew-translation question about *tannin* and *nachash* in the Exodus and Genesis serpent passages. It is the broadest single Q&A in the Tabor playlist — useful precisely because the questions are not pre-screened by a Paul-specialist host.

The most substantive thread, and the one that genuinely overlaps Catholic theology, is Matthew 16 — "you are Peter and on this rock I will build my church… I will give you the keys of the kingdom… whatever you bind on earth will be bound in heaven." Tabor's read is historian-careful and quietly dismantling of the Roman Catholic apostolic-succession framework. The Petrine confession scene is in Mark 8 first, without the "you are Peter" benediction. Matthew is rewriting Mark and *inserts* the benediction between Peter's confession and Peter's rebuke ("get behind me, Satan") — which Matthew preserves. Luke removes the rebuke entirely. The Petros/petra (rock) debate that has consumed Catholic-Protestant polemics for centuries Tabor calls "just crazy" — same root, Peter genuinely is given a rock-nickname, but the foundation of the church is Christ, not Peter. The "keys of the kingdom" and "binding and loosing" are rabbinic *halakha* language — every organization needs decision-making, and the parallel passage in Matthew 18 gives the binding-and-loosing authority to "the elders of the church" collectively, not to Peter alone. And the missing person in the Catholic statue program is James the brother of Jesus, who Acts 15, Galatians, and the early sources all show as the actual leader of the Jerusalem community.

The other Catholic-touching threads: Tabor is open to Jesus being married, doesn't see celibacy as the Jewish-rabbinic norm (it isn't), thinks Mary Magdalene is the strongest candidate, and reads the church's later anti-sexuality asceticism as Hellenistic-Augustinian rather than biblical. He treats the Talpiot tomb (the discovered first-century tomb south of Jerusalem with a cluster of names matching Jesus's family) as a serious possibility — and crucially, finding the body of Jesus would not refute resurrection because Paul's resurrection is cosmic transformation, not corpse resuscitation. On Mary Magdalene's "Magdalene" name, Tabor speculates it may be a nickname ("the Tower," parallel to Peter's "the Rock" and James-and-John's "Sons of Thunder") rather than a town-of-origin reference. On John's authorship, Tabor argues James the brother of Jesus is the strongest candidate for the Beloved Disciple. He briefly mentions the "Jesus hideout" in Jordan — the Wadi Cherith / Wadi al-Yabis region where Elijah hid, the most likely place Jesus retreated for safety from Antipas, identifiable only from John's geographic specificity (10:40, the place where John baptized).

Throughout, Tabor stays in historian mode — Catholic, Protestant, and Orthodox theological commitments are politely set aside. He's not picking fights, but his careful historical reads consistently undermine the medieval scaffolding of Catholic doctrine (Petrine primacy, papal infallibility-as-binding-and-loosing, the marriage of Jesus as scandal, the empty tomb as physical-resurrection proof). For a Catholic listener honestly engaging Tabor, the cumulative effect is the same cumulative effect Tabor's whole playlist produces: the medieval synthesis isn't quite what the first-century texts say.

## Key Teachings

### Matthew 16's "You Are Peter" Is Matthew's Editorial Insertion into Mark

The single most theologically loaded passage in the conversation. Tabor's careful sequence:

1. **Mark 8 is the source.** Mark is the earliest gospel. Matthew is rewriting Mark. Mark 8 has the Caesarea Philippi confession scene — Jesus asks "who do you say I am," Peter answers "the Christ" — and then immediately Peter rebukes Jesus's prediction of suffering and Jesus says "get behind me, Satan."
2. **Mark has no "you are Peter" benediction.** It's not there. The famous Catholic-foundational text is Matthew's editorial overlay.
3. **Matthew preserves Mark's rebuke,** but slots the benediction in between the confession and the rebuke: *confession → "you are Peter, on this rock I will build my church" → "get behind me, Satan."* The structure shows Matthew is doing what he does throughout his gospel — overlaying a more positive-Petrine layer on the harsher Markan original.
4. **Luke takes out the rebuke entirely.** Luke is later and consistently softens the failures of the disciples. Three times Mark shows the disciples misunderstanding Jesus's suffering predictions; Luke removes those failure scenes.

The result: the "rock" benediction is a Matthean theological addition reflecting Peter's later leadership role in the Twelve, not a verbatim historical saying of Jesus. Peter *did* recover and *did* become a leader of the Jerusalem community — Matthew is telling that part of the story by inserting this passage. But reading Matthew 16 as standalone foundation for an apostolic-succession papacy ignores the source-critical layering.

### The Petros/Petra Debate Is "Just Crazy"

Tabor's blunt take on a 500-year polemic. Catholics argue *petros* (Peter) and *petra* (the rock) are the same — Peter is the rock, the church is built on him. Protestants argue *petros* (masculine, small stone) is different from *petra* (feminine, bedrock) — Peter is just a pebble, Christ is the bedrock. Tabor:

> "This big Catholic-Protestant debate about the rock is just crazy in my opinion. Yes there's two words for a rock in Greek and it can mean different things, but obviously Peter's not the Messiah, so the foundation of the church is not Peter. Christ is the foundation of the church. But Peter is a rock — 'you are Peter, a rock' — his name is Simon, that's his real name. You don't name your kid Rock. Nobody does that."

The nickname framework matters: Peter = "Rocky," James and John = "Sons of Thunder," Mary Magdalene = possibly "the Tower" (*migdal* in Hebrew). These are nicknames, not theological titles. Peter being given a rock-nickname doesn't establish him as the metaphysical foundation of a 2000-year institutional church.

### Binding and Loosing Is Rabbinic Halakha, Given to "the Elders"

The "keys of the kingdom" and the "binding and loosing on earth bound in heaven" language is rabbinic *halakha* — community decision-making. The Talmud is full of this format: multiple rabbinic views debated, the leading rabbi finally rules, "the *halakha* will be this" (literally "the walk"). Every organization has to make decisions; this is just first-century Jewish organizational language.

The critical move: **Matthew 18:18 repeats the binding-and-loosing language but addresses it to "the elders of the church" collectively, not to Peter alone.** Matthew 16's *soi* ("to you" singular) is to Peter as the leader of the Twelve, but Matthew 18 distributes the same authority across the whole eldership. Catholics make this Peter-specific and papal; Protestants treat it as nothing; Tabor reads it as ordinary first-century Jewish community-leadership language. Whatever Peter is given here, James and John and the elders are also given.

### The Missing Statue at the Vatican Is James the Brother of Jesus

The most pointed Catholic-confronting passage in the conversation. Tabor:

> "When you walk into the main entrance of St. Peter's, you see two statues going up the steps — on the right is Paul holding books and a scroll, on the left is Peter. Half the bridges in Rome have the same two statues. Paul and Peter, Paul and Peter. But the missing thing that I would want to emphasize is James the brother of Jesus, because he actually becomes the leader."

The evidence is direct:
- **Acts 15** — Peter and Paul both speak, then James stands up and renders the decision ("my decision is…") and the letter goes out to the churches in James's name.
- **Galatians** — Paul refers to "the pillars" plural; James, Peter, John. James is named first.
- **First Corinthians 15** — Paul lists the resurrection appearances: Peter first, then the Twelve, then James separately. James receives his own appearance.

Tabor's framing: "Maybe they need three statues at the Vatican, with James in the middle, because he's very important." This is a quiet but pointed challenge to the apostolic-succession framework that runs Catholic ecclesiology through Peter alone.

### Jesus Likely Was Married — and Mary Magdalene Is the Strongest Candidate

Tabor's view has moved over the years. In *The Jesus Dynasty* (2006) he was non-committal. In this 2023 conversation he leans toward Jesus being married, almost certainly to Mary Magdalene.

The argument:
- **Jewish rabbis were married.** Celibacy was not the norm. Sexuality was holy ("be fruitful and multiply").
- **The Augustinian anti-sexuality framework is Hellenistic, not Jewish.** Neoplatonism, Manichaeism, Hellenistic asceticism. Augustine refused blankets, slept on cold stone, ate one piece of bread a day. None of that is in the Hebrew Bible or in Jesus's own reported practice.
- **Jesus is criticized for eating and drinking with sinners** — he attends weddings (Cana), banquets at wealthy tax collectors' homes, social events. Not a partier, but not an ascetic.
- **Mary Magdalene's intimacy with Jesus** is striking. She, his mother, and Salome lead the burial party — an intensely intimate role reserved for family and intimate companions in Jewish burial custom.
- **The Gospel of Philip and other later texts** preserve traditions of Mary as Jesus's special companion, with the male disciples jealous.

Tabor adds: even if Mary Magdalene is an older woman in fraternal companionship rather than a wife, the conversation is being honestly entertained rather than dismissed on "spiritual" grounds. Anti-Mary-Magdalene revulsion is "a very misplaced understanding of Hebrew culture and sexuality."

### Mary Magdalene May Mean "Mary the Tower," Not "Mary from Magdala"

A speculation Tabor flags from a recent article on his blog. *Migdal* in Hebrew means "tower." Mary Magdalene is traditionally read as "Mary from Magdala" (the town of Migdal on the Sea of Galilee). But:

- **Peter = "Rocky"** (nickname, not place)
- **James and John = "Sons of Thunder"** (nickname, not place)
- **Possibly Mary = "the Tower"** — a nickname for spiritual strength and support of Jesus's mission

She could still be from Magdala (Migdal Tower Town) — the two aren't mutually exclusive — but Tabor finds the nickname reading plausible parallel to other Jesus-given nicknames.

### The Gates of Hades and Mount Hermon — Tabor Is Cautious

A caller (William of "Converse Contender") pushed Michael Heiser's *Unseen Realm* reading: Caesarea Philippi sits at the base of Mount Hermon, the mythological place where the Watchers of 1 Enoch descended; "on this rock I will build my church and the gates of Hades will not prevail" reads as Jesus standing at the place-of-the-serpent and declaring the church on the offensive against the underworld forces.

Tabor's response is careful but skeptical:
- **Geographically:** Matthew 16's confession is at Caesarea Philippi, but the Transfiguration ("up to a high mountain") is *a week later* — Mark says about seven days. They aren't on Mount Hermon when the confession happens.
- **Gates of Hades = death.** Tabor's preferred reading: just as Paul says "the last enemy to be destroyed is death" and Revelation 20 says "death and Hades are cast into the lake of fire," gates of Hades in Matthew 16 means death — even death will not prevail against the community Jesus is establishing.
- **Tabor respects Heiser's work** on the *bene Elohim* and sons of God in Deuteronomy and the second-temple period generally — but doesn't think Matthew 16 specifically is about Mount Hermon mythology.

A useful methodological tell: Tabor will *consider* Second Temple apocalyptic context (he wrote his dissertation on Paul's ascent to paradise) but won't import it where the text doesn't demand it.

### The Tribe of Dan, Sea Peoples, and Samson — Out of Tabor's Lane

The same caller asked about a theory connecting the tribe of Dan with the Sea Peoples / Philistines (the Aegean pottery, the line in the Song of Deborah "why did Dan linger so long among the ships," the Samson-Hercules parallels). Tabor's honest answer: not my area, and absent ancient sources from the second-temple period, he doesn't speculate. He flags that Dan is consistently presented as the "fringe" tribe — way north, often syncretistic (the brass-serpent idol incident, possibly), left out of the tribal list in Revelation. Various legendary traditions place them as far as Ireland. Tabor's posture: interesting, possibly true, but without ancient sources he won't run with it.

This is the methodological consistency that makes Tabor worth listening to. He'll cite Josephus on the ten tribes living around the Black Sea (a first-century source), but he won't trust medieval Glastonbury legends about Jesus or Mary Magdalene traveling to the British Isles ("nope, it's always 1200-1300 CE — never a first-century source").

### John's Gospel: James the Brother of Jesus as the Beloved Disciple

A caller mentioned a forthcoming book by Dr. McGregor arguing for John the Elder as the author of John. Tabor briefly disagrees with the standard reading and pushes his own minority view: **James the brother of Jesus is the strongest candidate for the Beloved Disciple.** The arguments:

- Jesus turns his mother over to the Beloved Disciple at the crucifixion (John 19:26-27).
- James the brother of Jesus is the leader of the Jerusalem church after the resurrection.
- Jesus's mother stays in Jerusalem (per early traditions).
- It would be natural for Jesus to entrust his mother to his own brother who lives in Jerusalem.

Tabor hasn't won broad agreement on this, but it fits his consistent emphasis on James as the actual center of gravity of the Jesus movement, hidden by Pauline-Petrine canonical overlay.

### John Knows the Geography — the Jesus Hideout in Jordan

A side discovery Tabor flags from a YouTube lecture he posted that morning. Whoever wrote John knows specific geographic detail no other gospel has:

- **John 10:40** — Jesus "went across the Jordan to the place where John was baptizing." This isn't the southern Jordan near Jericho — it's up north near Bet Shean, at a place called Aenon-Salim (identifiable on the Oxford map).
- **Across from Aenon-Salim** is the *Wadi Cherith* (modern *Wadi al-Yabis*) — the brook where Elijah the Tishbite hid (1 Kings 17).
- **Two kilometers north of the Wadi** is Pella, the Decapolis city where the early Jewish-Christian community later fled after 66 CE.
- **Politically:** this is across the Jordan, outside Herod Antipas's jurisdiction — exactly the place a Jesus on the run from Antipas would hide.

Tabor flew to Jordan to verify this in person decades ago. The Wadi is a protected area with caves and water — exactly the kind of place Elijah-tradition would attach to and the early Jesus movement would later use. John's geographic specificity makes this discoverable; the synoptics don't preserve this detail.

The broader point: **John should not be dismissed as "late theological fluff."** Whoever wrote John knew first-century Judean and Galilean geography intimately — the high priest Annas as father-in-law of Caiaphas, the courtyard layout of the high priest's house (now excavated), the unused tomb near the place of crucifixion. These are not theological elaborations; they are historical anchors.

### The Talpiot Tomb — A Serious Possibility Even With the James Ossuary Caveat

The host gave Tabor space to lay out his Talpiot tomb position. The compressed version:

- **Discovered 1980** in Talpiot, south of Jerusalem (between Bethlehem and Jerusalem, on the Jerusalem side), by a bulldozer and dynamite blast building condominiums.
- **First-century tomb** with a cluster of six inscribed ossuaries.
- **The names:** Jesus son of Joseph, Mariamne (a Greek form sometimes used for Mary Magdalene), Maria, Yose (the specific form "Yose" — not "Yosi" — which is the name of Jesus's brother in Mark 6:3), Matthew, and Judah son of Jesus.
- **The statistical objection:** "the names are common." Tabor's response: yes, individually common, but as a cluster, with this *specific* set, no other tomb of the ~1500 first-century Jerusalem-area tombs matches. The Beatles analogy: George, Paul, John are common British names, but add Ringo and the cluster becomes unique.
- **The James ossuary** — if authentic and if it can be shown to come from the Talpiot tomb (recent forensic work suggests it can), then "James son of Joseph, brother of Jesus" + "Jesus son of Joseph" pushes the probability "way way over 50/50."

Tabor with the James ossuary: well over 50% likely this is Jesus's family tomb. Without it: still a serious possibility. He acknowledges this is a historical probability claim, not a faith claim.

### Finding Jesus's Body Would Not Refute Resurrection

The critical theological point most Christians miss. If the Talpiot tomb is Jesus's family tomb, the body of Jesus was buried there. Doesn't this disprove the resurrection?

Tabor: **No — not on Paul's framework.** Paul says the body is like a tent or clothing that is shed at death (2 Cor 5). At resurrection, the dead are "further clothed" with a new body — Paul cannot describe it ("you will be like Christ" but "what kind of body" he says he cannot tell). Revelation says "the sea gave up the dead that were in it" — you don't recover physical body parts from people who drowned at sea, and yet they are raised. Daniel speaks of those who "sleep in the dust" being raised.

The soul-sleeping-until-the-end-of-the-age framework is the Pauline and Danielic framework. Resurrection is not corpse resuscitation. Paul on the Damascus road did not see a flesh-and-blood Jesus floating in midair; he saw a light, heard a voice, and was blinded. He says the resurrection body is "glorious, immortal, divinized, powerful" but he cannot describe its substance.

So even if Jesus's bones are in the Talpiot tomb, the cosmic resurrection Paul preached is untouched. The popular Christian assumption that "no body = resurrection, body found = no resurrection" is a corpse-resuscitation reading Paul never held. (Cross-reference: this is the framework Tabor laid out in detail in Videos #01 and #09 of this playlist, and in his exchange with Michael Heiser on "why Christians are confused about the earliest Christian view of resurrection.")

### A Pre-Existent Messiah Was a Jewish Belief — But Not the Trinity

Asked whether any pre-Jesus Jewish rabbis taught the divinity of the Messiah or the Trinity:

- **Trinity:** No. The Trinity is formulated at Nicaea (325) and Chalcedon (451). It is not a first-century Jewish belief.
- **Divinity of Messiah:** more complicated. **A pre-existent Messiah was a widespread Jewish belief.** Pseudepigraphic literature contains views where the Messiah pre-exists and is sent by God at the right time. The Book of Enoch contains "Son of Man" imagery that pre-dates Jesus. Some rabbinic circles speak of *Lil-YHWH* ("little YHWH") at God's right hand — a divine son figure. The "Son of Man" coming on the clouds in Daniel 7 is a divine-throne figure.

So the New Testament Son-of-Man imagery and the high Christology of John don't appear ex nihilo — they have Jewish precedents. **What they are not is Trinitarian.** Father-Son-Spirit as one substance in three persons is a later Hellenistic-philosophical formulation.

### Ebionites, Nazarenes, and Mandaeans

The Vesper question: how do these early groups relate to each other and to the Jesus movement?

- **Ebionites and Nazarenes** — two related (possibly identical, possibly distinct) early Jewish-Christian groups who believed the Torah remains valid as interpreted by Jesus. They keep Sabbath and festivals. Gentiles don't need to convert to Judaism but should orient toward Torah-observant life. No circumcision required for Gentiles. **This is the closest preserved expression of the original Jerusalem-James theology** before Paul.
- **Mandaeans** — followers of John the Baptist. Their preserved texts (third or fourth century CE) reject Jesus and see John as the true prophet. But the question is whether the earliest followers of John were also anti-Jesus, or whether the two movements partially merged in the first century and later diverged. We don't have enough sources to know. The Mandaeans show heavy gnostic-dualist development by the time their texts are written, possibly influenced by hundreds of years in Iraq under Zoroastrian-Persian influence.

Tabor's caution: don't import later Mandaean theology back onto first-century John. John in the New Testament is a fairly straightforward Jewish apocalyptic preacher of repentance and the kingdom of God — not yet the gnostic-dualist of later Mandaean texts.

### *Nachash* and *Tannin* — Tabor's Tighter Hebrew Translation

A final viewer question about whether *tannin* in Exodus 7 (Aaron's staff becomes a *tannin*) should be translated "serpent" or "sea-serpent / crocodile." And: is *nachash* (the Eden serpent) really a "snake"?

Tabor's answer reflects his own transparent translation of Genesis:
- **Nachash** does not necessarily mean "snake." It means roughly "the Shining One" — something flashing, shiny, smooth. The root is the same as the word for "brass" — *nechosheth*. Tabor's translation leaves *nachash* as a defined Hebrew term rather than imposing "serpent."
- **Tannin** is some kind of large creature — possibly serpent, possibly crocodile, possibly sea-creature. Hebrew has many animal names that are difficult to identify without comparative work in Akkadian and Ugaritic.
- **Paul's "tempter"** language is probably the safest summary of what the *nachash* does in Eden: the adversarial-prosecutor figure. "Satan" in Hebrew literally means "the opposer." Tabor's image: "I think of [Satan] as a lawyer for the prosecution — no matter how innocent you are, he goes 'well wait a minute, now if we do this and that, wouldn't it…' always bringing up the other side."

The cumulative point: the English Bible's "snake-and-apple" framing of Eden has imposed iconography the Hebrew doesn't require. The Hebrew is more open, less determinate, less mythologically iconographic than centuries of Western Christian art have made it.
