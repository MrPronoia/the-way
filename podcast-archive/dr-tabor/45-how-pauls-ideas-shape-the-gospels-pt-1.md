## Summary

Dr. Tabor opens what he frames as Part 1 of a two-part argument by laying out the methodological move that underwrites his entire reading of Christian origins: **read the New Testament backwards.** The canonical order (Gospels → Acts → Letters → Revelation) tells the story as if Jesus came first, the church came next, and Paul came last. The chronological order reverses everything. Paul's seven undisputed letters (1 Thessalonians, Galatians, 1-2 Corinthians, Romans, Philippians, Philemon) date to the early 50s CE — 20 years after Jesus's death and a full generation before any of the gospels were written. If you put the sources in their actual chronological order, "a wholly different picture emerges with radical and far-reaching implications." Paul is not the late arrival explaining what the Twelve had already worked out — he is the **earliest written witness**, and the gospels that come after him are secondary literature shaped by his theology and written when Paul, Peter, and James were all dead and Jerusalem had been destroyed.

The bulk of the video walks through what this means for the resurrection question and for each of the four gospels. Tabor surveys the standard naturalistic explanations of the empty tomb (the disciples stole the body; a gardener moved it; Jesus swooned; Mark invented the whole burial story) and finds the "Mark invented it" position unconvincing — because **Paul already knows the tradition that Jesus was buried** (1 Corinthians 15). The gospels, by contrast, are anonymous productions written a generation later, none of them eyewitness accounts, all of them theological documents with distinct agendas. Mark comes first (c. 70-80 CE), with no birth narrative and — in the original ending — **no post-resurrection appearances**. Matthew is written a decade or more later, incorporates 90% of Mark, and adds the things Mark conspicuously lacks: a virgin birth and a spectacular resurrection scene with an earthquake, an angel descending from heaven, and the Great Commission to baptize in the Trinitarian formula (a formula Tabor notes is missing from some early Matthew copies). Luke comes later still, expanding the appearances further. John is the latest and most theologically embellished, with the highest Christology and multiple appearance accounts.

Embedded in this chronological reconstruction is a critical sub-thesis: **Matthew and Luke share a sayings source (Q) that Mark did not have — and Q never speaks of Jesus's resurrection.** This is one more clue that the resurrection narrative as Christians know it is a later development, layered onto the tradition in stages. Tabor ends the video by setting up the questions he says he will handle in the next episode: Why was Jesus's tomb found empty? What happened to the body? And how did the earliest followers actually understand the resurrection — given that the young man in Mark's tomb does not say "Jesus has been physically reanimated" but literally "he has been lifted up; you'll see him in Galilee."

## Key Teachings

### Read the New Testament Backwards

The single most important methodological move in Tabor's work. The canonical order is theological, not chronological. If you reverse it and put the documents in the order they were actually written, the earliest layer is **Paul's seven undisputed letters** (early 50s CE), the gospels come a generation later (Mark c. 70-80, Matthew/Luke 80s-90s+, John latest), and the picture of Christian origins changes radically. "Historians read the New Testament backward. Over the last 150 years they have made a significant discovery: if the New Testament writings are ordered chronologically according to the dates the various books were written, a wholly different picture emerges with radical and far-reaching implications."

The practical effect: Paul becomes the **master key**, not a late footnote. The gospels are read through Paul, because Paul is the source closest to the events and the source that shaped the documents that came after him.

### Paul Is the Only Firsthand Witness; the Gospels Are All Anonymous and Secondary

A point Tabor emphasizes that often surprises lay readers: **none of the four gospels is an eyewitness account.** The names Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John are 2nd-century attributions assigned by tradition. The texts themselves are anonymous — none of them begins "I, Matthew" or "I, Mark." (The author of Luke-Acts uses "I" but never identifies himself; the "we" sections in Acts may be an incorporated source rather than the author's own travelogue.) "Scholars are agreed that none of the gospels is an eyewitness account, and the names associated with them are assigned by tradition."

By contrast, Paul's seven undisputed letters are **firsthand testimony from someone who had direct dealings with Peter, James, and the other apostles.** They are also written before Jerusalem fell in 70 CE and before the founding generation died. So the chronology cuts cleanly: Paul = primary source; gospels = secondary literature composed after the Jerusalem church was scattered.

### Mark Has No Post-Resurrection Appearances — and That's the Original Ending

The most striking historical fact about the earliest gospel: **Mark's original ending (16:8) contains no appearances of the risen Jesus.** No earthquake, no angel descending from heaven, no Jesus showing his wounds, no Great Commission. Just an empty tomb, a young man (not an angel) inside it, and women fleeing in fear, telling no one. Tabor: "Most strikingly, in his original version, there are no post-resurrection appearances of Jesus to the disciples. And I want an exclamation mark there — I mean, that's amazing. This fact alone provides us with an important key to unraveling the mystery surrounding the empty tomb."

The implication: for several decades, when Mark was the only gospel in circulation, **Christians were telling the Jesus story without the two elements that later became foundational for the faith** — the virgin birth and the Easter morning appearances. What's more fundamental to modern Christianity than those two? And yet the earliest gospel has neither.

### Matthew Embellishes Mark — And Invents the Things Mark Lacks

Matthew is written at least a decade after Mark and uses Mark as his main source, incorporating roughly 90% of Mark's material. But Matthew "finds Mark's beginning and ending wholly unsatisfactory." So he supplies them. **Matthew composes the first miraculous virgin birth narrative.** And Matthew creates the spectacular resurrection scene — the great earthquake, the angel descending from heaven and rolling back the stone, the guards trembling like dead men, and the climactic mountaintop Great Commission with its Trinitarian baptismal formula (Matthew 28:18-20).

Tabor underscores that **early copies of Matthew lacked even the Trinitarian commission** — "you can tell liturgically to baptize in the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit is definitely something that developed later." So the most quoted line in Christian missionary history is itself an embellishment on an embellishment of an empty-tomb story that Mark told without any appearances at all.

### Luke and John Embellish Further — Multiple Appearances, Higher Christology

Luke (written end of 1st / beginning of 2nd century, in Tabor's read) expands the appearances even further than Matthew: multiple appearances to various individuals and to all the apostles, plus a virgin birth account quite different from Matthew's. John, the latest of the four, has the most theologically developed Christology (Jesus as pre-existent divine Logos in the opening verses) and the most extensive appearance traditions — appearances in Jerusalem and an appended chapter (John 21) of appearances on the Sea of Galilee.

The pattern is unmistakable: **the later the gospel, the more elaborate the appearance tradition and the higher the Christology.** This is what you expect if the resurrection story is developing across the decades, not what you expect if it began as a fixed eyewitness report on Easter morning.

### The Q Source Knows Nothing of the Resurrection

A deceptively quiet but devastating observation. Beyond their shared use of Mark, Matthew and Luke have access to a second source — the **Q source** — a sayings collection probably compiled around 50 CE. Q is reconstructed by extracting the material Matthew and Luke share that is *not* in Mark.

The bombshell: "this two-source material shared by Matthew and Luke that's not in Mark **never speaks of Jesus's resurrection** — whereas in Mark, who comes later, Jesus refers several times to being raised on the third day." So in our earliest sayings layer — the closest written approximation to what Jesus actually taught — there is no resurrection doctrine. The resurrection enters the tradition with Mark (and Paul before him), not with the earliest sayings tradition. This dovetails with Tabor's broader thesis (see #04, #30) that substitutionary atonement and the death-burial-resurrection framework start with Paul, not with the Jerusalem movement.

### The Naturalistic Theories Are Weak — But So Is the Bodily-Resurrection Apologetic

Tabor surveys the standard non-supernatural explanations and finds them lacking:

- **Stolen body theory** — mentioned in Matthew 28:13-15 as a rumor among the Jewish population.
- **Gardener moved the body** — appears in Tertullian (late 3rd century) and later Jewish texts.
- **Swoon theory** — popularized by Hugh Schonfield's 1965 bestseller *The Passover Plot*; still circulates (Ahmadiyya Islam holds a version with Jesus surviving and dying in Kashmir).
- **Mark invented the empty tomb** — the most common scholarly position. Tabor rejects this because **Paul already knows the burial tradition** (1 Cor 15) decades before Mark wrote.

Géza Vermes, in his late book *The Resurrection: History and Myth*, surveyed all of these and concluded "none of them stands up to stringent scrutiny." Tabor: historical investigation, given the contradictory and mythological nature of the New Testament evidence, has reached a dead end on its own terms. **But there is a way through**, and the way through is to put the sources in chronological order, give priority to Paul's firsthand testimony, and let the gospels be analyzed as the secondary, theologically motivated literature they actually are.

### The Gospels Are Theology, Not History — and One of Them Could Have Been Written by a Woman

A small but pointed Tabor aside: "none of them is writing history, but all four can rightly be called theologians from a distance." Each gospel writer "had his own motives and purposes in telling the Jesus story in a way that supported his — and I might even say her — particular perspectives, because one of the gospels could have been written by a woman conceivably."

The point isn't a gender claim — it's that the authors are unknown enough that we cannot assume anything about them beyond what their theological agendas reveal. The names are 2nd-century traditions. The texts themselves are anonymous. The differences between them, when carefully examined, reveal "a process of myth-making that went on within decades of Jesus's death."

| "If we read the New Testament in canonical order, we're reading it in historical order" | The canonical order is theological, not chronological. To do history, you have to read it backwards: Paul first, then Mark, then Matthew/Luke, then John. |
