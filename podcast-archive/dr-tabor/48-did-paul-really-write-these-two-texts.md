## Summary

Dr. Tabor opens a methodological mini-series on how scholars actually identify **interpolations** in ancient texts — not little phrase-level variants (that's textual criticism), but **chunks of material** dropped into a text by a later editor that reshape what the original author was saying. This sits alongside his "Lost in Translation" series and previews two more techniques he'll cover later: spotting **embedded/intertextual material** (where an author weaves in other sources) and **derivative texts** (Daniel rewritten as Revelation, the Enochic tradition, etc.). The promised payoff: how do you know when a chunk of biblical text wasn't written by the named author?

The interesting move in this video is that Tabor takes **two famous interpolation candidates** — passages that many heavyweight Pauline scholars argue are post-Pauline insertions — and **argues against the interpolation reading in both cases**. The two texts are: **1 Thessalonians 2:14-16** (the harsh "God's wrath is come upon them at last" passage about the Jews who killed Jesus and oppose Paul's Gentile mission), and **1 Corinthians 14:34-35** (the "women should keep silent in the churches" passage). Both look anti-Pauline at first glance — vindictive toward Jews in one case, contradicting the egalitarian-sounding Galatians 3:28 in the other. And both have respectable scholarly arguments for being later insertions.

But Tabor's read is more nuanced. He thinks **Paul wrote both passages** — they fit his historical situation (he was being chased by Jewish opponents), his apocalyptic timeframe (the world is passing away in 5-10 years, so don't worry about overthrowing social structures), and his pattern of using Torah for cultural ordering even while telling Gentiles they're not under Torah. The lesson: interpolation identification depends heavily on your **presuppositions** about what Paul "would have" said. Tabor uses these two cases to **muddy the water on purpose** — to show that not every uncomfortable-sounding Pauline passage is an interpolation. He'll give clearer interpolation examples in later videos.

## Key Teachings

### What Counts as an Interpolation (vs. Textual Variant or Forgery)

Tabor distinguishes four phenomena that get sloppily conflated:

- **Textual criticism** — phrase-level differences between manuscripts. Two copies of Mark from different periods read slightly differently; you compare and judge which is primary. *Not* what this video is about.
- **Interpolations** — **chunks of material** inserted by a later editor. A heavy-handed second hand wedges something significant into the original. This is what the video targets.
- **Embedded/intertextual material** — the original author themselves weaves in other sources, which then begin to relate to one another like scrambled eggs. (Future video topic.)
- **Derivative texts** — entire books rewriting an earlier book (Daniel → Revelation → 2 Esdras as Apocrypha; the Enochic tradition layering text on text). (Future video topic.)
- **Forgeries** — whole texts falsely attributed (the "Pontius Pilate letters to Tiberius," "suppressed Roman official's description of Jesus" garbage that keeps surfacing on social media). Tabor notes there are **more forgeries than genuine attributable texts** from antiquity. (Future video topic.)

The interpolation question is whether a *chunk* — not just a phrase — was wedged into an otherwise authentic letter to push it in a direction the original author didn't go.

### Text #1 — 1 Thessalonians 2:14-16 (the "Wrath on the Jews" Passage)

Paul to the Thessalonians, around 50-51 CE. They've been suffering from their fellow Greek Macedonians **just as** the Judean churches suffered from their Jewish countrymen — Paul then escalates: "the Jews, who killed both the Lord Jesus and the prophets and drove us out and displease God and oppose all men by hindering us from speaking to the Gentiles that they may be saved, so as always to fill up the measure of their sins. But God's wrath is come upon them at last!"

**Why scholars call it an interpolation:**
- It's vindictive and singles out "the Jews" as the other/alien opposing the new movement
- It contradicts Paul's tone elsewhere (Romans 9-11 — "I could wish myself accursed for my brethren")
- The phrasing "the Jews" sounds more like late-first-century Christianity once it had separated from Judaism
- "God's wrath is come upon them at last" reads to some scholars as a *retrospective* gloss after 70 CE (Temple destruction)

**Tabor's pushback — he thinks Paul wrote it:**
- The context before and after reads **seamlessly**. Interpolators usually find a little space to wedge into; here there's no obvious seam.
- This is not modern anti-Semitism — it's **intra-Jewish polemic**. Paul is Jewish, the movement is Jewish, and his opponents are also Jewish (likely Pharisees controlling synagogues). The Dead Sea Scrolls say **much worse** things about the Jerusalem Temple establishment, and no one calls *them* anti-Semitic.
- It matches Acts 17, where Paul's Jewish enemies in Thessalonica drove him out. The wound is fresh.
- Morton Smith's frame fits: "parties and politics in the late second temple period." Multiple Jewish groups competing for the same Gentile God-fearers attending synagogues. Paul is pulling those Gentile attenders off into his movement, and the synagogue authorities fight back.

So the passage *could* be used anti-Semitically later (and was), but Tabor argues that's not what it originally was.

### Text #2 — 1 Corinthians 14:34-35 (Women Silent in the Churches)

"The women should keep silent in the churches. For they are not permitted to speak, but should be subordinate, as even the Torah says. If there is anything they desire to know, let them ask their husbands at home. For it is shameful for a woman to speak in church... Was it from you that the word of God came? Or are you the only ones it has reached?"

**Why many scholars call it an interpolation:**
- It contradicts **Galatians 3:28** ("neither male nor female") in the same author
- It contradicts **1 Corinthians 11**, where women are explicitly *allowed* to prophesy and pray in the assembly (just with covered heads)
- The "subservient women" theology is a hallmark of the **deutero-Pauline letters** (1 Timothy, Titus, Ephesians) that most scholars don't think Paul wrote — so finding it in 1 Corinthians looks like a later editor retrojecting the deutero-Pauline view into the authentic early letter
- Some manuscripts move the verses around, which is sometimes a textual fingerprint of insertion

**Tabor's pushback — he thinks Paul wrote it too:**
- It fits Paul's **apocalyptic timeline**. The world is passing away (1 Cor 7); "the form of this world is passing away." Whether women speak in assemblies is small change relative to the imminent transformation.
- Paul wants Jewish synagogue culture to look on his Gentile movement favorably — so he reinforces the synagogue-style gender order (women silent, head coverings, etc.) even while telling Gentiles they're not under Torah generally.
- It matches 1 Corinthians 11 if you read 11 as "women may prophesy/pray with heads covered" and 14 as "women shouldn't *interrupt* with questions during the service." Different activities, different rules — both Pauline.
- Don't get married, don't seek to be freed from slavery, don't bother with social reform — Paul keeps telling people the current age is winding up. Telling women to stay silent in the meeting fits the same pattern.

### Both Cases Depend on Your Presuppositions

Tabor's honest point: **interpolation identification is rarely clean**. Two big-name scholars can look at the same chunk and reach opposite conclusions. The question is usually framed as "would Paul have said this?" — and the answer depends on which Paul you've decided is the real one. The hyper-egalitarian universalist Paul of Galatians 3:28? Or the apocalyptic Jewish-cultural-conformist Paul who tells slaves to stay slaves and the unmarried not to marry?

Tabor's framework — Paul as **apocalyptic eschatologist** with "already, not yet" mechanics — lets both texts stay in. The new age has dawned in Christ but the old order persists until the parousia, so you respect existing cultural structures *for now* even though they're "passing away."

### What the "Already, Not Yet" Frame Does to These Texts

This is the load-bearing concept. In Tabor's *Paul and Jesus*, the chapter "Already Not Yet" lays it out: Paul's followers are getting a foretaste of the age to come (new bodies, sons of God, transformed cosmos) but they're not there yet. So in the meantime:

- Don't try to reverse circumcision; don't get newly circumcised
- Slaves stay slaves
- Wives stay subordinate to husbands; women stay silent in assemblies
- Jewish opponents who block your mission are filling up the measure of their sins — and God's wrath is imminent
- The world's schema is passing away

If Paul thinks the parousia is **5-10 years out** (set against the Caligula statue-in-Temple attempt of 41 CE and the rising tensions with Rome), then social conservatism in the present age isn't a contradiction of his eschatological egalitarianism — it's a *function* of it. The new world is coming so fast that overhauling the old one would be a waste of effort.

### How to Spot a Real Interpolation (Tabor's Hints)

Tabor doesn't give a full method here (that's for a later video), but the clues he flags by implication:
- **Seams in the text** — interpolators wedge into gaps; if the context before and after flows naturally, the case is weaker
- **Vocabulary mismatch** with the surrounding author's voice
- **Contradiction with the author's clear positions elsewhere** — though as he shows, this can be argued either way
- **Convenient theological alignment** with later concerns rather than the original author's situation
- **Manuscript displacement** — when a chunk floats around to different positions in different manuscripts (this often signals it wasn't original)

The 1 Corinthians 14 women-silent passage actually does float in some manuscripts, which Tabor doesn't emphasize but is part of why the interpolation case for that one is widely considered stronger than for 1 Thessalonians 2.
