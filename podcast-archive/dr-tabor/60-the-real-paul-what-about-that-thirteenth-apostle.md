## Summary

This Q&A segment with Jeremiah Giehl tackles the foundational tension at the heart of the New Testament: Paul's claim to apostolic authority despite never having walked with Jesus. Tabor opens by laying out his mainstream-with-caveats position on Paul's authenticity — yes, Paul was a historical figure who wrote in the 50s CE; yes, the seven undisputed letters are his (Galatians, 1 Thessalonians, 1-2 Corinthians, Romans, Philippians, Philemon); and yes, his theology was thoroughly apocalyptic. He expected Jesus to return in a transformation so imminent that he told his followers not to start businesses, not to buy life insurance, not even to get married — because "the form of this world is passing away."

The bulk of the conversation pivots to the structural problem the New Testament tries to paper over. Acts 1 records the apostles selecting Matthias to replace Judas — explicitly setting the criterion that an apostle must have been with the movement "from the baptism of John." This is **the twelfth apostle**, ratified by the eleven and by Mary and the brothers of Jesus who were present. Paul shows up *after* this completed body of Twelve and claims a thirteenth slot — without meeting any of the Acts 1:22 criteria — based on a private vision and ongoing "communication" with the risen Jesus. Tabor's most striking move here is to show how the New Testament itself reveals that the actual structure of leadership ran through **James, the brother of Jesus** — second-born son of Mary, head of household after Jesus's death, presiding figure at the Jerusalem council in Acts 15, the unnamed Beloved Disciple in John, and the brother Jesus entrusts with his mother from the cross.

The conclusion: when you visit the Vatican today, Peter stands on the left and Paul stands on the right with the scrolls. James — the actual successor — has been erased. Tabor reads the New Testament as the artifact of that erasure: Acts works to present Paul as the natural extension of the Jesus movement, the Gospels are theologically Pauline in their final forms, even 1 Peter reads like Paul (with the Pauline interpolations crossed out, what remains "reads perfectly" as something much closer to James). The "13th apostle" framing is Tabor's polite way of naming the takeover. James was in charge. Paul was not.

## Key Teachings

### Why Paul Was Historical — and Why the Seven Letters Are Authentic

Tabor distances himself from the maximalist mythicism circulating in some corners ("Jesus didn't exist, Paul didn't exist, all the letters are forged"). His grounds are partly scholarly (computer studies, AI-assisted stylometry, vocabulary patterns all confirm the seven-letter Pauline core) and partly experiential. He wrote his University of Chicago dissertation on Paul, has read the letters in Greek "I don't know how many times — hundreds," and reports the literary fingerprint of a single personality: "You begin to feel a kind of a character. It's not just formal rhetoric."

The seven undisputed letters are: **Galatians, 1 Thessalonians, 1-2 Corinthians, Romans, Philippians, Philemon.** They share an unmistakable apocalyptic urgency — they're not trying to organize a long-term church, they're not worried about "the delay of the coming of the Lord," they read like a man who thinks Jesus is coming back next summer.

The other six (Colossians, Ephesians, and the Pastorals) are progressively further from Paul. Tabor flags Colossians and Ephesians especially for their cosmic Christology — "suddenly he's the creator of the universe. Paul never said that." Those later Pauline-school letters introduce the pre-existent, eternally-divine Christ. The original Paul stops short of that; he sees Jesus as the **firstborn of all creation**, glorified after the resurrection, but not eternal in the later Nicene sense.

### The Acts 1:22 Apostolic Criterion — and What Paul Didn't Meet

Tabor's central historical claim in this video. Acts 1:21-22 records the apostles defining the qualifications for replacing Judas:

> "One of these men who have accompanied us during all the time that the Lord Jesus went in and out among us, beginning from the baptism of John until the day when he was taken up from us — one of these men must become with us a witness to his resurrection."

Two candidates were proposed: Joseph called Barsabbas (Justus) and Matthias. **Matthias was chosen by lot.** He is the twelfth apostle. The Twelve are complete, with full Acts 1 ratification, *before* Paul ever shows up.

Paul fails the criterion on every front. He was not with the movement from John's baptism. He did not accompany Jesus during the public ministry. He was not a "witness to his resurrection" in the bodily-appearance sense the Twelve mean — his witness is a private vision on the road to Damascus, years later. Paul's own term for himself in 1 Corinthians 15:8 is striking: **"as to one untimely born"** (Greek *ektroma*, literally "miscarriage" or "abortion"). He knows he doesn't fit. His response is to claim a *higher* authority — direct revelation from the risen Jesus — that supersedes the criterion the Twelve themselves established.

This is what Tabor (and the podcast title) means by the "thirteenth apostle." Paul is operating outside the structure the original community set up. He is not a replacement, not an addition voted on by the Eleven — he's a self-appointed parallel authority who claims to have received his gospel "not from any man, neither was I taught it, but by the revelation of Jesus Christ" (Galatians 1:12).

### James the Brother of Jesus Was the Actual Leader

The piece the Vatican statues won't tell you. Tabor walks through the evidence layer by layer:

**Mark 6:3** — Jesus has four named brothers (James, Joses, Judas, Simon) and unnamed sisters. The historical Jesus had siblings. The "perpetual virginity of Mary" doctrine is a later theological imposition.

**Mary's family structure** — Jesus is firstborn. James is second-born. When Joseph disappears from the narrative (no mention after Luke 2 — Tabor suggests an older husband who died young), **Jesus is head of household.** When Jesus is crucified, that role passes to the next eldest son: James.

**The Beloved Disciple is James, not John** — Tabor's most provocative move in this segment. The Gospel of John keeps the Beloved Disciple unnamed precisely because the writer doesn't want to exalt James. But at the cross, Jesus says "Son, behold your mother. Mother, behold your son" — and "from that day that disciple took her into his own house." This is the ritual transfer of household responsibility from the dying eldest son to the next eldest. The idea that the Apostle John (son of Zebedee) took custody of Mary is a late Christian tradition that, as Tabor points out, makes no sense: "James is in charge of the movement when Jesus dies — and he's not in charge of his own mother? That just doesn't make sense."

**Acts 1:14** — In the upper room with the Eleven, "Mary the mother of Jesus, and his brothers" are present. The matriarch and the brothers are there at the selection of Matthias. They're not random observers — they're the family of the founder, integral to the leadership.

**Acts 15** — When the biggest dispute in early Christianity breaks out (must Gentiles convert to Judaism?), and after Paul and Peter have each had their say, **James stands up and renders the decision.** No introduction, no explanation of his authority. "My decision is..." (Acts 15:19). The text assumes everyone knows who's in charge.

**Galatians 2** — Paul's first-person account corroborates Acts. He calls James, Peter, and John the "so-called pillars" (Tabor reads this with a sarcastic edge). When Paul lists them, **James is named first** in some passages — outranking even Peter.

**Succession** — When James is martyred (~62 CE per Josephus), leadership passes to **Simon, another brother of Jesus.** This is what Tabor calls the **Jesus Dynasty** — a caliphate structure, family-centered, passing from eldest to next eldest. The Ebionites and the Clementine literature preserve this memory: "Don't believe anything you hear unless you've checked with James."

### Paul's "Untimely Born" Self-Description

Tabor doesn't dwell on this in the segment as long as one would expect, but the point is implicit throughout. In 1 Corinthians 15:8, after listing the resurrection appearances to Peter, the Twelve, the 500, James, and "all the apostles," Paul writes: "Last of all, as to one untimely born, he appeared also to me."

The Greek *ektroma* is harsh. It means a stillbirth, an abortion, a miscarriage — a birth out of proper sequence and outside the natural process. Paul is admitting that he doesn't fit the pattern. He is not "born" into the apostolate the way the others were (through walking with Jesus). His apostolic identity is something irregular, something tacked on at the end, something Paul himself describes with a word of self-deprecation that borders on apology.

But — and this is the move — Paul immediately follows this with: "But by the grace of God I am what I am... I worked harder than any of them." The "untimely born" gives way to "more than any of them." This is the rhetorical structure of his whole apostolic claim: I don't meet the criterion the Twelve set, but I have a higher claim that overrides the criterion.

### The Gospels Were Edited to Be Pauline

A piece Tabor returns to throughout the Paul playlist. The synoptic gospels were written *after* Paul's letters had been circulating for decades. By the time Mark, Matthew, Luke, and John reached their final forms, Paul's theology was the dominant framework in the Gentile mission. The Gospels reflect that.

"The theology of Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John is very Pauline on one level." Not in every detail — Luke's Paul, for instance, "is not really the original Paul," as scholars have long noted. But the overall theological structure (Jesus as pre-existent Son who came down from heaven, was born of a virgin, died for the sins of humankind, returned to heaven, and is coming again to gather his believers) is **Paul's gospel skeleton, retroactively woven into the Jesus story.**

Romans 1-8 is the manifesto. That is the Pauline message — and that message, "in its main elements," is what gets propagated as Christianity.

### 1 Peter Reads Like Paul — Cross Out the Pauline Interpolations and You Get Something Closer to James

A small but devastating exegetical observation. Tabor reports an experiment he's done: he went through 1 Peter and marked the passages that read distinctly Pauline. Then he crossed them out and read what was left.

"It reads perfectly without them. So I think that's been Pauline interpolated. A lot of scholars think that. It's called 1 Peter. So it's probably an original. It probably would have been originally much like James."

The implication: even the letters attributed to the *original* apostles have been edited to sound Pauline in their canonical forms. The original Petrine and Jacobian voices — Torah-observant Jewish messianism — have been overlaid with the Pauline frame.

The Epistle of James, by contrast, has survived **largely uninterpolated.** Tabor: "James, we do have, and it is not interpolated, except maybe one verse in chapter 2." That verse ("Don't hold the faith of our Lord Jesus in vain") fits James's larger argument — that real faith is shown by not letting the rich sit up front while the poor sit in the back of the synagogue (and Tabor flags: *synagogue*, not church — that's the word James uses).

### The Vatican Steps as a Symbol of the Erasure

The image Tabor returns to as the heart of the matter. Walk up the steps of St. Peter's. There's Peter on the left, the rock of the church. There's Paul on the right, holding scrolls — he "wrote most of the New Testament." Statues of Jesus and Mary at the top.

"And you go on up and I stand at the top and I look back and I think — where's James?"

That's the question. The biological brother of Jesus, the actual successor of the movement, the man whose decision settled the biggest dispute in the first decades of Christianity — has been written out. James who? his students ask. He's been so thoroughly removed that even Catholic students at Notre Dame, when Tabor announces he'll spend six weeks on "James the brother of Jesus," respond: "But I thought Mary was a perpetual virgin?"

The historical reality and the inherited story diverge sharply at this point. Tabor's books — *The Jesus Dynasty*, *Paul and Jesus*, and the forthcoming *Rediscovering Mary* — are the work of restoring James, Mary, and the family movement to the center of the original story.

### Where Tabor Is Going Next — The Trilogy Becomes a Library

Tabor closes by mapping his publishing trajectory. He's writing the books he thinks the field needs and not duplicating books that already exist:

- **John the Baptist** — James McGrath has written "two wonderful books" Tabor endorses. He won't duplicate that.
- **Jesus** — Tabor's *The Jesus Dynasty* covers this.
- **Mary (mother of Jesus)** — *Rediscovering Mary*, releasing September 30, 2025.
- **Mary Magdalene** — already well-covered by other scholars; Tabor won't write a separate book.
- **James the brother of Jesus** — John Painter's *Just James* is the definitive treatment; Tabor recommends it instead of writing his own.
- **Paul** — *Paul and Jesus* is the book.

Tabor's next planned book is *Jesus Betrayed* — a short ~150-page synthesis of how Jesus's original followers ultimately rejected, lost, or forsook his original message. That book is the capstone of the project this podcast playlist documents.
