## Summary

This is the third installment of Tabor's "Seven Fateful New Testament Texts" series, focused on **1 Corinthians 14:33-36** ("women should keep silent in the churches... should be subordinate, as even the Torah says... it is shameful for a woman to speak in church"), the parallel head-covering material in **1 Corinthians 11**, and the deutero-Pauline elaboration in **1 Timothy 2:11-15**. As with the previous videos in the series, Tabor is not asking what these passages may have meant *in context*. He is asking how they have been **pulled out, applied universally, and used over two thousand years to hold women back, oppress them, restrict them**, and reinforce a hierarchy that runs God → Christ → male → female. His verdict in the description is unsparing: outside of senseless wars, the suffering and loss is only comparable to slavery and child abuse.

Tabor walks through the 1 Cor 14 passage as authentic Paul. He acknowledges many serious scholars argue these verses are an interpolation (it parallels the contested 1 Thess 2 "wrath on the Jews" passage in feel, and contradicts Paul's own 1 Cor 11 where women clearly pray and prophesy in the assembly). But Tabor's read — consistent with his earlier video #48 — is that the Greek manuscript evidence does not support the interpolation case, and the vocabulary and argumentation fit Paul. Paul wrote it. Then Tabor walks back to 1 Corinthians 11 and does something many readers miss: he shows the famous "veil" passage is not about a veil at all. The Greek **never uses the noun for veil**. Paul is talking about **hair length and hairstyle** — long hair worn up rather than draped erotically over neck and shoulders in the Roman-Pompeian style. The translators imposed the veil. Paul wrote about hair as the natural covering.

The video then traces the trajectory forward into 1 Timothy 2 — which Tabor treats as deutero-Pauline, written after Paul's death — where Paul's situational ruling is hardened into doctrine: "I permit no woman to teach or have authority over men." Eve gets blamed for the fall. Women are saved through childbearing. Tabor connects all of this to Paul's apocalyptic frame from earlier videos: Paul thought Jesus was coming back within five to ten years, and in that interim he wanted **order** — slaves obey masters, women keep silent, Jews and Gentiles respect one another, everyone hold their position. None of it was meant as a permanent social architecture. Paul was running an emergency holding pattern for a movement he thought was about to be transformed. But the end didn't come. The holding pattern became the doctrine. The Churches of Christ in which Tabor was raised once fought over whether women could pass out the communion plate. The Catholic Church still debates ordination. Conservative congregations still split. Tabor closes by speculating that if Paul were brought back to the 21st century and shown what his few hundred words have done across two millennia, he would be "appalled" — and would say: "wait, wait, wait, this was for the time that fit. We have to have a new view of things."

## Key Teachings

### The Fateful Passage — 1 Corinthians 14:33-36

The text reads: *"As in all the churches of the saints, the women should keep silence in the churches. For they are not permitted to speak, but should be subordinate, as even the Torah says. If there is anything they desire to know, let them ask their husbands at home. For it is shameful for a woman to speak in church."* Tabor unpacks each element:

- **"As in all the churches of the saints"** — Paul is making a universal rule. *Churches* just means assemblies; *saints* just means the group's members, not the later Catholic technical term.
- **"Women should keep silence"** — when the congregation gathers, women do not speak.
- **"Subordinate, as even the Torah says"** — not Roman law. Paul is appealing to **Genesis 3:16** where Yahweh tells Eve, "your desire will be toward your man and he will rule over you." A creation-order hierarchy reaching back to Eden.
- **"Let them ask their husbands at home"** — Tabor flags how incomplete this is. Women in the early movement were in all sorts of states — single, widowed, enslaved, fatherless. The "ask your husband" instruction has no clean application for any of them.
- **"What, did the word of God originate with you?"** — Paul is responding to a report. The Corinthian women were apparently speaking up. He cracks down hard. The whole chapter is about "decently and in order" — Paul wants control.

Then verse 36 — the kicker Tabor highlights: *"If anyone thinks he is a prophet or spiritual, he should acknowledge that what I am writing to you is a command of the Lord. If anyone does not recognize this, he is not recognized."* Paul invokes apostolic authority. Disagreement = exclusion.

### Is This an Interpolation? Tabor Says No — But Many Scholars Say Yes

Tabor acknowledges the scholarly debate openly. Many major scholars treat 1 Cor 14:34-35 as a post-Pauline insertion, often grouping it with the 1 Thessalonians 2:14-16 "wrath on the Jews" passage as "nasty Paul" material someone else slipped in. The arguments for interpolation are strong:

- It contradicts **1 Corinthians 11**, where women plainly *do* pray and prophesy in the assembly (with proper hair)
- It contradicts **Galatians 3:28** — "neither male nor female"
- The "subservient women" frame is the hallmark of the **deutero-Pauline** Pastorals (1 Timothy, Titus) most scholars don't think Paul wrote
- In some Western-text manuscripts, verses 34-35 float to a different location (after v. 40) — a textual fingerprint sometimes associated with insertions

Tabor's pushback: the Greek manuscripts overall don't support removal, the vocabulary and argumentation fit Paul, and Paul's apocalyptic frame accommodates the apparent contradictions. The contested 1 Cor 11 passage (head/hair) and the contested 1 Cor 14 passage (silence) **share the same hierarchy logic** — God, Christ, male, female. If 11 is authentic Paul (and almost everyone grants it is), the underlying theology is already there for 14 to be Paul too.

### The "Veil" Passage Is Not About a Veil — 1 Corinthians 11

This is the move many readers miss. Tabor reads 1 Corinthians 11 carefully and shows the Greek **never contains the noun for veil**. The RSV and many translations insert "veil" — but Paul is talking about **hair length and hair arrangement**, and the hair *is* the covering. Read with veil removed:

- "Any man who prays or prophesies with his head covered dishonors his head" — meaning a man with **long hair**, not a man in a head wrap (men don't wear veils)
- "If a woman will not cover herself" — meaning, will not wear her hair up as a covering — "let her be shorn"
- "But if a woman has long hair, it is her pride; for her hair is given to her as a covering" — Paul says it explicitly

The cultural backdrop: a Roman lady of the 1st century, like the women painted on the walls of Pompeii, with hair up and braided, exposing the neck and shoulders. That, Tabor argues, is what Paul does not want to see in the assembly — hair worn down, neck and shoulders bared, in a style the period read as **erotic**. The "veil" interpretation, foisted on the text by later translators, has caused 2,000 years of unnecessary scarves, mantillas, and covered heads in worship — a tradition Paul did not actually prescribe.

### The Hierarchy — God, Christ, Male, Female (and "Because of the Angels")

In 1 Cor 11, Paul lays out the chain: *"The head of every man is Christ, and the head of a woman is her husband, and the head of Christ is God."* Picture a flowchart: Creator → Christ → males → women under their husbands. Tabor notes you could extend it downward — children under women, perhaps pets under children. The hierarchy is the architecture.

Then comes one of the strangest phrases in Paul: *"That is why a woman ought to cover her head, because of the angels."* Tabor offers two readings:

1. The angelic hierarchy itself — certain angels rebelled and "got out of their place" in Hebrew mythology (the Watchers tradition). A woman speaking out of place in the assembly mirrors that rebellion.
2. **Genesis 6** — "the sons of God saw the daughters of men that they were fair" and came down to take them as wives. Angels lusted after attractive human women. In Paul's frame, a woman with erotic hair in the assembly risks attracting that kind of attention from spiritual beings.

Either way, the underlying logic is the same as in many traditional religious cultures (Orthodox Judaism, Islam, conservative Christianity): in sacred space, men and women should be separated and women's bodies should not be sexually present. Different cultures, different definitions of modesty — in Tabor's grandmother's day, even an exposed ankle was scandal. Paul is reflecting a culturally Jewish-Hellenistic standard of his own time.

### The Deutero-Pauline Hardening — 1 Timothy 2:11-15

What was a situational ruling in Paul's authentic letters becomes doctrine in the post-Pauline Pastorals:

> *"Let a woman learn in silence with all submissiveness. I permit no woman to teach or have authority over men; she is to keep silent. For Adam was formed first, then Eve; and Adam was not deceived, but the woman was deceived and became a transgressor. Yet woman will be saved through bearing children."* (1 Tim 2:11-15)

Tabor notes the escalation:

- Paul in 1 Cor 14 banned speaking in the assembly. 1 Timothy bans **teaching and authority** entirely — any leadership role.
- Paul appealed to creation hierarchy (man first, then woman). 1 Timothy escalates to **fault and blame** — Adam wasn't deceived, the woman was. Women are responsible for the fall.
- Paul talked about women's anxieties. 1 Timothy assigns women a salvific role through **childbearing** — your role is to bear children, the man's role is to till the earth, both echoing Genesis 3.

This is the text most scholars and Tabor treat as written by a Pauline disciple decades after Paul's death, projecting Paul's situational rulings forward into permanent church discipline. The damage done by 1 Timothy is hard to overstate: it has been the load-bearing proof-text for excluding women from ordination, preaching, teaching, eldership, and leadership in conservative Christianity for nearly two thousand years.

### Paul's Apocalyptic Frame Explains Everything

This is the load-bearing concept Tabor returns to from his earlier videos. Paul believed Jesus would return within his lifetime — within a few years. The dead would rise, the living would be transformed into glorious spirit beings "above the angels," and the categories of male/female, slave/free, Jew/Gentile would all dissolve. In Galatians 3:28 Paul says it explicitly: in Christ there is neither male nor female. But Paul also knows that until the parousia arrives, the old creation persists. So his interim ethics:

- Slaves, obey your masters
- Wives, submit to your husbands; women, keep silent in the assemblies
- Jews respect Gentiles, Gentiles respect Jews
- Each person remain in the state they were called in

Tabor's read: Paul probably **didn't care that much** about the substance of the cultural order. He cared about not scandalizing the more conservative Jewish-Christian wing — James and the Jerusalem church, who would expect Jewish-style synagogue order. Paul was managing perceptions during the short interim before the new creation arrived. The frame is **"already, not yet"** — the new age has dawned in Christ but the old age persists until the parousia.

The problem: the parousia didn't come. The interim became permanent. Paul's emergency social conservatism became Christianity's eternal social architecture.

### What Paul Would Say If He Came Back

Tabor closes by speculating directly. If you could bring Paul back to the 21st century and show him what his few hundred words have done across two thousand years — women still struggling for ordination, still split conservative churches, still the lived oppression of half the human race — what would he say?

Tabor's read: Paul would be "appalled." He would say: *"Wait, wait, wait, this was for the time that fit. For today we have to have a new view of things — because my view of the imminent apocalypse that I was going to even live to see obviously did not come about."* The fateful tragedy isn't that Paul wrote these words. It's that the church, **knowing the end did not come**, kept enforcing the interim ethics anyway.
