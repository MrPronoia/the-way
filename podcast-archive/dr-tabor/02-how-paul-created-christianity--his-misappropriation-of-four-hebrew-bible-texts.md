## Summary

Dr. Tabor advances a sharp thesis: **Paul did not just reinterpret Hebrew scripture rabbinically — he created a new religion** by taking four (really five) specific texts and giving them meanings their original contexts cannot support. The distinction matters. Rabbis routinely pluck phrases from context to draw connections ("out of Egypt I called my son" applied to Jesus is fine rabbinic play, since the analogy never claims to override the original meaning). What Paul does is categorically different: he takes these passages and **builds the foundational doctrines of a new faith on them** — justification by faith, the singular-Messiah reading of the Abrahamic covenant, the dismissal of Torah ethics around animal welfare, the eschatological erasure of social distinctions.

Tabor works through each text patiently with his RSV in hand. **Habakkuk 2:4** ("the just shall live by faith") is not about how to *become* righteous — it's a prophet telling a discouraged community to keep trusting God's timing when the promised end is delayed. The Dead Sea Scroll community quotes this verse the same way Habakkuk meant it. Paul re-grammars it to mean "*he who through faith is righteous shall live*" — and then builds the entire doctrine of justification by faith on top of it. **Genesis 15:6** ("Abraham believed God and it was reckoned to him as righteousness") — Tabor argues a more honest reading is that *Abraham counted God as righteous*, i.e., counted God faithful to the promise of a son. **Genesis 12 / Galatians 3:16** — Paul argues "seed" (Hebrew *zera*) is singular and means Christ. But *zera* is a uniplural noun like English "seed"; the entire Abrahamic covenant is plural — a great nation, descendants like the stars. **Deuteronomy 25:4 / 1 Corinthians 9:9** — the command not to muzzle the threshing ox is plainly about animal welfare. Paul: "Is it for oxen God is concerned? It is written entirely for our sake." Tabor, a vegetarian, says this one disturbs him almost more than the theological ones — Paul has stripped the Torah's compassion-for-animals heart and turned the verse into self-justification for ministerial funding.

Why is Paul doing this? Tabor's reading: Paul is fighting Jewish-Christian missionaries who wanted to circumcise Gentile converts. He needs a theological framework where Gentiles can join the family of Abraham without becoming Torah-observant Jews. The "seed = Christ" argument hands him a workaround: you don't have to be Abraham's biological seed; you just have to be "in" the one seed, Christ. The whole framework is eschatologically driven — Paul thinks the end is **five to ten years away** (Caligula's near-attempt to put his statue in the Temple in 41 CE, Nero's persecution, the imminent war with Rome). That's why he tells people not to marry, why slaves shouldn't seek freedom, why male/female and Jew/Greek distinctions don't matter — *the appointed time has grown very short*. Paul's mystical ascent to paradise gave him a "head in heaven" framework that the synoptic gospels (concerned with the destruction of earthly Jerusalem in 70 CE) don't share.

## Key Teachings

### Matthew's Allusion vs. Paul's Re-Foundation

Tabor draws a crucial distinction. Matthew quotes "Out of Egypt I called my son" from Hosea 11:1 — originally about the people of Israel — and applies it to Jesus's family fleeing to Egypt. **That's normal rabbinic play.** Matthew isn't building a new religion on the Hosea verse; he's making a literary connection. Even the "virgin shall conceive" passage in Isaiah 7 (Hebrew *almah* = "young woman," not specifically virgin) is rabbinic typology rather than doctrinal cornerstone.

Paul does something categorically different:
- He takes ~half a dozen key Hebrew Bible verses
- He reads them in ways their context **forbids**
- He **builds the Bedrock of the Christian faith** on these readings — justification by faith, the singular-seed Christology, the dismissal of Torah ethics
- These aren't allusions decorating a larger argument. They ARE the argument.

### Text #1 — Habakkuk 2:4 (Romans 1:17, Galatians 3:11)

**Paul's version:** "*He who through faith is righteous shall live*" — quoted as scripture proving justification by faith.

**Original Habakkuk context:** The prophet has been given a vision that the end is coming. The community is becoming discouraged because it tarries. Habakkuk's word from God: "If it tarries, wait for it; it will surely come. The righteous one will live by faith [in the vision]." The verse is about **a righteous person trusting God's timing**, not about how to *become* righteous.

**Dead Sea Scrolls confirm:** The Qumran community quotes this same verse in their pesher commentary on Habakkuk. Their context? They're decades past the Teacher of Righteousness's death; the predicted end hasn't come. They use the verse exactly the way Habakkuk meant it: keep trusting; the vision will come.

Tabor's verdict: this is "Paul's favorite verse" and "the whole Christian Gospel is built on this one verse" — but Paul has changed what the verse means.

### Text #2 — Genesis 15:6 (Romans 4, Galatians 3:6-9)

**Paul's version:** "Abraham believed God and it was reckoned to him as righteousness" — taken to mean Abraham's faith was credited as a righteousness Abraham didn't have. From this Paul builds: Gentiles don't need circumcision; faith alone justifies (because Abraham was justified in Genesis 15 *before* his circumcision in Genesis 17).

**Tabor's better reading:** The grammar can also support "Abraham believed God, and [Abraham] counted [God] as righteous." Meaning: Abraham trusted God's promise of a son in his old age — "he did not stagger in unbelief" — he counted God as faithful to the promise. This matches Habakkuk: a *righteous* person trusts God; faith is not the *mechanism* of righteousness.

The original Genesis 15 context is not about a sinful Abraham being justified. Abraham is not in a state of sin in that passage; God has just promised him a son. Abraham's belief is trust in the promise, not credit-conversion from unrighteousness.

### Text #3 — Genesis 12 / Galatians 3:16 (the singular "seed" argument)

**Paul's version:** "Now to Abraham and his offspring were the promises made. He does not say 'and to offsprings,' as referring to many, but referring to one, 'and to your offspring,' who is Christ" (Gal 3:16).

**The problem:** Hebrew *zera* (seed) is what linguists call uniplural — singular form with plural meaning. English does the same thing: "his seed scattered" doesn't mean one sperm. The Abrahamic covenant is unambiguously about plural descendants: "I will make of you a great nation"; "your offspring shall be like the stars of heaven"; "in your seed all the nations shall be blessed." The promise to *Isaac* and *Jacob* uses the same word the same way.

Genesis 12:1-3 — the foundational Abrahamic promise — is about a **great nation, the land, blessings to the world**. It cannot mean "one guy named Jesus someday."

Tabor's read on Paul's motive: he's making a convoluted argument so Gentile converts don't need to be circumcised. If you're "in Christ" (the one seed), you're Abraham's child by adoption — no circumcision required. Watch the slip: Paul says seed is singular when it refers to Christ, but then *plural* when he applies it to believers: "if you are Christ's, then you are Abraham's seed" (Gal 3:29).

### Text #4 — Deuteronomy 25:4 / 1 Corinthians 9:9 (Don't muzzle the ox)

**Paul's version:** "You shall not muzzle an ox while it treads out the grain. Is it for oxen that God is concerned? Or does he not certainly say it for our sake? Yes, for our sake it was written" (1 Cor 9:9-10) — Paul uses this to argue ministers should be paid.

**Original context:** The Torah is teaching animal welfare. The ox threshing the grain should be allowed to eat some of it — basic compassion for working animals. This sits within a broader Torah ethic: rest your animals on the Sabbath; feed your animals before sitting down to eat yourself; don't take a mother bird with her young; "a righteous man regards the life of his animal" (Proverbs 12:10).

**Tabor's reaction:** "Paul didn't care about animals — that's clear." Worse than the theological misreadings, because here Paul takes a passage that establishes a core ethical principle — Torah compassion for the non-human world — and explicitly **denies it**: "Is it for oxen God is concerned?" Yes, actually. That is precisely what God is concerned about in the passage.

This connects to Tabor's larger argument about the Essene/Nazarene tradition: vegetarian, anti-sacrifice, compassionate toward animals. Paul represents a sharp break from that line.

### Text #5 — "Neither Jew nor Greek, Slave nor Free, Male nor Female" (Galatians 3:28)

This one is more sympathetic — Tabor doesn't accuse Paul of pure misreading here, but unpacks it.

The contrast: Rabbi Akiva's student's morning prayer thanked God *not* to be a Gentile, *not* to be a slave, *not* to be a woman. Paul flips the script: in Christ, those distinctions don't matter.

But the framework is **eschatological**, not egalitarian for its own sake. In 1 Corinthians 7 Paul says:
- If you're circumcised, don't try to undo it
- If you're uncircumcised, don't get circumcised
- If you're a slave, don't seek freedom
- If you have a wife, live as if you don't

And in 1 Corinthians 11 he reasserts a male/female hierarchy.

The reason: "The appointed time has grown very short." Paul thinks the end is in 5-10 years. So distinctions don't matter because *this age is passing away*. Whether Paul would have applied "neither male nor female" as a present-time ethic if he had known the church would last 2000 years — that's the open question.

### Paul's Apocalyptic Timeline

Tabor reconstructs why Paul thinks the end is imminent:
- **41 CE:** Emperor Caligula tries to put his statue in the Jerusalem Temple. Fulfills Daniel's "abomination of desolation" for many. Doesn't happen, but it's a trial run — proves the prophecy could land.
- **50s CE:** Nero comes to power. Eventually persecutes Christians, blames the Rome fire on them.
- **666 = Nero:** The Book of Revelation's gematria. By the time Paul is writing 1 Corinthians (51-52 CE), the political instability looks like the end.

Paul probably thinks: 5 years, maybe 10, before the parousia.

That's why he can say:
- Don't get married
- Slaves stay slaves
- Vegetarianism doesn't matter (Romans 14)
- Eat the meat sacrificed to idols, doesn't matter (1 Cor 8-10)

It's all "the time is short" — but the time wasn't short, and 2000 years later those instructions are still in the Christian canon as timeless doctrine.

### Paul's Spatial Theology vs. the Synoptics

Mark 13 and Luke 21 are about the **destruction of earthly Jerusalem** (70 CE). They're geopolitical, anti-Roman, sometimes anti-Jewish (Luke especially: "great wrath upon this people; they will be led captive among all nations").

Paul, by contrast, never talks about earthly Jerusalem's fate. In Galatians: "Jerusalem above is the mother of us all... Jerusalem below is in slavery with her children." His framework is **vertical** (heaven/earth, above/below), not horizontal (now/then in history). Tabor connects this to Paul's mystical ascent in 2 Corinthians 12 — "his head is in heaven." Paul is a mystic; the synoptic gospel writers are not.

### Why Paul Did This

Tabor speculates the motive: Paul faced Jewish-Christian missionaries (likely sent or backed by James's Jerusalem community) who wanted Gentile converts to be circumcised — become full proselytes, become Jews. Paul rejects this. Maybe he believes Gentiles cannot become Jews ("you didn't get snipped on the eighth day"). Maybe he wants a Gentile-friendly mission.

Either way, he builds the theological scaffolding to bypass circumcision: justification by faith alone (Habakkuk), Abraham as proto-faith-justified before he was circumcised (Genesis 15 before 17), Gentile adoption as Abraham's seed through the "one seed" Christ (Galatians 3).

The result: a portable, Gentile-friendly faith that no longer requires Torah observance. **A new religion.**

## The Galatians 3 "Seed" Sleight-of-Hand

A specific tell that Tabor flags: in Galatians 3:16 Paul argues "seed" is singular and refers to Christ alone. Thirteen verses later in 3:29 he says "if you are Christ's, then you are Abraham's seed" — and now *seed* is plural again, referring to the whole community of believers. Paul makes the grammatical claim that suits his argument in each verse and ignores the contradiction.
