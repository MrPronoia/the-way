## Summary

Dr. Tabor lays out his reconstruction of what likely happened at the first Easter, working from the chronologically earliest account (Paul's first-person vision) forward to the latest (John's flesh-and-bones encounter). The argument is straightforward: the resurrection narrative we inherit is **the result of about 40 years of apologetic embellishment**, starting from a visionary experience and ending with a resuscitated corpse eating fish. Paul, our earliest witness, describes nothing like Luke or John's Jesus. He describes a "metamorphosis" — a transformation of the dust-body into a glorified Spirit body. He himself doesn't know if his experience was in or out of the body. By the time you reach John 20-21, Thomas is touching wounds and Jesus is grilling breakfast on the beach. Same event? Tabor: not even close.

The kicker is Tabor's reconstructed timeline. The disciples flee to Galilee in despair. The Gospel of Peter (non-canonical but ancient) describes seven days of "weeping and mourning" — not the celebratory meetings the later gospels imply. Then Peter, James, and the others begin to have overwhelming visionary experiences of Jesus's presence. Years later, Paul has his own version. The empty tomb, Tabor suggests, is *not* incompatible with Paul's framework — but Paul would picture a body that simply **dematerialized** into a glorified state, leaving shroud-residue behind. The corpse-walking-around imagery in Luke and John, Tabor argues, is later apologetic — designed for second-century skeptics like Celsus who said "everybody has visions; prove it was physical." So the gospels gradually concretized the encounters. **The earliest experience was visionary; the later accounts are embellishments.**

## Key Teachings

### Resurrection Is Metamorphosis, Not Resuscitation

Paul's word is *metamorphosis* — the same root behind caterpillar-to-butterfly. Resurrection in Paul means transformation from a "breathing dust being" (Hebrew *nephesh*, the same animating breath given to animals) into a glorified, immortal, powerful Spirit being. **It is not a corpse getting up and walking out of a tomb.** The dust-body is gone; what comes forth is something categorically new.

Paul mixes two metaphors at once in 2 Corinthians 5:
- **Tent imagery** (Platonic echo): you live in a tent, you exit the tent, you receive a permanent building
- **Clothing imagery:** you take off old clothes, you put on new ones — or get "further clothed" without going through the naked/dead state

Paul's hope is not "to be found naked" (disembodied) but to be re-clothed in the new glorified body. This is dualistic — *you are not your body* — but it ends in an embodied state, just a transformed one.

### How the Tomb Could Be Empty Without Being a Resuscitation

Tabor disagrees mildly with Crossan and Ehrman, who treat the empty tomb as a later legend. He thinks Paul's framework can accommodate an empty tomb — just not in the way modern Christians imagine.

Picture a Herodian rock-cut tomb. A body wrapped in a shroud, laid on the *arcosolium* niche. **An "energy field" sweeps the tomb.** The physical remains — whatever's left of the dust-body — transmogrify or melt away. Only some shroud residue remains. You walk in and ask, "Where is he?" — not because a corpse stood up and left, but because the body that was there has been transformed into something that no longer occupies that space.

This is what Tabor thinks Paul would picture. **Not corpse-revival. Not a stolen body. A dematerialization.**

### Paul's "What About the Bodies?" Indifference

Paul is genuinely uninterested in the physical remains. By his analogy: if you've changed clothes, why would you carry your old clothes around with you? Tabor cites Crossan's provocation — early Christians could in principle have visited Jesus's tomb *with a corpse still in it* and said "this is testimony to the resurrection; he left his tent behind." The point isn't whether bodily atoms are accounted for. The point is that Jesus is now in a glorified state at the right hand of God. **The body is the discarded tent.**

Does the glorified Jesus need lungs? A stomach? Organs to respirate? Tabor: of course not. None of that is the new body Paul is talking about.

### The Gospel Timeline of Embellishment

Tabor sketches the chronological development:

- **Mark (earliest, ~70 CE):** No resurrection appearances. The Transfiguration in Mark 9 is Mark's resurrection moment — *prolepsis*, a foretaste. The young man at the empty tomb says "you will see him in Galilee, as he said." That's pointing back to Mark 9's transfigured glorified Christ, not promising a campfire reunion.
- **Matthew (~80 CE):** Two appearances. The mountain encounter (Matt 28) is *misty* — clouds wafting, "some doubted" even after seeing him. Probably set on the same high mountain as the Transfiguration. Still visionary in tone.
- **Paul's own experience (chronologically earlier, ~37 CE):** A blinding-light vision. He shows up to the others terrified of him; they accept his testimony. He says he saw Jesus, heard a voice — but admits he doesn't know if it was in or out of the body (2 Cor 12).
- **Luke (~85 CE):** Now Jesus eats fish. The corpse needs to be *demonstrably* physical because skeptics are pushing back.
- **John (~90-100 CE):** Thomas can put his finger in the wounds. Jesus eats. **And yet** — Jesus also walks through locked doors and just kind of disappears, like an angelic epiphany. The author is trying to have it both ways: physical enough for apologetics, spiritual enough to fit the early tradition.

The trajectory is clear. **Visionary → embellished physical → resuscitated corpse with a side of "but also spiritual."**

### The Gospel of Peter — Seven Days of Weeping

A telling detail Tabor draws from the non-canonical Gospel of Peter: after the crucifixion, the disciples *wept and mourned for seven days*. Then Peter says, "I'm going fishing" — and the fragment breaks off there.

Tabor: this fits a despair-then-vision arc, not a we-just-had-breakfast-with-Jesus arc. The disciples fled to Galilee broken. **Then** the visionary experiences began. The Galilee mountain encounters of Matthew, the Galilee fishing scene of John 21 — all of these post-despair appearances. The Jerusalem-resurrection-Sunday narrative is itself a later compression.

### Why the Appearances Are Only to Believers

Williams raises a sharp point: the resurrection only ever appears to disciples. Never to Pilate, never to a Pharisee, never to a Sanhedrin member, never to anyone who hadn't already followed Jesus. If it were a corporeal resuscitation event, why no testimony from outside the inner circle?

Tabor: exactly. And even *inside* the inner circle, Matthew 28 admits "some doubted" — *after seeing the risen Jesus*. The appearances are tentative, private, partial. **That's what you'd expect from visionary experiences within a grief-shattered community. Not what you'd expect from a publicly resuscitated corpse.**

### The Best Resurrection Account Is the First 10 Verses of John 20

Tabor's favorite reconstruction passage: **John 20:1-10.** Mary Magdalene goes alone, early, to the tomb. The stone is already rolled away. She thinks someone has taken the body. She sees a gardener. She runs away afraid. Two disciples come, see the tomb is empty, and leave.

That's the natural account. Stop reading at verse 10. Everything after that — the Risen Jesus appearing, Thomas's wound-touching, John 21's breakfast on the beach — is the embellishment layer. Tabor argues those scenes are added to satisfy the apologetic need to *prove* the resurrection against skeptics like Celsus who pointed out that visions don't count as evidence.

## Misconceptions Addressed

| Misconception | Tabor's Response |
|---------------|------------------|
| "The resurrection accounts all line up" | They progressively embellish. Mark has no appearances. Paul describes a vision. By John 20-21, Jesus eats breakfast and lets Thomas touch his wounds. The trajectory is unmistakable. |
| "Paul's experience was the same kind of event as the gospels' encounters" | Paul himself says he doesn't know if it was "in the body or out of the body." That is categorically different from Thomas's finger-in-the-wounds. |
| "The empty tomb proves a physical resuscitation" | Even granting an empty tomb, Paul's framework would picture *dematerialization* into a glorified body — not a corpse standing up and walking out. |
| "The risen Jesus appeared to many independent witnesses" | The appearances are exclusively to existing disciples. No Pharisees, no Romans, no neutrals. And even within the inner circle, "some doubted." |
| "Mark's silence on appearances is just a manuscript problem" | Tabor argues Mark's *theology* of resurrection is the Transfiguration (Mark 9). The "you will see him in Galilee" line points back to that, not forward to a campfire reunion. |
