## Summary

This is the sixth installment of Tabor's "Seven Fateful New Testament Texts" series, focused on **Romans 13:1-7** — Paul's instruction that *"every person be subject to the governing authorities, for there is no authority except from God, and those that exist have been instituted by God."* The rulers are God's servants, God's ministers; they bear the sword to execute God's wrath on wrongdoers; pay your taxes; render honor to whom honor is due. Tabor's verdict matches the pattern of the series: the original context is one thing, but the centuries of harm produced by lifting these words out of that context and treating them as a timeless universal rule have been catastrophic. The text became the textual engine of the **divine right of kings** in Christian Europe — the theological brake that kept populations from overthrowing tyrants because the church taught them that resisting the ruler meant resisting God's appointed agent.

Tabor traces the same passage forward into **1 Peter 2:13-17** — "be subject for the Lord's sake to every human institution, whether to the emperor as supreme or to governors... fear God, honor the emperor" — which he reads as pseudo-Petrine material that effectively brings the historical Peter into the Pauline theological fold. The decisive interpretive move Tabor makes: Paul wrote this under one of the most oppressive periods of Roman imperial history, during the reigns of Caligula, Claudius, and Nero. He cannot possibly have meant that every emperor — including ones whose cruelty was already legendary — was morally good. What Paul meant was situational: the apocalypse was imminent (five to ten years out in his mind), Rome was about to be crushed by the returning Messiah, and during the brief remaining interval Paul wanted his communities to be respectable, tax-paying, non-revolutionary citizens. The end didn't come. The interim ethic became permanent doctrine. And rulers across two thousand years — from medieval kings claiming divine right, to slaveholders justifying their authority, to apartheid theologians, to clergy ordering Christians to obey the Nazi state — quoted Romans 13 as their warrant.

## Key Teachings

### What Romans 13:1-7 Actually Says

Paul's instruction is sweeping. *"Let every person be subject to the governing authorities, for there is no authority except from God, and those that exist have been instituted by God."* Tabor flags the precision of Paul's language: not "from God" loosely but **"from God" as in instituted by God** — and not just some authorities but *those that exist*. Paul continues: *"Therefore he who resists the authorities resists what God has appointed, and those who resist will incur judgment."* Rulers are not a terror to good conduct but to bad — so do good and you receive the ruler's approval, "*for he is God's servant for your good.*" If you do wrong, be afraid, because *"he does not bear the sword in vain."* Tabor notes the sword imagery: a sword is not for whipping or restraining — it is for **execution**. This is the proof-text capital-punishment advocates have used for centuries. Paul closes: pay taxes, pay revenue, render respect, render honor — *"for the authorities are ministers of God, attending to this very thing."*

### Paul's Apocalyptic Context Makes the Rule Situational

This is the load-bearing point Tabor returns to from his earlier videos in the series. Paul is **intensely apocalyptic**. He believes Jesus is going to appear in the clouds of heaven within his lifetime. He says elsewhere that "the appointed time has grown very short" (1 Cor 7:29) and that the form of this world is passing away. In **2 Thessalonians** Paul writes that when Jesus returns he will come "in flaming fire taking vengeance on those who do not obey the gospel." In **Romans 1** — the very same letter as the chapter 13 passage — Paul declares that the wrath of God is being revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and offers a long litany of deeds God will punish in the judgment. So Paul is not pro-Roman in any deep theological sense. He thinks **Rome is about to be crushed** by the returning Messiah and all the powers of the world will fall. The instruction to obey Roman authorities is an **interim ethic** for the brief remaining interval before the parousia — keep your head down, pay your taxes, don't get crushed by Rome before God crushes Rome. Strip the eschatology and the rationale collapses. The verse should not have outlived the timeline.

### The Roman Context — Caligula, Claudius, Nero

Tabor underscores how dark the period actually was. Paul wrote Romans in the late 50s CE. The recent and contemporary emperors were not "good rulers" by any reasonable moral standard. **Caligula** (37-41 CE) had ordered his own statue erected in the Jerusalem temple — an apocalyptic flashpoint averted only by his assassination. **Claudius** (41-54 CE), conventionally remembered as the better one, expelled the Jews from Rome during Paul's lifetime. And **Nero** (54-68 CE) was emperor when Paul wrote Romans — the man who would later turn Rome's first major Christian persecution into a public entertainment. Tabor points out that contemporary accounts of all these reigns are full of murders, palace intrigue, poisonings, cruelty, and abuses of power. Even Augustus and Tiberius — Tiberius being emperor when Jesus was crucified — were not morally clean by any account. The text cannot have meant what later interpreters made it mean. Paul knew exactly who was on the throne. He was writing about civic order under apocalyptic compression, not endorsing imperial morality.

### How Romans 13 Became the Divine Right of Kings

Once Europe became Christianized politically and militarily, Romans 13 became the **theological brake** on revolt. Tabor offers the historical question: why weren't kings more often overthrown? They had armies, economic power, and social leverage — but a major restraint was **the church's teaching**, taught to the people, that *"you have no business overthrowing one of God's ministers, one of God's agents."* The doctrine of the **divine right of kings** runs the whole way back through Christian Europe to its textual root in Romans 13. Governmental authorities have loved this passage because it does the work for them — it converts political compliance into religious obedience and political resistance into sin against God. Tabor notes the historical pivot: when democracies start to arise in the 18th century — the American Revolution, the French Revolution, other violent overthrows of hereditary powers — they are **essentially going against this divine right of kings**, which means going against the church's reading of Romans 13. The Enlightenment had to break this verse's hold to make modern political freedom possible.

### 1 Peter 2:13-17 — Pseudo-Petrine Pauline Reinforcement

Tabor pulls in the companion passage from 1 Peter: *"Be subject for the Lord's sake to every human institution, whether it be to the emperor as supreme, or to governors as sent by him to punish those who do wrong and to praise those who do right... Live as free men... fear God, honor the emperor."* The phrasing is nearly identical to Romans 13. Tabor reads 1 Peter the way most critical scholars do: it is **not actually by Peter**. The letter is an attempt by a later author to bring the historic Peter into the Pauline fold, and most of its theological content is second-generation Pauline material — sometimes verbally identical to Paul's authentic letters. The effect: the bloodguilt-of-the-Jews charge gets a Petrine second witness in Matthew 27, and the obey-the-emperor charge gets a Petrine second witness here. What was Paul's situational apocalyptic ethic now appears in canon under both Paul's and Peter's names, doubling its enforceability. Tabor flags the Hebrew Bible companion (Exodus 22:28: *"you shall not revile God nor curse a ruler of your people"*) — but even there, the underlying word can mean "revile" or "defy" or "speak against" rather than the strong English sense of "curse," and the verse does not exempt rulers from prophetic critique elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible.

### Why This Is a "Fateful" Passage

Tabor's verdict: Romans 13, more than perhaps any other passage in the New Testament, has been used to **immobilize** Christians against tyranny. The text has been read non-historically — as if it were a general principle for humankind in all times and places rather than an apocalyptic interim ethic written under specific conditions. The damage has been institutional and continuous: medieval kings claiming God's mandate; Confederate theologians defending slavery as a divinely ordained order; apartheid South Africa's Dutch Reformed leadership citing Romans 13; German Lutherans instructed to obey Hitler's state as God's minister; pastors today citing it to silence congregations who want to resist injustice. The text Paul wrote thinking he had five years to live before the apocalypse has been wielded for two thousand years to tell oppressed peoples that their oppression is God's will.
