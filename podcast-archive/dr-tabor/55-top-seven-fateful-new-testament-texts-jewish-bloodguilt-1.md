## Key Teachings

### Matthew 27:25 Is a Matthean Insertion — Not in Mark or Luke

Tabor's most important point comes through visual comparison. Mark 15 has Pilate's trial scene. Luke 23 has Pilate's trial scene. Both record the crowd's cry of "crucify him" and Pilate's release of Barabbas. **Neither has the self-curse.** Tabor literally circles the whitespace on his screen where the verse would be. Matthew, writing later and using Mark as a primary source, **inserts** verses 24-25 — Pilate washing his hands, declaring his own innocence, and then "all the people" answering "*his blood be on us and on our children*." This is not a stray detail. This is a redactional addition by one evangelist out of four. Tabor refuses the common harmonizing move of flattening the gospels into a single composite story; he insists that scholars must let each gospel speak for itself. And Matthew's unique addition is the textual root of the Christ-killer charge.

### How "His Blood Be On Us" Became the Charge of Deicide

The trajectory from a single Matthean verse to a 2000-year hatred runs through one theological move: the deification of Jesus. In the Greco-Roman world, gods are eternal or immortal — they cannot die. But once Jesus is theologized as God incarnate, killing Jesus becomes killing God. Tabor flags the term **deicide** (god-killing) as a real medieval charge against the Jewish people — though "Christ-killer" was the more common shorthand. The mechanism is straightforward: take a redactional line by one gospel author, generalize "all the people" to mean the entire Jewish people across all generations, extend "and on our children" to mean every Jewish descendant forever, and you have a textual warrant for collective, hereditary, perpetual guilt. The same theological move turned a localized trial scene into a permanent religious indictment.

### Paul's Companion Passage — 1 Thessalonians 2:14-16

Tabor pairs Matthew 27:25 with Paul's blanket charge in 1 Thessalonians 2: "*the Jews, who killed both the Lord Jesus and the prophets and drove us out and displeased God and oppose all men... but God's wrath has come upon them at last.*" This is one of the harshest anti-Jewish passages in the New Testament, and many scholars want to **interpolate** it out — argue it was inserted later by a non-Pauline hand because Paul could not have written something this severe. Tabor disagrees. He has spent his entire career on Paul, written two books on him, and reads this as authentic Paul writing under specific historical pressure. Around 51 CE Paul has lived through Caligula's near-desecration of the Jerusalem temple, Claudius expelling Jews from Rome, ongoing revolt and unrest from Herod the Great's death (4 BCE) through to the eventual destruction of Jerusalem (70 CE). Paul thinks the apocalypse is breaking. "*God's wrath has come upon them at last*" is an apocalyptic verdict, not a casual slur — but read out of that frame it becomes the second textual pillar of Christian Jew-hatred.

### John 8:44 — "You Are of Your Father the Devil"

The third passage in Tabor's cluster is **John 8:44**, where Jesus tells his Jewish interlocutors — translated in John as simply "*the Jews*" — that they are "of your father the devil, and your will is to do your father's desires." Tabor is careful: in context this is directed at specific opponents of Jesus, not the entire Jewish people. But John's use of "the Jews" as a blanket designation, combined with Matthew 27:25 and 1 Thessalonians 2, creates a textual triad — gospel narrative, apostolic letter, and direct words attributed to Jesus — that medieval Christianity weaponized into a unified case for the Jewish people as a demonic, Christ-killing, prophet-killing collective.

### Paul's Apocalyptic Frame — Why "God's Wrath Has Come Upon Them at Last"

Tabor's defense of the authenticity of 1 Thessalonians 2 hinges on understanding Paul's apocalyptic timeline. Paul writes in 1 Corinthians (not long after Thessalonians) that "*the appointed time has grown very short*" — language pulled straight from Daniel. He expects a final evil ruler to appear in the Jerusalem temple and claim to be God (2 Thessalonians 2). He has lived through Caligula's order to install his own statue in the temple — averted only by Caligula's assassination by his Praetorian Guard. The Jewish people are in turmoil — Rome is squeezing them, revolts are erupting, the world feels like it is breaking. Paul, in apocalyptic mode, reads the chaos as God's wrath manifesting against those who rejected Jesus the Messiah. The passage is **dated** — anchored to a specific apocalyptic moment. Strip that frame and absolutize the line into permanent doctrine and you have Christian antisemitism's textual engine.

### The Michelangelo's Moses Horns — Visual Trace of the Damage

Tabor closes with a striking visual: Michelangelo's Moses in Rome, an iconic Renaissance statue — and Moses has **horns**. The mythology behind it: Christian Europe believed Jews had hidden horns under their hair, marking them as devil-children. The origin is a **mistranslation**. Exodus 34 describes Moses descending Sinai with his face beaming rays of light. The Hebrew word for rays is *karen* — which can also mean *horn*. The Vulgate rendered it as "horned." Combined with the Matthew/Paul/John triad and the "Christ-killer" charge, the horns become visual confirmation: Jews are demonic, Jews killed God, Jews are marked by the devil. The "joke" Tabor mentions — *here, feel my head, no horns* — is not funny, he says, when you count the millions of Jewish lives ended by exactly this mythology.
