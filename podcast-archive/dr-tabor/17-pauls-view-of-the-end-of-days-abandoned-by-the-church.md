## Summary

In this informal lecture pulled from his "Quest for the Historical Paul" course, Dr. Tabor walks through **every passage in the seven undisputed letters of Paul** that touches on the end of days. The thesis is that Paul's eschatology was not the "go to heaven when you die" framework the church later adopted. It was an **apocalyptic, imminent, cosmic-transformation framework** with a three-stage process found nowhere else in the New Testament — and the church abandoned it because it didn't fit the developing doctrine of eternal heaven and hell.

Tabor catalogs Paul's vocabulary of the end: *parousia* (arrival/presence — the same Greek word used for the Emperor Augustus's official visit to Ephesus), *apocalypsis* (revealing — pulling back the curtain), *the day of the Lord*, *the wrath to come*, *the day of Christ*, *the telos* (the completion). He demonstrates that Paul names the parousia five or six times in 1 Thessalonians alone, includes himself among "we who are alive and remain," and tells the Thessalonians the dead won't miss out because they will be raised first when Christ descends. This is not metaphor. This is not "spiritual" in the modern abstract sense. Paul expected this in his own lifetime.

The lecture's payoff comes in the analysis of **1 Corinthians 15** as Paul's three-stage cosmic process: (1) Christ as firstfruits, already raised, (2) those who belong to Christ at his parousia — living and dead transformed in the twinkling of an eye, and (3) the *telos*, when Christ has destroyed every rule, authority, and power, the last enemy death is destroyed, and **God becomes all in all**. Tabor draws out Paul's *cosmic salvation* in Romans 8 — the creation itself groans in labor pains, waiting to be set free from its bondage to decay. Humanity is the "mockup version" of what God is producing: glorified offspring who will rule *above the angels* in a transformed cosmos. Tabor closes by including 2 Thessalonians (which he thinks may be authentic) and noting Paul's Caligula-statue-in-the-Temple context — the apocalyptic urgency Paul felt was rooted in real Roman political crises. The church traded all of this for "accept Jesus and go to heaven." Tabor's point: what Paul actually believed is *more* radical and *more* interesting than what replaced it.

## Key Teachings

### Parousia Means "Arrival" — and It's Imperial Language

The Greek *parousia* literally means presence/arrival/appearance. Tabor's favorite illustration: an inscription describing **the visit of Emperor Augustus to Ephesus** uses *parousia* for the emperor's official state arrival. Paul uses the same word both for his own visits to his churches ("not only in my presence but also in my absence" — Phil 2:12) and for the future arrival of Jesus. Jesus is currently *absent* (in heaven, present only through the Holy Spirit). The parousia is when he *arrives*.

Rapture theology often confuses translations of "coming with his holy ones" (1 Thess 3:13). Tabor: the holy ones are the angels. "Rapture people make a big deal about that but they don't understand the Greek."

### Paul's Eschatological Vocabulary

Tabor walks through Paul's terms as a working dictionary:

- **Parousia** — arrival/presence (1 Thess 2:19, 3:13, 4:15, 5:23; 1 Cor 15:23; Phil 1:26)
- **Apocalypsis** — revealing, pulling back the curtain (1 Cor 1:7)
- **The day of the Lord / the day of Christ / the day** (1 Cor 1:8, 3:13, 5:5; Phil 1:6, 1:10, 2:16)
- **The wrath to come** — apocalyptic judgment (1 Thess 1:10; Rom 1:18, 2:8)
- **Erkomenou** — "the one coming" (1 Cor 4:5)
- **Telos** — the completion, the end (1 Cor 15:24)

Each term carries Paul's expectation of a definite, near, public event.

### 1 Thessalonians 4 — The Dead Won't Miss Out

Paul's earliest letter has the most parousia language. The Thessalonians are worried: people in the community have died — did they miss the resurrection? Paul's answer:

> "The Lord himself will descend from heaven with a cry of command, with the archangel's call and the sound of the trumpet of God. And the dead in Christ will rise first; then we who are alive, who are left, will be caught up together with them in the clouds to meet the Lord in the air."

Tabor's emphasis: Paul includes himself in *we who are alive and remain*. He expects to be alive for this. The dead are raised first as a courtesy — they don't miss out. The living are caught up *with them*.

### 1 Corinthians 15 — Paul's Three-Stage Cosmic Process

This is the only place in the New Testament where Paul (or anyone) lays out a staged eschatology in this detail. The Q source, the Didache, Mark 13, the Gospel of John — none of them have this structure. Without 1 Corinthians 15, Paul's eschatology would look much like Mark's: Christ comes, gathers the saints, end of story.

**Stage 1 — Christ the firstfruits.** Already raised. The first of a new kind of being.

**Stage 2 — Those who belong to Christ at his parousia.** Living and dead transformed in the twinkling of an eye at the last trumpet. The mortal "puts on" immortality.

**Stage 3 — The telos.** An interim period during which Christ "must reign until he has put all his enemies under his feet." Every rule, authority, and power is destroyed. **The last enemy to be destroyed is death itself.** Then Christ delivers the kingdom to the Father, and "God will be all in all" (1 Cor 15:28).

Tabor: "It's a process of reigning, of defeating what he calls every rule, authority, and power... certainly for Paul, Satan, the God of this world, all of his angels, various rulers and authorities that would oppose the rule of God."

### Cosmic Salvation in Romans 8 — Creation in Labor

Paul's eschatology isn't just about humans. **The cosmos itself is being saved.** Romans 8:

> "The creation itself will be set free from its bondage to decay and obtain the freedom of the glory of the children of God. We know that the whole creation has been groaning in travail together until now."

Tabor's reading: Paul thinks creation is *pregnant* with the new age. The Earth itself is in labor, waiting to deliver the glorified children of God. Humanity is currently the **"mockup version"** — disposable, corruptible, a test run. The permanent model is what comes after the parousia.

> "We are mockup versions of a permanent model that could be produced later — but now it's disposable. It's a test. If it doesn't work out for you, then the mockup is your last thing and you don't get the permanent version."

### Judgment Is Universal — and Cuts Through Believers Too

Paul talks repeatedly about judgment — and crucially, **including for Christians**. 2 Corinthians 5:10: "We must all appear before the judgment seat of Christ, so that each one may receive good or evil." 1 Corinthians 3 — the fiery test of each person's life-work: gold/silver/precious stones vs. wood/hay/straw.

Tabor's commentary: "This thing about *accept Christ as your savior and there is no judgment* — well, it depends on how you live." Romans 2 — God will render to every person *according to his works*. To those who patiently do good, eternal life. To those who do evil, wrath and fury. **No partiality** between Jew and Greek.

Tabor cites Jason Staples's lecture at Bart Ehrman's 2024 conference: most Christians don't understand "works" in Paul. The judgment is according to works. What faith does is reorient the heart so that good works follow. Not earning salvation by merit — but not a free pass either.

### Resurrection Means Transformation, Not Resuscitation

Paul's "resurrection of the dead" is **not** Lazarus-style coming-back-in-the-same-body. There are three resurrections in the Hebrew Bible and several in the Gospels (Jesus raises Jairus's daughter, the widow's son, Lazarus), but Paul doesn't count those as *anastasis nekrōn* in his technical sense. Those people came back in corruptible bodies and died again.

For Paul, resurrection means **metamorphosis** — the corruptible body transformed into an incorruptible spirit-body, like the one Jesus now has. "Even the living get resurrection of the dead, even though you say 'well they're living, they're not dead' — yeah, they're dead. They're in a dead body, a corruptible body. We're all the walking dead."

### Paul Believes Humans Will Be Above the Angels

A point Tabor underlines repeatedly across the playlist: Paul's eschatology is **not** humans-become-angels. It's humans-become-*more*-than-angels. 1 Corinthians 6:3: "Do you not know that we are to judge angels?" The glorified ones will rule the cosmos with Christ.

This is why Tabor often says Paul's gospel sounds "Mormon-like" — and notes that Mormonism explicitly drew from this Pauline framework. Mainstream Christianity, focused on "go to heaven," lost the actual scope of Paul's vision.

### The Imminent Timeline and the Caligula Trigger

Tabor connects Paul's urgency to specific historical events:

- **41 CE** — Emperor Caligula orders his statue placed in the Jerusalem Temple. He's stopped, but it's a "trial run" of Daniel's "abomination of desolation." The Jewish world goes crazy. Paul writes 1 Thessalonians not long after.
- **54 CE** — Nero comes to power after Claudius. Eventually persecutes Christians, blames the Rome fire on them.
- **Paul probably thinks**: 5 years, maybe 10, before the parousia.

This is why Paul tells people not to marry (1 Cor 7), why slaves shouldn't seek freedom, why social distinctions are passing. "The appointed time has grown very short."

### Was Paul a Universalist?

Tabor weighs two readings of stage 3 ("God will be all in all"):

- **Universalist reading** — every Adam of the cosmos eventually redeemed. The death that spread to all is matched by the life that spreads to all (1 Cor 15:22). 2 Cor 5:19 — "God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself."
- **Annihilationist reading** — the unredeemed simply cease to exist (the Hebrew Bible's Sheol = nothingness, per Ehrman's *Heaven and Hell*).

Either way, **eternal conscious torment is not Paul.** That came later.

Tabor's hesitation on full universalism: Paul also believes in free will alongside predestination. The judgment passages are real. But the door to universal redemption is at least open in Paul's text.

### Paul Is Not a Gnostic — The "Fall" Is Later Theology

Important clarification. Gnosticism teaches that humans are pre-existent souls of light who *fell* into matter and must be rescued back to the world of light. Paul's framework is **stages of creation**, not fall-and-return.

> "Paul's idea is stages of creation. First you have the material world made purposely by God — remember he says it was subjected to futility *by God*. So it's not 'you're perfect, you fall into the world, and then you go back.' Very important to understand."

The temporary corruptible world is a deliberate first stage. The glorified cosmos is the goal. Humans aren't returning home — they're becoming something that has never existed before. The Christian doctrine of "the Fall" is post-Pauline, developed partly in reaction to Gnosticism.

### 2 Thessalonians and the Man of Sin

Tabor diverges from consensus here: he thinks 2 Thessalonians may be **genuinely Pauline**. The standard objection is the "delayed expectation" — 1 Thessalonians says the day comes like a thief, but 2 Thessalonians says first the "man of sin" must sit in the Temple of God and proclaim himself God.

Tabor's response: this is Daniel 11:40 territory — the final evil ruler in the Temple. Given that Caligula nearly fulfilled it in 41 CE, Paul could plausibly hold both: the timing is imminent, *and* one more Daniel-style fulfillment is needed first. All early Christians were drenched in Daniel. Mark 13 also tells the reader to "let him understand" — referencing Daniel.

### The Kingdom Is *This* Cosmos Transformed — Not a Different Place

Tabor pushes back on the popular reading of "we will always be with the Lord" as escape to a separate heaven. Paul's vision is **this creation transformed**, not abandoned. Romans 8 makes the creation itself the patient of salvation. Isaiah 11's "the earth will be full of the knowledge of the Lord as the waters cover the sea" is the picture. **New earth**, set free from bondage to decay.

This is why Tabor calls Paul's gospel *participatory* — humans are co-glorified with Christ within a transformed material cosmos, not extracted to a non-material elsewhere.

## Misconceptions Addressed

| Misconception | Tabor's Response |
|---------------|------------------|
| "Paul predicted the end in his lifetime and was wrong, so his eschatology is discredited" | Paul did expect the parousia soon — but the Caligula crisis of 41 CE made that historically reasonable. The deeper framework (cosmic transformation, three stages, God becoming all in all) doesn't depend on the timing. The church chose to discard the framework along with the timing rather than separate them. |
| "Christians go to heaven when they die" | Paul's framework is *not* disembodied heaven. The dead are temporarily "naked" — without a body — until the parousia, when they receive a transformed body. The end-state is a transformed *cosmos*, not extraction to a different realm. |
| "The Rapture is in 1 Thessalonians 4" | The text says the living and the dead meet the Lord in the air, but Paul's framework has them then descend with him to a transformed earth. "Caught up to meet" uses the same Greek imagery as a city's officials going out to meet an arriving emperor and escorting him *back into the city*. |
| "Resurrection means resuscitation — coming back in the same body" | Paul explicitly says no. The corruptible "puts on" the incorruptible. The mortal "puts on" immortality. This is metamorphosis, not corpse revival. |
| "The Christian doctrine of the Fall is Pauline" | The Genesis "fall" as a developed theological category came later, partly in response to Gnosticism. Paul's framework is staged creation, not paradise lost and regained. |
| "Paul believed in eternal conscious torment" | Paul's framework supports either universalism ("God all in all," "in Christ all shall be made alive") or annihilationism (the wicked simply "perish"). Eternal conscious torment as a fixed doctrine is post-Pauline. |
| "Paul's 'judgment' is just for unbelievers" | Romans 2, 2 Cor 5, and 1 Cor 3 all describe judgment of *Christians* by works. The "fiery test" of 1 Cor 3 is for the believing community. |
| "Paul thinks humans become angels" | Paul thinks humans become *more* than angels: "Do you not know that we are to judge angels?" The glorified ones rule the cosmos with Christ. |
| "Paul has no apocalyptic timeline at all (full preterism)" | Paul clearly expects a public, cosmic, future event involving the dead being raised and creation being transformed. He uses imminent language but the framework itself is future-oriented. |
| "1 Corinthians 15 proves bodily (physical-flesh) resurrection" | 1 Cor 15:50 — "flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God." Paul calls the second Adam "a life-giving spirit." The body is real but transformed, not fleshly. |
