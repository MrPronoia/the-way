# Did the Apostle Paul Believe that Jesus was God?

**Speaker:** Dr. James Tabor (PhD, Univ. of Chicago; Professor Emeritus, UNC Charlotte)

**Published:** March 12, 2024

**Playlist:** Paul (#12 of 77)

## Summary

Dr. James Tabor poses the title question as bluntly as possible: did Paul — restricted to his seven undisputed letters — believe Jesus was God in the sense the later church meant (fully God, eternal, one with the Father, the God of the Hebrew Bible becoming a man)? His answer, walked through six load-bearing passages, is **no**. Paul calls Jesus "Lord" (*kyrios*), he calls Jesus "Son of God," he believes Jesus has been exalted to God's right hand and will return to judge the world — but he never collapses Jesus into the one God of the Shema. The first three passages (1 Thessalonians 1:9-10, 1 Corinthians 8:4-6, Romans 1:1-4) all carefully distinguish "the one God the Father" from "the one Lord Jesus Christ, his Son." Paul is doing first-century Jewish messianism, not fourth-century Nicene trinitarianism.

The bulk of the talk is Tabor's careful demolition of the three passages most commonly used to prove Paul taught Jesus's divinity. **Romans 9:5** ("from them comes the Messiah who is God blessed forever") is a punctuation problem — repunctuated naturally, it reads "from them comes the Messiah. God who is over all be blessed forever." Paul never elsewhere calls Jesus God. **1 Corinthians 10:4** ("the rock was Christ") is an allegory about Christian baptism and eucharist, not a claim that Jesus is the Yahweh of Sinai — Paul would be appalled at that reading. **2 Corinthians 8:9** ("though he was rich, for your sake he became poor") sounds incarnational only if you import that frame; in context it parallels Jesus's own teaching in Mark 10 about giving up everything to follow the kingdom.

The climax is Philippians 2:5-11, the so-called Christ hymn — the passage Tabor treats at greater length in Video #08. Here he condenses the argument: Philippians 2 is built on the Adam typology, not on preexistence. The first Adam, in the form/image of God, *grasped* at equality with God (Genesis 3, the *nakash*) and ended up dust. Jesus, the second Adam, did not grasp; he emptied himself, became a servant, was obedient unto death, and *therefore* God exalted him and gave him the name above every name. Equality with God is given as a gift — not held eternally and temporarily set aside. This makes Paul consistent with himself: Jesus is the *firstborn of many brothers and sisters* (Romans 8), the prototype of a transformed humanity, not a unique god-man category. Paul's gospel is metamorphosis — flesh-and-blood "men of dust" transformed into "life-giving spirits" — and Jesus is the first to walk that road.

## Key Teachings

### What Does "God" Even Mean? Three Layers of Confusion

Before the textual work, Tabor lays out the terminological mess that makes this question impossible to answer without precision:

- **"Son of God" vs. "God the Son"** — Two completely different concepts. Luke 3:38 calls Adam "the son of God." The phrase doesn't carry trinitarian freight; it's covenantal/relational language.
- **"Divine" / "Divinity"** — Slippery. Does it mean "shares God's nature ontologically" or "filled with God's spirit such that you encounter God through him"?
- **Eternal vs. exalted** — The Arian controversy of the fourth century. Did Jesus *always* exist (Nicene), or was he a *created* being later exalted (Arian)? Paul's framework, Tabor argues, looks closer to the second — though even that's anachronistic; Paul isn't in the fourth-century debate.
- **Agent / image / reflection** — Even Hebrews 1 (which Tabor notes is not actually a Pauline letter) says Jesus "reflects the glory of God" and "bears the very stamp of his nature." Reflecting God perfectly is not the same as being God ontologically.

Tabor's working definition of the question: when he asks "did Paul believe Jesus was God," he means *fully God, eternal, one with the Father, the God of the Hebrew Bible becoming a man*. That is the common Christian view. And to that question, the seven undisputed letters say no.

### The Methodological Fence: Seven Letters Only

Tabor restricts the analysis to the seven undisputed letters scholars universally accept as Paul's own:

- 1 Thessalonians (his earliest)
- Galatians
- 1 & 2 Corinthians
- Romans
- Philippians
- Philemon

Set aside: Ephesians and Colossians (deutero-Pauline — show Pauline language but probably not Paul's hand), and obviously the Pastorals. Set aside also: Acts (Paul is quoted but not writing) and Hebrews (Pauline-flavored but not Paul). This matters because **Colossians and Ephesians are where the high-Christology imagery starts to appear**, and reading them back into the seven letters is one of the main ways the divinity-of-Jesus argument gets constructed.

### Three Passages That Distinguish God from Jesus

The foundation of Tabor's argument. Three early texts where Paul carefully separates the one God from the one Lord:

**1 Thessalonians 1:9-10** — Describing Gentile converts: they "turned to God from idols, to serve a living and true God, and to wait for his Son from heaven, whom he raised from the dead, Jesus who delivers us from the wrath to come." There is **God**, and there is **his Son** whom God raised. Two distinct subjects.

**1 Corinthians 8:4-6** — "There is no God but one... for although there are many so-called gods... yet for us there is **one God, the Father**, from whom are all things and for whom we exist, and **one Lord, Jesus Christ**, through whom are all things and through whom we exist." This is Paul deliberately echoing the Shema while distinguishing two categories: the one God (the Father, Creator), and the one Lord (Jesus Christ, the agent of the new creation, the second Adam through whom the new humanity is being implemented).

**Romans 1:1-4** — Paul's most systematic statement of his gospel: "concerning his Son, who was descended from David according to the flesh and *designated* Son of God in power according to the Spirit of holiness by his resurrection from the dead." The Greek *horizō* means *designated, marked off, declared*. Jesus is a human being of Davidic descent who was *designated* Son of God at his resurrection. He's not the eternal Second Person of the Trinity setting aside divinity to slum it.

### Romans 9:5 Is a Punctuation Problem, Not a Divinity Prooftext

A favorite proof-text of evangelical apologists: "from them comes the Messiah, who is God over all, blessed forever, amen." Tabor's answer: the Greek has no punctuation. Repunctuate naturally and you get:

> "from them comes the Messiah. [And then a doxology:] God who is over all be blessed forever, amen."

Paul **never elsewhere** calls Jesus God. He has just spent three letters (1 Thess, 1 Cor 8, Romans 1) carefully distinguishing them. Why would he, in passing, suddenly contradict himself in a single ambiguous Greek string? Tabor's answer: he doesn't. The "Messiah is God" reading is a translation choice driven by later theology, not by the Greek itself.

### "The Rock Was Christ" Is Allegory, Not Cosmology

1 Corinthians 10:4 — Paul reads the Exodus story allegorically. Israel passed through the sea and was "baptized into Moses"; they ate manna and drank from the rock "and the rock was Christ." Some readers take this as proof Paul thought Jesus *was* the Yahweh of the Hebrew Bible, pre-incarnate, appearing as the rock at Horeb.

Tabor: it's an analogy. Paul is comparing Christian baptism and eucharist (the next chapter is the Last Supper) to ancient Israel's experience. Just as Israel was sustained by God in the wilderness through manna and water-from-rock, Christians are sustained by Christ through baptism and the bread and wine. To turn this into ontology — "Jesus is Yahweh of Sinai" — is to mistake an allegorical comparison for a metaphysical claim. Paul, Tabor says, would be **appalled** at the modern habit of reading the Hebrew Bible's LORD (Yahweh) as "Jesus Christ already appearing, just not yet in flesh."

### "Though He Was Rich, He Became Poor" Is Not Incarnation

2 Corinthians 8:9 — Paul is raising money for the poor saints. He uses Jesus as a model: "though he was rich, for your sake he became poor, that by his poverty you might become rich." A common reading: rich = preexistent divine glory in heaven; became poor = gave up divinity and took on flesh.

Tabor's read: look at Mark 10:28-30, where Peter says "we have left everything and followed you" and Jesus promises a hundredfold return now plus eternal life later. Jesus called his followers to give up house, family, land — to enter the egalitarian kingdom community as the poor. *That* is what "though he was rich, he became poor" maps onto. Jesus, like his followers, gave up a normal human livelihood (he had brothers, sisters, a place in the world) to embody the kingdom of God. The verse is about *kingdom economics*, not pre-incarnate descent.

### Philippians 2 — The Adam-Christ Reading

The big one. Paul writes:

> "Have this mind among yourselves, which is yours in Christ Jesus, who though he was in the form of God did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant, being born in the likeness of men. And being found in human form he humbled himself and became obedient unto death, even death on a cross. Therefore God has highly exalted him and bestowed on him the name which is above every name..."

Standard reading: Jesus was preexistent in heaven, *was* God, gave that up to become a man, died, and was restored to where he started. Tabor's reading: **Adam typology**.

- **"Form of God"** = Genesis 1:26-27. Adam was made *in the image and likeness of God* — not equal to God, but with the potential.
- **"Did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped"** = Genesis 3. The *nakash* (the "shining one," not yet Satan) tells Adam he can become *like God* by eating the fruit. Adam *grasps* — and ends up dust.
- Jesus is **Adam #2**. Also in the form/image of God. But unlike Adam #1, he *did not grasp*. He emptied himself, became a servant (*doulos*, slave), humbled himself to death — even crucifixion, the most degrading death in the Roman world.
- **Therefore** God exalted him. Equality with God is given as a gift, not seized. The same equality that Adam tried to take, Jesus receives by going the opposite direction.

This makes Paul consistent with himself: Jesus is the *firstborn of many brothers and sisters* (Romans 8). He's not the unique god-man; he's the prototype. The pattern is repeatable. "Have this mind among yourselves" — Paul is literally telling the Philippians to do what Jesus did. If Jesus's mind was "I am the eternal God who graciously decided to slum it as a human," that exhortation makes no sense. If Jesus's mind was "I'm a man of dust who refused to grasp and instead humbled myself," then the exhortation is intelligible — you can do that too.

### Sons of God, Plural — Jesus Is the First, Not the Only

Paul's broader framework, picked up across the seven letters but especially Romans 8: God is begetting **many sons of God** through his Spirit. Jesus is the *firstborn of many brothers and sisters*. The followers, begotten by the Holy Spirit, are themselves being formed into glorified sons of God — they will be transformed at the *parousia* into the same kind of spiritual body Jesus already has.

This is why Tabor keeps insisting: whatever Paul believes about Jesus, he also believes about Jesus's followers. They share the same destiny — the metamorphosis from flesh-and-blood Adam to "life-giving spirit." Jesus is not in a separate ontological category. He's the first one through the door.

If Paul thought Jesus was eternally and uniquely God, this whole framework collapses. Followers are not going to become *God*. But they *are* going to become what Jesus has become — glorified spirit beings, sons of God, rulers of the cosmos above the angels. Which means whatever Jesus became, it's a creaturely exaltation, not divine self-identity.
