## Summary

This short video is Dr. Tabor letting two evangelical broadcasts speak for themselves — and inviting viewers to be the judge. The first clip is from The Ankerberg Show, where host John Ankerberg and a guest named Gary discuss *The Jesus Dynasty*. They actually concede Tabor's scholarly credentials ("University of Chicago grad, department head at UNC Charlotte"), then summarize his thesis fairly well: Tabor distinguishes the family-of-Jesus tradition (Mary, James, the bloodline) from the later Pauline divinization track. They get the Pantera material right enough — that Tabor *speculates* about a Roman soldier named Pantera as Jesus's biological father — but then accuse him of letting "could be" slide into "well, sort of is." The critique here is mostly tone-policing dressed up as scholarship.

The second clip is Hank Hanegraaff, the "Bible Answer Man," responding to a caller from Tyler, Texas who's been reading *Paul and Jesus*. The caller correctly identifies Tabor's argument — that Paul's letters predate the gospels, that the Last Supper "body and blood" language is Pauline rather than original to Jesus, and that drinking blood is foreign to Jewish culture. Hanegraaff's response: "James Tabor is an unreliable guide to the historical Jesus because… he's driving a wedge between the teachings of Paul and the teachings of the other apostles. And this is completely unwarranted." His proof? "The Bible itself emphatically precludes that possibility." That's the entire argument. Then he pivots to communion theology and an inerrancy claim that the entire New Testament — including Revelation — was completed before 70 CE because "it would be inconceivable" that the destruction of the temple wouldn't be mentioned if it had already happened. Tabor's response in the video description is dry: "Read the books. You might be pleasantly surprised."

## Key Teachings

### The Ankerberg Show Gets the Thesis Mostly Right

Credit where it's due: John Ankerberg and his guest Gary actually summarize *The Jesus Dynasty* with reasonable accuracy. They acknowledge Tabor is a real scholar, not a sensationalist. They correctly identify the two-track framework: a family-of-Jesus tradition (Mary, James, bloodline) versus a Pauline track that "slams the door" on that earlier tradition. They get that Tabor accepts Jesus died (unlike some popular alternative-theory books), but holds that the body was moved — possibly by Mary and the other women.

Where they push back is mostly tonal. Their objection: Tabor says "could be" about Pantera, but spends so much time on it that the suggestion functionally hardens into "sort of is." This is a real critic's point — but it's not a refutation of the underlying historical work. It's an observation about how readers receive carefully hedged scholarly speculation.

### The Pantera Tradition — What Tabor Actually Says

The Ankerberg clip raises the Pantera material. For context, Tabor's actual position (per his linked essay at jamestabor.com) is that the Pantera traditions — including the gravestone of a Roman soldier named Tiberius Julius Abdes Pantera found in Germany, traced back through Sidon — are one possible historical thread for Jesus's biological paternity. **Tabor explicitly says we cannot know whether it was a rape, a willing relationship, or whether the connection is correct at all.** The critics in this clip turn his speculative footnote into the main scandal of the book, which it isn't.

### Hanegraaff's Argument: "The Bible Itself Precludes That"

The caller asks Hanegraaff a real scholarly question: is Tabor right that Paul's letters were written before the gospels, and that the Last Supper "body and blood" formula originates with Paul (1 Corinthians) before showing up in Mark? Hanegraaff's response avoids the question entirely. He doesn't engage the chronology. He doesn't address whether the eucharistic formula in 1 Corinthians 11 predates Mark 14. He simply asserts that Tabor is "unreliable" because he's "driving a wedge" between Paul and the other apostles — and that the Bible itself rules this out.

This is circular: Paul cannot disagree with the other apostles because the Bible (which includes Paul) says they all agreed. It's the same move that flattens Galatians 2's documented confrontation between Paul and Peter into a non-conflict.

### The Drinking-Blood Problem Hanegraaff Doesn't Address

The caller correctly notes: drinking blood is **explicitly forbidden** in Torah (Leviticus 17, Genesis 9). The Jerusalem Council's letter to Gentile believers (Acts 15:20, 29) lists "abstain from blood" as one of only four requirements. So if Jesus actually said "drink my blood" at a Passover meal, he'd be commanding his Jewish disciples to violate a foundational Mosaic prohibition.

Hanegraaff sidesteps this entirely. His response: "When we take communion, we don't drink blood. What we do is remember… the broken body of Jesus Christ, the shed blood of Jesus Christ for the complete remission of sins. So the blood of Jesus Christ is emblematic." This concedes the caller's point — communion *is* symbolic, not literal — while still insisting Tabor is wrong to identify the formula as Pauline innovation.

### The Pre-70 CE Inerrancy Argument

Pressed on the dating question, Hanegraaff makes a stronger and more revealing claim: "All of the Bible including… the book of Revelation counter-Tabor was completed prior to the destruction of Jerusalem or the temple. It would be absolutely inconceivable that the most apocalyptic of Christ's claims would have been fulfilled, the destruction of the temple and Jerusalem, without the biblical writers drawing attention to it."

This is a minority position even among evangelical scholars. Mainstream scholarship dates Mark to ~70 CE, Matthew and Luke to 80-90 CE, John to 90-100 CE, and Revelation to ~95 CE under Domitian. Hanegraaff is committed to pre-70 dating because **the gospel apocalypses (Mark 13, Matthew 24, Luke 21) describe the temple's destruction with too much specificity to be pre-event prophecy** — unless one is committed to that prophecy being supernatural. This is doctrine driving dating, not evidence driving dating.
