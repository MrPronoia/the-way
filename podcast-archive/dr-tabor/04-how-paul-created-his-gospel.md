## Summary

Dr. Tabor reverses the standard scholarly assumption about Paul. The usual story: Paul inherited the basic Christ-message from the Jerusalem apostles (Peter, James, John), then adapted it for Gentiles. Tabor argues the opposite. **Paul created his gospel.** He didn't get it from the Jerusalem pillars — he got it from his own reading of Isaiah's Suffering Servant songs and from direct mystical revelation. He says so himself. Galatians 1:11-12: "I did not receive it from man, nor was I taught it." 1 Corinthians 11:23: the Last Supper words came to him "from the Lord," not from people who were there.

The bombshell follows. Once you accept that Paul created the message — sins-of-the-world atonement, death-burial-third-day-resurrection, substitutionary sacrifice — you have to ask what happens next. Tabor's answer: Mark builds a gospel narrative around Paul's theology. Matthew and Luke follow Mark. John runs his own track but still preaches Paul's Lamb-of-God Jesus. 1 Peter, 1-3 John, Hebrews, even Jude — all Pauline productions. The Q source embedded in Matthew/Luke is the rare non-Pauline survival, but it's framed inside a Markan (and therefore Pauline) container, so most readers never notice it. The Letter of James is the only relatively intact non-Pauline witness in the New Testament — and tellingly, it sounds nothing like Paul. It sounds like Jesus's ethical teaching: honor the poor, hold the faith of Jesus, watch out for the rich.

The implication is enormous. The "Christianity" that comes down through Augustine, the creeds, and the medieval church isn't the religion of Jesus or his Jerusalem followers. It's the religion Paul built from Isaiah 53 and his own visions, packaged in 13 letters, narrativized by Mark, sealed by the Greek-language Book of Revelation, and crowned by Marcion's canon and the church fathers. The original Jewish-Christian movement under James continued in the *Pseudo-Clementine* literature, where its members ask the question that should still be asked: **Why should we trust Paul, who only had visions, over the apostles who walked with Jesus?**

## Key Teachings

### Read the New Testament Backwards

The New Testament's table of contents puts the gospels first, then Acts, then Paul. Chronologically, **Paul's letters are the earliest things in the New Testament.** They were written before Mark, before any gospel. So when you read the documents in canonical order, you're reading them in reverse historical order — and you're reading the gospels through Paul, even though Paul wrote first.

Tabor's claim: Paul's gospel-message permeates Mark. Mark then becomes the source for Matthew, Luke, and Acts. John, while running his own track, is also deeply Pauline. So almost the entire New Testament is "Pauline" — Paul's theology dressed up in narrative form.

### "I Received It" Doesn't Mean "From the Apostles"

The standard reading of 1 Corinthians 15:3 — "for I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received" — is that Paul got the death-burial-resurrection creed from the Jerusalem apostles. The Greek verbs (*paralambanō*, to receive officially; *paradidōmi*, to deliver officially) are the official chain-of-tradition language Jewish teachers used.

But Paul explicitly says where he got it. **Galatians 1:11-12:** "The gospel which was preached by me is not a human gospel. For I did not receive it from man, nor was I taught it, but it came through a revelation of Jesus Christ." **1 Corinthians 11:23:** "I received from the Lord what I also delivered to you" — referring to the Last Supper words. Paul says it came from the Lord, not from people who were at the supper.

So when Paul says "what I received," he means: received by revelation, received from scripture, received from the risen Christ — not received from Peter, James, and John.

### The Whole Gospel Is Isaiah 53

Tabor walks through Isaiah 52:13–53:12 — the fourth Suffering Servant song — and shows that every major beat of Paul's gospel is already there:

- **"My servant will prosper, be exalted and lifted up"** — but first brought "very, very low." This is Paul's "humiliation then exaltation" framework (Philippians 2).
- **"Marred beyond any human semblance"** — the suffering disfigurement.
- **"He will startle the nations; kings shall shut their mouths"** — Paul's mission to the Gentiles is right there.
- **"Despised and rejected, a man of sorrows, acquainted with grief"** — Paul uses this language about himself constantly.
- **"Wounded for our transgressions, bruised for our iniquities"** — substitutionary atonement.
- **"All we like sheep have gone astray"** — Romans 3: "None is righteous, no, not one."
- **"By oppression and judgment he was taken away... cut off from the land of the living"** — death.
- **"They made his grave with the wicked and a rich man in his death"** — burial (Joseph of Arimathea).
- **"He shall prolong his days; he shall see light"** (this last phrase is in the Dead Sea Scrolls Isaiah, not the Masoretic) — resurrection.
- **"My servant shall make many righteous"** — and Mark 10:45 picks up the word "many" directly: "to give his life as a ransom for many."

Tabor's verdict: **"this is clearly where he got it."** The four "thats" of 1 Corinthians 15 (*that* Christ died, *that* he was buried, *that* he was raised, *that* he appeared) are Paul's distillation of Isaiah 53.

### Paul Heard Voices

Paul wasn't only reading scripture. He was also having ecstatic experiences. **2 Corinthians 12:** he was caught up to the third heaven, entered paradise, and heard "things that cannot be told, which man may not utter." Tabor wrote his dissertation on this (*Paul's Ascent to Paradise*, University of Chicago) — Paul's entire theology is shaped by a mystical experience in which he received conversational messages from a heavenly Jesus.

This means Paul's "I received it from the Lord" is not metaphor. He genuinely believed Jesus was dictating things to him. The Last Supper words. The order of the resurrection. The cosmic transformation framework. All of it came (in his mind) directly from a heavenly Christ — not from any human teacher, including the people who actually walked with Jesus.

### The Pseudo-Clementines Asked the Right Question

In the 2nd-3rd centuries, after Jerusalem's destruction, the Jewish followers of Jesus (the *Desposyni* — "the Master's relatives," literally the family of Jesus) continued to hold what they believed was the original faith. They produced literature — the **Pseudo-Clementine correspondence** — in which Peter and Paul (figured as "Simon Magus") debate sharply.

Their question: **Why should we trust Paul — who's receiving visionary experiences and revelations — over the apostles and James who were actually with Jesus in the flesh?**

Paul has an answer (a controversial one): "We no longer know Jesus after the flesh" (2 Cor 5:16). Knowing him "in the flesh" is irrelevant; what matters now is the heavenly Christ. Tabor: Paul never met Jesus face to face. He might have seen him at a distance during the crucifixion (he was in Jerusalem then, by his own account, cheering on the suppression). But he never knew the human Jesus. So his answer to the Jerusalem question is essentially: it doesn't matter that you knew him. I have the heavenly version.

### Mark Builds the Gospel Around Paul

The opening line of Mark — **"The beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God"** — already signals what Mark is doing. The "gospel of Jesus Christ" means both *the good news about Jesus* and *the good news that comes from Jesus*, which is Paul's gospel.

In the structural center of Mark (chapters 8, 9, and 10), **three times** Jesus predicts his suffering, death, and resurrection on the third day. The language is lifted directly from the Servant songs: rejected, mocked, spit upon, scourged, killed, rise the third day. The "third day" comes from Hosea 6 (about Israel's healing — which connects to the Servant songs because Israel is "the servant" 20 times in Isaiah 40-53). The climax of Mark — and of these three predictions — is **Mark 10:45**: "The Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for **many**." The word "many" is the giveaway. It comes straight out of Isaiah 53.

Then the Last Supper in Mark 14: "This is my body... this is my blood." Paul says he got this "from the Lord" in 1 Corinthians 11. Mark turns it into narrative. Matthew gets it from Mark. Luke gets it from Mark.

### Paul's Legacy Is the Whole New Testament

Tabor lays out Paul's reach across the canon:

- **Paul's 13 letters** — the theological core.
- **Mark** — narrativizes Paul's gospel.
- **Matthew & Luke** — built on Mark, so they inherit Paul's framework.
- **Acts** — Luke's continuation, Pauline through and through.
- **John** — different idioms, but presenting Jesus as the "Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world" — direct Pauline development.
- **Hebrews** — some have even argued Paul wrote it (Tabor disagrees) — but it's a "monument to Pauline theology."
- **1 Peter** — "such a Pauline production in the name of Peter," Tabor says.
- **1-3 John** — also Pauline.
- **Jude** — partly Pauline (though Jude was Jesus's brother).
- **The Greek Book of Revelation** — Pauline, though Tabor argues there's a *pre-Christian* version underneath without Pauline content.

The Apostolic Fathers, the early church fathers, Augustine — the whole package down through the centuries. **"Signed, sealed, delivered, packaged with a big ribbon — that's Christianity. It's Paul's legacy."**

### What Survived That Isn't Paul

Two surviving witnesses to the original (pre-Pauline) movement:

1. **The Q source** — a sayings collection embedded in Matthew and Luke (the Sermon on the Mount material, the wisdom sayings). Q doesn't glorify Paul's ideas at all. But because Q is embedded inside Matthew and Luke, which are built on Mark, which is built on Paul — most readers absorb Q as if it's Pauline too.

2. **The Letter of James** — Tabor's strongest argument for what the Jerusalem movement actually sounded like. James mentions Jesus only twice in the entire letter (both in greeting/passing). It's addressed to "the synagogue" (not the church). It tells believers to hold the "faith of Jesus" — *his* faith, not faith *in* him. It emphasizes honoring the poor, not the rich. **It reads like the ethical teaching of Jesus in Q.** Tabor: "this is amazing... I think this is our only reliable remnant of what the movement was like before 70 in Jerusalem led by James, Peter, and John."

If you want to know what the original followers of Jesus believed — before Paul built his gospel out of Isaiah 53 — read the Letter of James and read Q. Don't read Romans, don't read 1 Corinthians 15, and don't read the gospels as they stand now.

### Substitutionary Atonement Started with Paul

The heart-and-soul doctrine of Christianity — that Jesus took our sins on himself, that he was "made to be sin who knew no sin" (2 Cor 5:21), that God "so loved the world that he gave his only Son" — Tabor is **convinced this started with Paul.** Not with Jesus. Not with the Jerusalem apostles.

"People would say, well Jesus revealed it to him. But most interpreters have said no, it goes all the way back to Jesus himself. I'm suggesting to you that it starts with Paul."

The mechanism: Paul read Isaiah 53's servant — "wounded for our transgressions" — and identified the servant as Jesus. From that identification flowed the entire atonement theology. The early followers of Jesus, Tabor argues, thought Jesus was the Messiah, thought he had been exalted to heaven — but they didn't have the substitutionary-blood framework. Paul invented it from his reading of Isaiah.
