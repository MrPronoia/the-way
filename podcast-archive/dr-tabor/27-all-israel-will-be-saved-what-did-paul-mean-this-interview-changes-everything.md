## Summary

This is the conversation Tabor calls "the most significant interview I have ever recorded." His guest, Jason Staples, has written two Cambridge University Press books arguing that nearly everyone — scholars included — has misunderstood what Paul means when he uses the word **Israel**. Staples' thesis: when Paul says "all Israel will be saved" in Romans 11, he is not talking about ethnic Jews coming to faith in Christ at the end of time, nor about a "spiritual Israel" of all believers, nor about supersessionist replacement. He is talking about the **literal regathering of the scattered northern tribes of Israel** — the ten tribes lost after the Assyrian deportation in the 8th century BCE — through the Gentile mission. The "fullness of the Gentiles" is the mechanism by which scattered Israel, assimilated into the nations, gets reincorporated into the covenant people.

Staples works from a tight philological argument. The terms **"Jew" (Ioudaios)** and **"Israelite"** are not coextensive in second-temple usage. They are not synonyms with different audiences. They refer to two different (overlapping) groups: Jews are the subset descended from the southern kingdom of Judah, the only kingdom that survived; Israelites are the larger covenantal twelve-tribe people, most of whom were divorced by God (Hosea 1-2) and scattered among the nations after the Northern Kingdom's fall. The biblical prophets — Hosea especially, but also Ezekiel 36, Jeremiah 31, Joel 2, and the Song of Moses (Deuteronomy 32) — promise that one day God will say "to those who are not my people, you are my people" and bring scattered Israel back. Staples argues Paul reads this prophetic substructure and concludes: the uncircumcised Gentiles receiving the Spirit *are* scattered Israel coming home. They are being **ethnically transformed** into restored Israel by receiving the Spirit promised to Israel.

The interview also tells the origin story of how Staples arrived at the thesis as a first-year grad student at UNC under Bart Ehrman — who initially thought it sounded like "the work of an autodidact" and sent him to Duke to be talked out of it by Joel Marcus, Douglas Campbell, and Richard Hayes. Instead, Staples' 2008 SBL paper drew a standing-room crowd, and Pauline scholar Robert Jewett pulled him into a book stall to say, "I've been working on this question for 30 years and I think you've solved it." Tabor uses the conversation to promote the upcoming Bart Ehrman "New Insights in the New Testament" virtual conference (Sept 21-22, 2024) where Staples will lecture on "Not by Faith Alone — Paul's Gospel Wasn't What You Think." The conversation closes with Staples previewing that talk: Paul's gospel is not about God overlooking sin because Jesus took it — it is about **transformation by the Spirit** so that, at the final judgment, the believer is judged justly *as just*.

## Key Teachings

### Israel and Jew Are Not Synonyms

The keystone of Staples' argument. The standard scholarly view since a 1937-38 article by Karl Georg Kuhn (a German Nazi-era scholar — one of the first thousand to join the SA) holds that "Jew" and "Israelite" are coextensive: same people, different registers. Insider language = Israelite. Outsider language = Jew, sometimes derogatory.

Staples shows this collapses on contact with the data. In Philo of Alexandria, certain tractates use "Israel/Israelite" and never "Jew"; others use "Jew" and never "Israelite." When both appear in the same tractate, they are separated by hundreds of lines — Philo has changed subjects. Josephus shows a similar split. The terms are not stylistic variants. They refer to **different (overlapping) groups** based on what the author is talking about. Kuhn's "depreciatory sense" thesis — that "Jew" was a slur on outsider lips — cites zero ancient examples. The bias was Kuhn's own Weimar-era projection, where Berlin's Jewish community had already started calling themselves "Israelite" because "Juden" was being weaponized.

Tabor's framing: this isn't just a footnote correction. The entire modern theological edifice of "Israel = Jews = God's people" rests on a category error inherited from a 1930s German Nazi.

### The Two-Kingdom Backstory Paul Presumes

Staples reconstructs the framework every first-century hearer would have brought to the word "Israel":

- **United Israel** under David and Solomon = twelve tribes.
- **Northern Kingdom of Israel** (10 tribes, led by Ephraim/Joseph) splits off after Solomon.
- **Southern Kingdom of Judah** (Judah + Benjamin + much of Levi) keeps Jerusalem and the Temple.
- **8th century BCE:** Assyria deports the Northern Kingdom. Scattered. Gone. Assimilated into the nations.
- **6th century BCE:** Babylon exiles Judah. Some return after 70 years. These returnees become **"Jews"** — people of the kingdom of Judah.

After the deportations, "Jews" = the surviving identifiable group from the southern kingdom. "Israel" = the larger twelve-tribe covenantal people, most of whom are now lost — scattered, assimilated, no longer recognizable as Israelite. The biblical prophets (and the synoptic gospels picking it up) promise God will one day **regather all twelve tribes**. When Jesus says the disciples will sit on twelve thrones judging the twelve tribes (Matt 19:28 / Luke 22:30), it is not boilerplate — it is a staggering claim. Reuben hasn't been seen in 700 years. Gad? Manasseh? Where are they? Jesus is announcing they're coming back.

### Hosea 1-2 — The Divorce and the Return

The exegetical pivot. Hosea prophesies to the *Northern* Kingdom of Israel — *not* to Judah. God says through Hosea: "You are not my people, and I am not your God" (Hosea 1:9). This is divorce language — the inverse of the covenantal "I will be your God and you will be my people." Jeremiah later refers to this as God giving "your sister Israel a writ of divorce." Israel is scattered, cast off, no longer in covenant.

**Critical point Staples emphasizes:** No Jewish interpreter, ancient or modern, has ever read Hosea 1-2 as applying to Jews. It is universally understood to apply to the Northern Kingdom — to scattered Israel, not to Judah. Judah was *not* divorced.

But Hosea also says: "I will say to those who are not my people, 'You are my people'" (Hosea 2:23). One day God will bring scattered Israel back. **Paul reads this and finds his framework.** He looks around at uncircumcised Gentiles receiving the Spirit — the Spirit promised by Ezekiel 36, Jeremiah 31, Joel 2 to a restored Israel — and concludes: *this is Hosea coming true*. The Gentiles being filled with the Spirit are scattered Israel coming home, ethnically transformed back into the covenant people through the Spirit of Israel's Messiah.

### Romans 11:25-26 — "The Fullness of the Gentiles" Decoded

This is the verse Staples wrote his dissertation on: "A hardening has come upon part of Israel until the fullness of the Gentiles come in, and so all Israel will be saved."

The standard readings, all of which Staples rejects:
- **Dispensational:** end-times ethnic Jewish conversion.
- **Supersessionist:** the Church replaces Israel.
- **Spiritual:** "Israel" = all believers regardless of ethnicity.

Staples' reading: **"The fullness of the Gentiles"** is a deliberate echo of Genesis 48:19, where Jacob blesses Joseph's son Ephraim with the promise that his seed will become "the fullness of the nations" (Hebrew *melo ha-goyim*; Greek LXX *plēroma tōn ethnōn*). Ephraim — the dominant tribe of the Northern Kingdom that got scattered — was promised that his descendants would become the *fullness of the nations*. Paul is saying: Ephraim's prophecy is now coming true. Scattered Israel, assimilated into the nations, is becoming the "fullness of the Gentiles" coming back in. *And in this way* (Greek *houtōs*, not "then" — a manner clause, not a temporal one) **all Israel** — the full twelve-tribe people, Judah-Jews plus regathered Northern Israelites in Gentile bodies — **will be saved**.

### Ethnic Transformation, Not Spiritualization

Staples is emphatic on a counterintuitive point: Paul is *not* saying "Gentiles become Israel spiritually while staying Gentile ethnically." Paul thinks something more literal is happening. Gentiles who receive the Spirit are being **ethnically transformed** — incorporated into the body of Christ in a quasi-material sense, made into Israelites by participation in Israel's Messiah.

Evidence: in 1 Corinthians 10, Paul calls the Exodus generation "**our** fathers" — addressing a mixed Jewish-Gentile congregation in Corinth. The Gentiles' patriarchs are now Abraham, Isaac, Jacob. Their story is now the Exodus. They start reading Torah (in Greek, since most don't know Hebrew). The transformation is not metaphor — it is genealogical incorporation through the Spirit.

Staples doesn't argue Paul thinks each Gentile convert has literal Israelite DNA buried in their ancestry. (He suspects 1 Timothy's warning against "endless genealogies" pushes back against converts who tried to find such ancestry.) The argument is closer to this: Israel got so assimilated into the nations through intermarriage that God's solution is to bring *the whole nations* in. Some of them are literal scattered Israel; others are absorbed alongside. The result is the same — all Israel restored, accompanied by the nations being drawn in, exactly as the prophets foretold (Isaiah 2, Micah 4 — the nations stream to Zion).

### Romans 2:28-29 — "Real Circumcision Is of the Heart"

Tabor raises this verse as a test case. Is Paul saying Gentiles become Jews here? Staples reads it more conservatively: Paul is doing what observant Jews of his day routinely did — saying that **possession of Torah without obedience to Torah** doesn't save you. The verse is not redefining who is a Jew. It is saying that a Jew who breaks Torah forfeits the privileges of circumcision; a Gentile who keeps Torah's righteousness has done what God required. This is mainstream Jewish ethics, not Pauline novelty.

The reason Paul *switches* from "Jew" language (Romans 2) to "Israel" language (Romans 9-11) is precisely Staples' thesis: in Romans 2 Paul is talking about the subset (Judah-derived Jews). In Romans 9-11 he expands to the larger covenant people. Different vocabulary because different referent.

### The Origin Story — How Staples Convinced Bart Ehrman

A revealing aside about how heterodox academic ideas survive. Late 2007: Staples, a first-year UNC grad student TA-ing for Bart Ehrman, mentions in a five-minute walk to lecture that he already knows his dissertation thesis — and lays out the Romans 9-11 / scattered-Israel argument. Ehrman's response: "How many classes on Paul have you taken?" Staples: "None." Ehrman: "Yeah, this sounds like the work of an autodidact. Go take some classes at Duke with Joel Marcus, Douglas Campbell, and Richard Hayes — they'll explain why this doesn't work."

Staples instead submitted the argument as a paper to SBL 2008 — without running it past his advisor first (he didn't know the protocol). Standing-room crowd. The next day Pauline scholar **Robert Jewett** cornered him at the book exhibit, shook his hand, and said: "I've been working on this question for 30 years and I think you've solved it. The thing that impacted me most is you were willing to follow the data wherever it went, whereas the rest of us just stopped short." Jewett offered to be an outside reader and called Ehrman within 24 hours saying the dissertation absolutely needed to happen. The next day at Ehrman's reception, a fellow student turned to Staples: "You presented something he didn't know about?" Ehrman: "Look up 'anomaly' in the dictionary — you'll find his picture."

The 2011 *JBL* article that came out of that paper is the published seed of *The Idea of Israel in Second Temple Judaism* (Cambridge, 2021) and *Paul and the Resurrection of Israel* (Cambridge, 2024).

### Not by Faith Alone — Paul's Gospel as Transformation, Not Imputation

Staples previews his upcoming Ehrman conference talk: the phrase "by faith alone" appears exactly **one** time in the entire New Testament — and it is **preceded by the word "not"** (James 2:24: "not by faith alone"). Luther added "alone" to Romans in his German translation. The textual case for *sola fide* as the Pauline gospel is essentially nonexistent.

What Paul actually preaches, on Staples' reading: God is just. People will be judged justly. We deserve a bad judgment. The question is not "how can God overlook our sin?" but "how can people actually become just?" Paul's answer: **the Spirit transforms you** so that you become an actual doer of justice. At the final judgment, you are judged justly *as just* — not because Jesus stood in your place and God overlooked you, but because by the Spirit you have actually become someone who lives righteously. Salvation as transformation, not as forensic substitution.

This puts Paul much closer to the prophets, to James, and to traditional Jewish ethics than the Lutheran tradition can comfortably allow.
