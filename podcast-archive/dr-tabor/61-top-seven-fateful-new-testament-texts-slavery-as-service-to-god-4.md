## Summary

This is the fourth installment of Tabor's "Seven Fateful New Testament Texts" series — videos that bracket what a verse *originally meant* and focus instead on **how the verse has been pulled out of context, applied universally, and used to cause real harm across two thousand years**. The fateful cluster here: the New Testament's "**slaves obey your masters**" passages — 1 Corinthians 7, Ephesians 6, Colossians 3, Titus 2, 1 Peter 2, and the entire letter of Philemon. The provocative twist Tabor surfaces from the title: these texts do not merely tell slaves to be obedient. They tell slaves they are **actually serving God** when they obey their masters — that the slavery itself is a form of devotion to Christ. That theological move — slavery reframed as Christian service — is the engine that made these verses so devastatingly weaponizable across 400 years of the transatlantic slave trade and the American institution it built.

Tabor recommends two books up front. First, *Enslaved: The Sunken History of the Transatlantic Slave Trade* by filmmaker Simcha Jacobovici and Sean Kingsley, drawn from the six-part 2020 documentary Jacobovici made with Samuel L. Jackson. Second — and the one Tabor frames as freshly Monumental — Candida Moss's *God's Ghostwriters: Enslaved Christians and the Making of the Bible*, which reopens settled assumptions about who actually wrote the New Testament and what role enslaved scribes played in producing it. Tabor then walks through each fateful text in chronological order: starting with the **authentic Paul** of 1 Corinthians 7 (slaves stay slaves, "but if you can gain your freedom, avail yourself of the opportunity"), through the **deeply ambiguous letter to Philemon** (Paul sends a runaway slave back to his Christian master with a heavy-handed appeal but no clear demand for emancipation), and into the **deutero-Pauline hardening** in Ephesians, Colossians, Titus, and 1 Peter where slaves are told to obey "with fear and trembling," to serve as if serving Christ Himself, and to accept beatings even when unjust as participation in Christ's suffering.

The mechanism is the same as in the earlier "Fateful Texts" videos: Paul's social ethics ride on his **apocalyptic timeline**. He says it three times in 1 Corinthians 7 — "the present distress," "the appointed time has grown very short," "the form of this world is passing away." Paul thinks the end is five to ten years out. In the **interim** between Jesus's death and his imminent return, all worldly categories — slave/free, male/female, Jew/Gentile — are dissolving but not yet dissolved. Galatians 3:28 declares them gone in Christ. So Paul tells slaves to stay slaves, but to consider themselves "free in the Lord," and tells masters to consider themselves "slaves of Christ." Already, not yet. The problem: **the end didn't come**. The emergency interim ethics — slaves stay slaves, accept beatings, your suffering is service to God — became the eternal doctrine of the church. American slave owners then weaponized those exact verses against the people they were beating in the fields. Tabor's verdict, quoting his video description: "the continued adherence to the ideas long after the apocalypse failed to come about is **evil — plain and simple**."

## Key Teachings

### The Fateful Twist — Slavery Reframed as Service to God

Tabor's central insight, telegraphed in the provocative title: the New Testament's slavery texts go beyond ordinary obedience. They tell the slave that **his slavery is service to God**. Ephesians 6:5-7: "Slaves be obedient to those who are your earthly masters with fear and trembling and singleness of heart, **as to Christ**... rendering service with a good will **as to the Lord and not to men**." Colossians 3:23: "Whatever your task as a slave, **work heartily as serving the Lord and not men**." Titus 2:9-10: bid slaves to be submissive "so that in everything they may **adorn the doctrine of God our Savior**." 1 Peter 2: even when beaten unjustly, your endurance has **God's approval**, because Christ also suffered, "leaving you an example that you should follow in his steps." The theological move: slavery becomes a vehicle for Christlike suffering and obedience. The cruelty of the master becomes an occasion for the slave's spiritual growth. **This is the part that makes these passages exceptionally weaponizable** — they don't just tolerate slavery, they sanctify it. American slave owners did not have to twist the text to defend their institution. They could quote it directly.

### Paul's Authentic Position — 1 Corinthians 7

The earliest layer is genuine Paul, writing in the early 50s CE. Paul takes up slavery in the same chapter where he addresses celibacy and marriage — and the logic is identical: stay where you are, the end is coming. "Were you a slave when called? Never mind. But if you can gain your freedom, avail yourself of the opportunity. For he who was called in the Lord as a slave is a freedman of the Lord. Likewise he who was free when called is a slave of Christ" (1 Cor 7:21-22). Tabor's read: Paul flips the categories. The slave who answers the gospel is **already free in the Lord**. The free master who answers the gospel is **already a slave of Christ**. In Christ the distinctions are dissolving. But in the meantime, you stay in the social position you're in. Why? Because Paul says it three times in this chapter: the **present distress**, the **appointed time has grown very short**, the **form of this world is passing away**. Paul is not building a permanent social ethic. He is running an emergency holding pattern for a movement he thinks will be transformed within a decade.

### The Philemon Ambiguity

The shortest book in the New Testament — Paul's personal letter to a slave owner named **Philemon** about a runaway slave named **Onesimus** (Greek for "useful," and Paul plays on the name). Onesimus has fled his master, encountered Paul in prison, become a Christian, and now serves Paul. Paul is sending him back. The letter is a masterclass in Paul's signature back-and-forth psychology: *"I would have been glad to keep him with me... but I prefer to do nothing without your consent, so that your goodness would not be by compulsion but of your own free will... I appeal to you for my child Onesimus, whose father I have become in my imprisonment."* Tabor: this is the exact same rhetorical move Paul uses on celibacy — "I'm not commanding you, but I know you'll do the right thing, and the right thing is what I want." But **the text never explicitly demands Onesimus's emancipation**. Paul wants him back to serve Paul. He wants Philemon to receive him "no longer as a slave but more than a slave, as a beloved brother." Whether that means **legally freeing him** or just **treating him kindly while still owning him** is genuinely ambiguous — and that ambiguity has been the wedge defenders of slavery used for centuries. If even Paul, with a chance to demand emancipation directly, didn't demand it — then surely Christian masters are within their rights to keep their slaves.

### The Deutero-Pauline Hardening — Ephesians, Colossians, Titus

Tabor — like most critical scholars — treats Ephesians, Colossians, and the Pastoral Epistles (1 & 2 Timothy, Titus) as **not authentically Pauline**. They contain some Pauline material but were written by later admirers in Paul's name, extending his thought into permanent church discipline. The slavery instructions in these letters are where the doctrine hardens. The apocalyptic urgency that grounded Paul's interim ethics in 1 Corinthians 7 has **faded**. The instructions are now framed as permanent Christian ethics:

- **Ephesians 6:5-9** — Slaves obey "with fear and trembling and singleness of heart, as to Christ." Masters told to "do the same to them" and not threaten, knowing the heavenly master is impartial. *No demand for emancipation.* Just better treatment.
- **Colossians 3:22-25** — Slaves obey "in everything," work as "serving the Lord." Wrongdoers will be paid back, no partiality. *No demand for emancipation.* Just appeal to divine justice in the eschaton.
- **Titus 2:9-10** — Slaves submit, don't answer back, don't pilfer, show "entire and true fidelity" so they may "adorn the doctrine of God our Savior." Slavery is now decoration for the gospel.
- **1 Peter 2:18-21** — Submit even to overbearing masters. Endure beatings patiently. "What credit is it if when you do wrong and are beaten you take it patiently? But if when you do right and suffer for it you take it patiently, you have God's approval... for to this you have been called, because Christ also suffered for you."

Tabor pauses on that 1 Peter passage. The word for "beaten" is literal corporal punishment — and the writer is telling the slave to **take it patiently, because Christ suffered too**. The slave's beating becomes a participation in Christ's suffering. This is the most devastating piece of the cluster. It is the verse that has been quoted to enslaved people in chapels in Mississippi to keep them from running away.

### Paul's "Already, Not Yet" — The Apocalyptic Engine

This is the load-bearing concept Tabor returns to from his earlier videos. Paul lives in what Albert Schweitzer called **the interim** — the period between Jesus's death and his expected return (the *parousia*). In Christ the new age has dawned: Galatians 3:28 declares "neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male nor female" in the new creation. But the old age persists until the parousia arrives. Paul's interim ethics:

- **Slaves stay slaves** — but you're free in the Lord
- **Masters stay masters** — but you're a slave of Christ
- **Married stay married, single stay single** — but consider both states as transitional
- **Jews keep Torah, Gentiles don't** — but in Christ the distinction dissolves

The whole chapter of 1 Corinthians 7 is one long application of this logic. Paul thinks Jesus is coming back **within five to ten years**. The old social order is fading in front of his eyes. He doesn't need to restructure earthly society because the cosmos itself is about to be restructured. **The problem**: the end didn't come. Two thousand years later, the interim ethics are still being enforced as eternal doctrine. The emergency holding pattern became Christianity's social architecture.

### How American Slave Owners Used These Texts

Tabor names the historical consequence plainly. Over the past 500 years, "the institution of slavery and slave owners have defended it by quoting the Bible." The transatlantic slave trade — 400 years of it, documented in Jacobovici's *Enslaved* — was sustained in part by Christian masters who could point to Ephesians 6, Colossians 3, Titus 2, 1 Peter 2, and Philemon and say: **the Bible never tells slaves to be free; the Bible tells slaves to obey, to serve their masters as serving Christ, and to endure beatings as participation in Christ's suffering**. Slave catechisms were written. Sermons were preached. The verses were quoted at the whipping post. And the theological move that made it work — that the slavery itself was *service to God* — came directly from the New Testament's text. The fateful consequence: untold suffering and misery on a scale Tabor refuses to calculate, and a Christianity whose moral credibility took centuries to begin recovering from what it allowed itself to sanctify.

### Tabor's Verdict on the Continued Adherence

This is the sharpest line Tabor draws. He is willing to *contextualize* Paul. Paul thought the end was near. He was running an emergency holding pattern. His interim instructions had a built-in expiration date. **But the church kept enforcing the interim rules after the rationale had collapsed.** That, in Tabor's words from the video description, is "evil — plain and simple." Two thousand years of human beings owned, beaten, worked to death, raped, and torn from their families — and Christian masters quoting Pauline verses to justify all of it. The originating apocalyptic frame was lost. The verses remained. And the harm compounded across centuries. Tabor's diagnostic question: once you know that Paul's "stay a slave" was tied to a five-year apocalyptic countdown that never arrived, what justification can the church offer for two thousand years of using those same verses to keep people in bondage? **None.** That is what makes the text *fateful*.
