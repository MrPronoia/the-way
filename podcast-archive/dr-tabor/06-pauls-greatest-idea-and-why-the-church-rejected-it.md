## Summary

This is Tabor's most ambitious solo lecture on Paul — a single sustained argument that what Paul himself would have considered his **greatest idea** was systematically dismantled by the church bearing his name. The idea: **cosmic apotheosis**. Salvation, for Paul, is not forgiveness-of-sins-so-you-can-go-to-heaven. It is the literal transformation of dust beings (Adams) into a new genus of spiritual beings — glorified sons of God — beginning with Jesus as firstborn and culminating with the entire cosmos itself being birthed into its perfected state. Justification by faith is only stage one. The real point is **glorification**, a word Tabor traces through Romans 8, 1 Corinthians 15, 2 Corinthians 3, Philippians 2-3, and 1 Thessalonians like a tracer running through Paul's whole corpus.

Tabor opens with his teacher Jonathan Z. Smith, the University of Chicago historian of religions whose Hellenistic-religion framework cracked Tabor's "cosmic egg" in the 1970s. Smith distinguished two religious modes in the ancient world: the **locative** (older, conformity-to-place, "the earth is the right place for love") and the **utopian** (Hellenistic, escape-from-the-world, salvation-as-liberation). Most Hellenistic salvation religions go utopian — get the soul out of this evil prison and ascend. Paul does something stranger. He has the cosmic-escape vocabulary of Hellenistic religion, but he is still standing in Genesis 1-3. The dust creature is good. The world is good. What's needed isn't escape — it's **transformation**. The whole cosmos in labor pains (Romans 8), birthing the many sons of God. Jesus as the engineering prototype — a dust mockup transformed by God into a "life-giving spirit" — proves it can be done. The followers are a beachhead. Jesus is D-Day; the rest of the cosmos is the campaign that follows.

The juicy historical question Tabor presses: why did this get rejected? Because the church needed Jesus to be the **one and only** begotten Son, not the first of many. As soon as you grant Paul's framework, you have a god-family, a new genus of glorified beings, humans becoming "above the angels," judging angels — a vision so close to Mormonism that Tabor notes the LDS tradition actually drew on this directly. To preserve the unique deity of Jesus and the eventual Trinity, the church had to flatten Paul's Adam-Christology (Jesus as a human transformed) into incarnation-Christology (Jesus as a pre-existent divine being who descended). Philippians 2 — usually read as proof of pre-existence — Tabor reads as **Adam Christology**: Jesus was in the form of God (so was Adam, Genesis 1:26-27) but unlike Adam he did not grasp at equality with God; therefore God exalted him. The lecture closes with what happened when Paul's apocalyptic timeline failed: the horizontal hope (this-age-to-next-age cosmic transformation) flipped vertical (souls-go-to-heaven), and Paul's actual gospel became unrecognizable in the religion built on his name.

This is one of those Tabor lectures where the throughline matters more than any individual point. The cumulative argument is: **the church kept Paul as scripture and rejected Paul as theologian.** What modern Christianity preaches as "Pauline soteriology" is Paul filtered through Augustine, Luther, and 1700 years of incarnational orthodoxy. Paul's actual gospel was a Jewish apocalyptic cosmic-transformation framework that almost no contemporary pulpit could deliver without sounding like science fiction or LDS theology.

## Key Teachings

### Jonathan Z. Smith and the Locative vs. Utopian Framework

Tabor opens by crediting his Chicago teacher Jonathan Z. Smith (1938-2017) as the most influential thinker in his intellectual life. Smith's framework for understanding ancient religion divides the Mediterranean world into two modes:

- **Locative** — the older view (Mesopotamian, archaic Greek). The cosmos is a network of relationships; each being has its place. Gilgamesh is told give up the quest for immortality — you are dust, be content. Robert Frost: "Earth's the right place for love."
- **Utopian** — Hellenistic period (~300 BCE onward). The cosmos is a prison ruled by hostile powers, the seven planetary spheres, despotic archons. Salvation = escape. "Strangers in a strange land." Gnostic question (Theodotus): "Who were we? What have we become? Where were we? Whither have we been thrown? From what are we redeemed?"

This shift toward utopian/salvation religion frames everything Paul does. The standard Hellenistic answer is **escape**. Paul does something weirder.

### Paul Has Hellenistic Vocabulary But a Hebrew Bible Framework

Tabor's key tension: Paul talks like a Hellenistic salvation religion (deliverance, escape from "elemental spirits," ascent, transformation, glorification) **but** he is grounded in Genesis 1-3, where the dust creature is good and the cosmos itself is destined for redemption. This makes Paul almost unique. He's not Plato (escape the body), not the Buddha (escape the cycle), not Cicero or Philo. He's a Jewish apocalyptic mystic with a cosmic-Genesis backstop.

The result: Paul wants the **transformation of the cosmos**, not its abandonment. The world isn't a prison to flee. It's a womb in labor (Romans 8) bringing forth the new race of glorified humans.

### Three Stages of Cosmic Salvation

Tabor lays out Paul's three-stage map (most explicit in 1 Corinthians 15):

1. **Stage 1: Christ as firstfruits** — already happened. Jesus, a Davidic Jewish human, was transformed at resurrection into the prototype "life-giving spirit," exalted to God's right hand. He is the engineering mockup proving it can be done.
2. **Stage 2: Those who belong to Christ at his parousia** — the beachhead. A select group (Paul's congregations) will be transformed "in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trumpet" when Jesus returns. Living and dead together undergo metamorphosis from dust body to glorified spiritual body.
3. **Stage 3: The end (telos)** — Christ subdues every rule, authority, and power. The last enemy destroyed is death. Then "God will be all in all" (1 Cor 15:28). Possibly universalist — every Adam of the cosmos eventually redeemed.

This isn't "Christ died for your sins so you can go to heaven." It's "Christ is the firstborn of a new genus of cosmic beings, and you're being born next."

### Glorification Is Paul's Actual Gospel

Tabor underlines the word **glory / glorification** as the throughline of Paul. Texts (all RSV, all from undisputed letters):

- Romans 8:30 — "Those whom he justified he also **glorified**"
- Romans 8:18 — "The sufferings of this present time are not worth comparing with the **glory** that is to be revealed to us"
- Romans 8:21 — Creation will obtain "the **glorious** liberty of the children of God"
- 1 Corinthians 2:7 — "God decreed before the ages for our **glorification**"
- 2 Corinthians 3:18 — "Being changed into his likeness from one degree of **glory** to another"
- Philippians 3:21 — "Will change our lowly body to be like his **glorious** body"
- Colossians (deutero) 1:27 / Romans 5:2 — "Hope of sharing the **glory** of God"

Justification (forgiveness of sins) is real but instrumental. You are justified **for the purpose of glorification**. The end is not pardon. The end is participation in the glory of God as a transformed cosmic being above the angels.

### The Three Views of Paul (Tabor's Taxonomy)

Tabor situates his own reading among the three major scholarly approaches:

1. **Juristic (Luther, Augustine)** — Paul is about how guilty sinners get their sins forgiven. Justification by faith is the center.
2. **Mystical / Participatory (Schweitzer, E.P. Sanders)** — Paul is about being "in Christ," cosmic initiation into the body of Christ. The center is union, not pardon.
3. **Cosmic / Apocalyptic (Jonathan Z. Smith, Morton Smith, Tabor)** — Paul is about the salvation of the entire cosmos. Individuals get caught up in a larger cosmic transformation. The center is the birthing of a new creation.

Tabor sits firmly in #3 while crediting Schweitzer's *The Mysticism of the Apostle Paul* as the gateway. Note: Schweitzer's mystical Paul is "in Christ" is a stepping stone — but Schweitzer didn't quite go all the way to cosmic apotheosis.

### Jesus Did Not Pre-Exist (in the Seven Genuine Letters)

A bold claim Tabor defends from the text. In the seven undisputed letters (1 Thessalonians, Galatians, 1 & 2 Corinthians, Romans, Philemon, Philippians) Tabor argues there is **no evidence Jesus pre-existed as a divine being**.

The key text people cite — **Philippians 2:5-11** — Tabor reads as **Adam Christology**, not pre-existent Christology:

- "Being in the form (morphe) of God" — so was Adam (Genesis 1:26-27, made in God's image).
- "Did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped" — Adam **did** grasp at equality (the Forbidden Tree: "you will be as gods"). Jesus, the second Adam, did not.
- "Emptied himself, took the form of a servant" — Jesus chose the path Adam refused: humility instead of grasping.
- "Therefore God highly exalted him" — God's response to Jesus's faithfulness.

Reading: Jesus is a human in Adam's situation who chooses correctly and is therefore exalted. Not a divine being descending and re-ascending. Tabor points to James Dunn's *Christology in the Making* and Charles Talbert as scholarly company on this reading.

Romans 1:3-4 confirms it: Jesus "descended from David according to the flesh and designated (horisthentos) Son of God in power by the resurrection from the dead." He **becomes** Son of God in power — at resurrection. He wasn't an eternally pre-existent divine being.

### Resurrection Is Metamorphosis, Not Resuscitation

Tabor returns to a point central across his entire Paul corpus: Paul's resurrection language has nothing to do with corpses being reanimated. The Greek word is **anastasis** (standing up). The category is **transformation**:

- Sown perishable, raised imperishable
- Sown in dishonor, raised in glory
- Sown in weakness, raised in power
- Sown a physical body (soma psychikos), raised a spiritual body (soma pneumatikos)

Paul uses metaphors: a seed dies and a plant grows (1 Cor 15:36-38); a tent gets torn down and a permanent dwelling replaces it (2 Cor 5:1-5); a caterpillar enters a cocoon and emerges as a butterfly (Tabor's gloss, not Paul's, but the underlying Greek metamorphoo is the same root).

The point: God does not care about bones, dust, cremated ashes, or what's in the ossuary. The body that comes up is a **new body** — not a refurbished version of the old one. "Flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God" (1 Cor 15:50). Tabor: "Paul is the only first-person witness, and he says 'I can't tell you what kind of body.'"

This is why the gospels' resurrection scenes (Thomas touching wounds, Jesus eating fish, "a ghost does not have flesh and bones as I have") **are not Paul's view**. Those are resuscitations. Paul's resurrected Jesus is a star being, a glorified spirit body that appeared to Paul in blinding light.

### The Apocalyptic Timeline Driving Everything

Why does Paul tell people not to marry, slaves to stay slaves, women to stay subordinate, the rich to live as if they had no goods? Because "the appointed time has grown very short" (1 Cor 7:29) and "the form (schema) of this world is passing away" (1 Cor 7:31).

Tabor reconstructs the clock Paul was reading:

- **41 CE** — Caligula tries to install his statue in the Jerusalem Temple. For Paul this nearly fulfills Daniel's "abomination of desolation" — the apocalyptic clock has hit midnight. It didn't happen, but it proves the prophecy could land.
- **Daniel 11-12** — Paul reads the last verses of Daniel 11 (from v. 40 on) as describing his own present moment. The model is Antiochus Epiphanes (165 BCE), but Paul expects a replay any day.
- **50s CE** — Paul writes 1 Corinthians believing 5-10 years (maybe less) until parousia. Possibly under Nero's early reign.

Every social instruction Paul gives is calibrated to this clock. The neither-male-nor-female / slave-free / Jew-Greek erasure in Galatians 3:28 isn't egalitarianism for its own sake — it's "these distinctions are passing away with this age." Whether Paul would have written any of it the same way knowing the church would last 2000 years is the great open question.

### Why the Church Rejected Paul's Adam Christology

This is the heart of the lecture. Tabor's answer to "why discarded?":

Paul's framework requires Jesus to be the **firstborn of many** — the first instance of a new genus of glorified humans-becoming-gods. As soon as you grant that, you have:

- Humans becoming "above the angels" (1 Cor 6:3 — "Do you not know that we will judge angels?")
- A god-family with multiple sons of God, Jesus being one (the first) among many
- A theology that sounds, as Tabor admits, **a lot like Mormonism**

The church's developing Trinitarian orthodoxy (4th century onward) couldn't tolerate this. Jesus needed to be the **one and only** begotten Son, eternally pre-existent, of one substance with the Father. The early debates with the Arians ("was there a time when he was not?") were a rear-guard action against the natural reading of Paul's Adam Christology.

So the church kept Paul's letters in the canon but read them through a filter that flattened the Adam-Christology into incarnation-Christology, the cosmic-glorification into individual-soul-going-to-heaven, and the genus-of-glorified-humans into "Jesus is God; we just get to be with him forever."

The result: the religion called "Pauline Christianity" preaches almost nothing of what Paul actually taught.

### What Happened When Paul's Apocalyptic Failed

The lecture's final move. Paul's expectations all failed. The end didn't come in 5 years, or 10, or 50. Jerusalem fell in 70 CE; no parousia. The whole cosmic-takeover timetable collapsed.

What did Christianity do? It flipped Paul's **horizontal** framework (this age → age to come, cosmic transformation in history) into a **vertical** framework (souls go up to heaven when they die). Hellenistic utopianism reasserted itself. The Christian funeral became "the departed has gone to be with Christ" — which Tabor argues is a misreading of Philippians 1:21-23, where Paul is actually wrestling with whether to stay alive for the brethren or "depart" (which Tabor argues means die-and-wait-asleep-until-the-parousia, not soul-ascending-to-heaven).

The Hellenistic gravity won. Paul's apocalyptic Hebrew Bible framework lost. What remained was a religion that called itself Pauline but had reverted to the very Hellenistic salvation-of-the-soul framework Paul's Jewish cosmic-transformation framework had been trying to transcend.

## Misconceptions Addressed

| Misconception | Tabor's Response |
|---------------|------------------|
| "Salvation = sins forgiven so you can go to heaven when you die" | Forgiveness is stage one. Paul's actual goal is **glorification** — transformation into a new genus of cosmic spirit beings. Justified *for the purpose of* glorified. |
| "Philippians 2 proves Jesus pre-existed as a divine being" | Tabor reads it as Adam Christology. "Form of God" = Genesis 1:26-27 (humans made in God's image). "Did not grasp equality with God" = Jesus didn't repeat Adam's grasping. Therefore exalted. No pre-existence required. |
| "Paul preached the Trinity" | No. In the seven undisputed letters Jesus is a human being designated Son of God *at* resurrection (Romans 1:4). The Trinity is a fourth-century construction read back into Paul. |
| "Resurrection means corpses come back to life" | Resurrection (anastasis) = metamorphosis from dust body to spiritual body. "Flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God." Paul explicitly says he can't even describe what kind of body it is. |
| "Christianity is uniquely about love / cosmic transformation; other ancient religions were primitive" | Paul is drawing on (and modifying) Hellenistic salvation-religion vocabulary current in the Mediterranean world. His distinctiveness is the Genesis-grounded affirmation of the material world's transformation, not its abandonment. |
| "Paul thought the church would last 2000 years" | No. He thought 5-10 years tops. Every social instruction (don't marry, slaves stay slaves, etc.) is calibrated to a parousia he expected within his lifetime. |
| "The dead Christian goes to heaven to be with Jesus" | This is the post-failure Hellenistic flip. Paul's view is the dead **sleep** until the parousia; then living and dead are simultaneously transformed at the last trumpet. Philippians 1 has been misread to support the soul-to-heaven view. |
| "Paul died in Rome under Nero" | The earliest source (1 Clement, c. 96 CE) says Paul went to "the furthest parts of the West" and eventually died. Doesn't say Rome, doesn't say Nero. If Paul had died in Rome, Clement (writing from Rome) would have made a big deal of it. Tabor now thinks Paul probably did reach Spain. |
