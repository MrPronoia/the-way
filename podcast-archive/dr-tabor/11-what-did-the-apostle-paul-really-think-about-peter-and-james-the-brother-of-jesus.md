## Summary

Dr. Tabor opens this Paul series entry by reframing one of the most papered-over fractures in early Christianity: the relationship between Paul and the original Jerusalem apostles — James the brother of Jesus, Peter, and John. The book of Acts presents a tidy, Kumbaya harmony at the Jerusalem conference. Paul's own letters — our **only first-person eyewitness sources** from the first decades of the movement — tell a very different story. Tabor unpacks Galatians 1-2, which he calls "two of the most important and revealing chapters in the New Testament," and argues that Paul went out of his way to establish his independence from the Jerusalem apostles, calling them "the so-called pillars" and insisting his gospel came directly from a revelation of Jesus Christ, not from anyone in Jerusalem.

The deeper fracture isn't over whether Gentiles need to be circumcised — Tabor thinks Paul and James actually agreed on that, with non-Jews following something like the basic Noahide laws while Jews remained under the full Torah. The real fracture is whether **Jews themselves still need to keep the Torah**. Paul's letters reveal that he thinks they don't — not because Torah is bad, but because "the form of this world is passing away." Paul is an apocalyptic mystic; he expects the parousia within years; he believes the distinctions of Jew/Greek, slave/free, male/female are dissolving as a new cosmic age dawns. James, by contrast, remained committed to a Torah-observant messianic Judaism centered in Jerusalem. When Paul arrives back in Jerusalem in Acts 21, James confronts him with rumors that he's been telling Jews they no longer need to keep the Torah. Paul never gets to answer in Acts — Luke is working to preserve the harmony — but in 1 Corinthians 9 Paul himself admits the strategy: "To the Jews I became as a Jew... to those under the law as one under the law (though not being myself under the law)."

Tabor's conclusion, after wrestling with this question for years in *Paul and Jesus*, is that he ultimately sides with the much-maligned 19th-century scholar **F. C. Baur**: there was a real break. Paul kept it largely hidden when he was face-to-face with James and Peter — playing Torah-observant in their presence — but in his letters to Gentile churches he reveals a theology that, if the Jerusalem apostles fully understood it, would have produced an absolute split. The "battle of the apostles" was real, and modern New Testament scholarship has mostly papered over it.

## Key Teachings

### The Trilogy: Where This Argument Lives

Tabor opens by situating this discussion in his three-book sequence:
- ***The Jesus Dynasty*** — the historical Jesus and the family movement
- ***Paul and Jesus*** — the chapter "The Battle of the Apostles" lays out this argument in full
- ***The Jesus Discovery*** — archaeology of the Jesus movement (Talpiot tombs, etc.)

He notes a fourth book — ***Paul's Ascent to Paradise*** (his University of Chicago dissertation under Jonathan Z. Smith) — which grounds the mystical foundation of Paul's theology.

His preferred name for the movement that produced Christianity: **"the John the Baptist / Jesus / James movement,"** with Paul as a later, distinct figure.

### Galatians 1-2 Is the Smoking Gun

Tabor calls Galatians 1-2 "two of the most important and revealing chapters in the New Testament" and "unquestionably authentic." This is a Paul in the 50s CE, testifying in first person about his own experiences. The key claim in Galatians 1: **"I did not learn it from anybody. I did not receive it from anybody. I got it as a revelation of/from Jesus Christ."**

The Greek genitive is ambiguous — "of Jesus Christ" can mean either *about* Jesus (revelation concerning him) or *from* Jesus (revelation given by him). Tabor argues both are true, but the *from* reading is critical: Paul means **Jesus revealed himself directly to me**, and that revelation is independent of — and authoritative over — anything the Jerusalem apostles taught.

This is the bedrock of Paul's gospel: **his gospel**, as he calls it (not "the gospel" or "our gospel" — *my gospel*). Tabor flags this possessive as a "very definite term" that signals Paul's independence from the Jerusalem stream.

### "The So-Called Pillars" — Paul's Language Choice

When Paul describes the Jerusalem conference (Galatians 2), he writes that he laid out his gospel before "those who were reputed to be pillars" — and twice says "what they are means nothing to me." Some translations (Tabor likes the RSV) render this as **"the so-called pillars."**

Tabor's read: this isn't open contempt. Paul isn't saying he hates James and Peter. He's saying their authority doesn't constrain his gospel. Two issues were on the table at that conference:
1. Is Titus okay? (Paul brought him as a clean, uncircumcised Gentile example — "my show and tell")
2. Is my gospel okay?

Paul says he went up "by revelation" — meaning Jesus told him to go. He laid out his gospel. The apostles "added nothing." Paul is satisfied. **But** the language reveals what would have happened if they hadn't agreed: Paul would have walked out. He says he wanted to know if he was "running in vain" — but he also makes clear that if Jerusalem had said Gentiles must convert to Judaism, he would have rejected it. "I'm going to listen to Jesus, guys, and I know you were with him a long time ago, but you need to get updated — because I've heard from Jesus."

### The Real Agreement: Two-Track Torah

Tabor argues Acts 15 is "probably accurate on the main parameters." The actual deal:
- **Jews** stay Jews — they keep the Torah (with whatever interpretive tradition / oral Torah / halakha they follow)
- **Gentiles** follow basic universal ethics: sexual morality, no blood (associated with idolatry), no idolatry, basic Noahide principles

This is essentially rabbinic — the rabbinic position has long been that righteous Gentiles have a place in the world to come if they follow the basic laws applicable to all humanity. Tabor: "I think the decision was generally agreed. It's rabbinic, really."

So the **Jerusalem agreement was not the fracture point**. Paul agreed to it. James agreed to it. Titus was accepted as a model Gentile.

### The Real Fracture: Do Jews Still Need to Keep the Torah?

The hidden disagreement — the one that would have caused an absolute split if exposed — is whether **Jewish believers in Jesus still need to keep the Torah in the full observant way**. James says yes. Paul says no (privately, at least to Gentile audiences).

Tabor reconstructs Paul's actual position from 1 Corinthians 9:
> "To the Jews I became as a Jew, in order to win Jews. To those under the law I became as one under the law (though not being myself under the law) that I might win those under the law. To those outside the law I became as one outside the law... that I might win those outside the law."

The parenthetical is the tell: **"though not being myself under the law."** Paul is playing Torah-observant strategically when he's around Jews, but his own theology has moved past Torah observance because he believes "the form of this world is passing away" (1 Cor 7:31).

### Acts 21: The Confrontation That Acts Won't Let Paul Answer

Years after Galatians, Paul returns to Jerusalem with the collection from the Gentile churches — fulfilling what he sees as the prophecy that Gentiles would bring gifts to Jerusalem in the last days. James greets him and then drops the hammer (Tabor paraphrasing):

> "Paul, great to see you. But there are rumors that you're going beyond our agreement — that you're telling Jews they don't need to keep the Torah. We need to clear this up."

James proposes that Paul publicly demonstrate his Torah observance by sponsoring four men under a Nazirite vow at the Temple. The author of Acts wants you to believe Paul agreed and proved the rumors false. But Tabor notes the structural giveaway: **Paul never speaks in this scene.** Luke doesn't let him answer. "It's not a witness to what actually happened. It's a witness to the author of Acts wanting you to know that Paul is still Torah observant, whether he is or not."

The rumors James reports were not unfounded. 1 Corinthians 9, 1 Corinthians 7, Romans 14, and Colossians 2 ("touch not, taste not, handle not... these are a shadow of what is to come") all show Paul treating Torah practice as a strategic adaptation rather than a binding obligation.

### Paul's Strategy: "Become as a Jew" / "Become as a Gentile"

Tabor reads 1 Corinthians 9 as the most honest description of Paul's working method. When Paul is in Jewish settings — visiting James in Jerusalem, dining with observant believers — he keeps kosher, observes the festivals, plays the part. When he's in Gentile settings, he tells believers to "eat what is put before you" (Romans 14) without asking whether the meat came from the Isis temple's meat market.

This isn't simple hypocrisy. In Paul's framework, all of it is passing away. The forms don't carry the eternal weight that James and Peter believe they carry. So adapting to local custom for the sake of the mission is consistent with Paul's theology. But to James and the Jerusalem community, the same behavior looks like apostasy — Paul telling Jews that they no longer need to keep the covenant God gave at Sinai.

### The Pharisees Were Actually the Liberals

A side detour Tabor wants on the record: the popular image of Pharisees as legalistic conservatives is backwards. The Pharisees were **the liberals** of Second Temple Judaism — they were the ones figuring out how to make Torah livable. The famous example: Torah forbids carrying a burden on the Sabbath, including outside one's house. The Pharisees developed the *eruv* — a symbolic boundary that effectively extends the "house" to cover the whole neighborhood, so people can go out to the toilet, carry needed items, and live normal lives on the Sabbath. (Tabor notes the Qumran community took the opposite, stricter view — they actually built their latrines 200 cubits outside the camp and apparently waited the full Sabbath day.)

The Pharisees, the Qumran community, and Jesus himself were all operating with different interpretive traditions about *how* to keep the Torah. Tabor speculates that Paul and Peter and James were probably all relatively "liberal" — closer to the Pharisaic free-and-open interpretive style — but the question of *whether to keep it at all* is a different question, and that's where Paul departed.

### Did Paul Break With Them? F. C. Baur Was Right

Tabor describes the central scholarly question of his book *Paul and Jesus*: did Paul ultimately break with the Jerusalem apostles, or did they maintain an uneasy coexistence in which Paul played Torah-observant in person while preaching something different in his letters?

His final answer — and the position he says nearly all his colleagues reject — is that **F. C. Baur was right.** Baur (Ferdinand Christian Baur, 1800s, Tübingen school, *not* to be confused with the Walter Bauer of the New Testament dictionary) argued there was an absolute split between Pauline and Petrine Christianity, and that the New Testament was assembled in part to paper over that split.

Tabor's evidence for the break comes especially from 2 Corinthians 10-13 (the chapters his dissertation focused on). Paul writes:
> "Are they Hebrews? So am I. Are they Israelites? So am I. Are they apostles? So am I — and a better one."

Most scholars assume these "super-apostles" are unknown rivals — not the Jerusalem leaders. Tabor went back to Baur and concluded they were exactly that: **James, Peter, and the rest.** Paul is no longer being diplomatic. He's positioning himself as superior to the Jerusalem leadership.

### Why Paul Could Believe Torah Was Passing Away

Paul's theology is not anti-Torah. He's not arguing the Torah was wrong. He's arguing it belonged to an age that is closing. Tabor highlights:
- 1 Corinthians 7:29-31 — "The appointed time has grown very short... the form of this world is passing away"
- 1 Corinthians 7:29 — "Those who have wives should be as though they had none"
- Galatians 3:28 — "Neither Jew nor Greek, neither slave nor free, neither male nor female"
- The Hebrews opening (which Tabor doesn't attribute to Paul but says Paul "would sign on to"): "What is obsolete is passing away"

For Paul, every cultural and biological distinction is dissolving as the new creation breaks in. Torah belongs to the present age. The new age is "in Christ" — and in Christ, the old distinctions don't apply. **Tabor: "I don't think he was against the Torah. He just thought it was passing away."**

This is why Paul could honestly tell James he agreed with the Jerusalem decision (Jews keep Torah, Gentiles don't) while privately believing the entire framework was obsolete within years.
