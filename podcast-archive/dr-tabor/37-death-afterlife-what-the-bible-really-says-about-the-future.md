## Summary

This is the eleventh installment of Tabor's Death & Afterlife series and functions as the **survey-overview episode** — the one where he steps back, looks across the whole biblical sweep, and tries to answer in a single hour the question Morton Smith once asked him to answer in a single chapter: **What does the Bible really say about the future?** The audacious title is borrowed (Smith's 1988 Prometheus Press volume *What the Bible Really Says* assigned each contributor an impossible topic — Tabor got "the future"), and Tabor confesses up front that no responsible academic would speak this way except that the Bible, being a library of documents from many times and authors, says all kinds of contradictory things. His method in the chapter and in this video is **chronological tracing of two intertwined sub-topics**: the future of *the world* (eschatology proper) and the future of *the individual* (afterlife). They overlap. They evolve. They get hijacked. He walks the listener from the early Hebrew Bible's three-decker flat-earth cosmos, through the post-exilic emergence of an "age-to-come" eschatology, into the Hellenistic-era introduction of bodily resurrection, and finally into the Greek-immortal-soul fusion that colonizes Christian thought from the second century onward.

The argumentative spine is **change**. Tabor refuses the standard religious framing of "progressive revelation" — the idea that earlier biblical writers were primitively groping toward truths fully revealed only in Jesus or Paul. He insists on the historian's framing: these are *different views* held by different communities at different times, sometimes in conflict, sometimes evolving, sometimes simply contradicting each other. The early Hebrew Bible has no resurrection, no immortal soul, no heaven-for-the-righteous — Adam and all his descendants die, return to dust, and sleep in Sheol regardless of righteousness. The future of the world is the endless cycle of Ecclesiastes ("a generation comes, a generation goes"). Then exile changes everything. With Jeremiah and Isaiah, Tabor traces the emergence of an **eschatological future** — a coming "new age," a Davidic Messiah, a renewed creation where the wolf lies down with the lamb and the earth is full of the knowledge of Yahweh. Crucially, this is *still on earth*. Not heaven. Not soul-flight. A transformed cosmos, here. By the time of Daniel ~165 BCE, bodily resurrection makes its first clear appearance: "Many who sleep in the dust shall awake." Daniel 12 is the load-bearing text — astral immortality, the dead shining like the stars.

Then comes the collision. Greek philosophical anthropology — Plato's intrinsic immortality of the soul, the body-as-prison, the upward escape to the stars — meets Hebrew/Jewish bodily-resurrection eschatology in the Hellenistic period (300 BCE onward). The Wisdom of Solomon already shows the fusion in progress: the "souls of the righteous are in the hand of God," but they will also "shine forth" and rule the nations at God's visitation. By the New Testament, Paul has worked out a distinctively Jewish reading: not soul-escape, but **re-clothing** — the perishable dust-body sown into death, the imperishable spiritual body raised at the parousia. Paul's 1 Corinthians 15 is the keystone. The cosmic transformation is the goal. The body is your *mode of existence*. Death is the enemy that gets destroyed last. But — and this is Tabor's closing move — the church fathers, especially Origen, Clement of Alexandria, and supremely Augustine, were neoplatonists down to the bones. They could not avoid fusing the Greek soul-framework onto the Pauline body-framework. The next installment (video #38, the eventual #12 of the series Tabor decides to extend to fourteen) will deep-dive that fusion. This video ends with the promise: we're about to leave the Bible behind and watch what Christianity *did* with the Bible.

## Key Teachings

### The Audacious Title — Morton Smith's 1988 Prometheus Volume

Tabor opens by explaining the title's provenance. **Morton Smith** — Columbia University, the encyclopedic historian of ancient religions who had helped Tabor through his dissertation in the 1970s — called Tabor at home in Williamsburg around 1987 and asked him to write the chapter on "the Future" for a forthcoming Prometheus Press volume he was co-editing with **Joseph Hoffman**: *What the Bible Really Says*. The volume's other chapters covered capital punishment, government, marriage and divorce, miracles, segregation, slavery, war, wealth, wisdom, women, the world.

Tabor's initial response on the phone: "The Bible and the future — are you kidding? Like the whole Bible and the whole future?" Smith's answer: yes. Survey it. Start with the ancient Near Eastern Hebrew Bible view and work your way down. Include the Apocrypha. Include early Christianity. One chapter.

It became the hardest article Tabor had ever written, but he is "pretty pleased with it" and has used it for years in his Death & Afterlife course. He has now uploaded the PDF free of charge on his blog (jamestabor.com), and this video is built around it — a recap of the chapter's structure before the series leaves the biblical material behind.

### The Three-Decker Hebrew Cosmos — Flat Earth, Sheol, Heaven Above

Tabor reprises the cosmology established in earlier videos. The Hebrew Bible pictures a **three-decked universe**: heaven above (where Yahweh dwells, fitted with sun, moon, stars under a dome / firmament), the flat earth below (the realm of the living), and Sheol underneath (the realm of the dead — a sleeping/shadowy state, not hell, not torture, just *non-participation*). Psalm 115:16-18 is the load-bearing text: "The heavens are Yahweh's heavens, but the earth he has given to the sons of men. The dead do not praise Yahweh, nor any who go down into silence."

This cosmos is *static* with respect to the future. Generations come and go. People are born, live, die, return to dust. Their shadow-selves rest in Sheol. There is no resurrection. There is no individual afterlife in the Greek sense. The dead are simply *out of the game*. The recurring metaphor in both Testaments is **sleep** — and Tabor insists the metaphor matters: sleep is not non-existence, it is non-participation. You are taken off the field, on the bench. If you wake up, that's resurrection — but Job doesn't expect it. Ecclesiastes doesn't expect it. Most of the Hebrew Bible doesn't expect it.

### Nephesh Chayyah — Humans Are "Living Breathers," Same as Animals

The pivotal mistranslation. Genesis 2:7 in the King James says "Adam became a living soul." The Hebrew is **nephesh chayyah** — literally "a living breather." And the *exact same phrase* is used for every land animal, insect, and creeping thing in Genesis 1. There is no Hebrew anthropological category for "immortal soul" that humans have and animals don't.

Tabor: "A human is a living breather. A dog is a living breather. A cricket is a living breather. We are all dust creatures. We all return to dust." The KJV's "living soul" smuggles Plato's anthropology into Genesis 2 — and that single mistranslation has done more theological damage than almost any other. The "Breath of Life" God breathes into Adam is the same biology that gives oxygen-breathing creatures their respiration. It is not a divine spark unique to humans.

This is the bedrock for everything downstream: the Hebrew framework cannot generate Platonic soul-flight because it has no soul to flee. It has a *breather* who returns to dust.

### Eschatology Emerges After Exile — A New Age, Not a New Place

Tabor's chronology breaks the Hebrew Bible into two stages with the **Babylonian Exile (586 BCE)** as the hinge. Before exile, the dominant view is the static Genesis-Ecclesiastes picture: birth, death, dust, Sheol, repeat. After exile, with Jeremiah and Isaiah and the prophetic books, something new appears — **eschatology**, the doctrine of "the last things," the idea that history is moving toward a *new age* on earth.

The texts: Jeremiah 31 — a new covenant written on the heart; Isaiah 11 — the wolf and the lamb, the leopard with the kid, the child playing over the asp's den, the earth full of the knowledge of Yahweh "as the waters cover the sea"; Jeremiah 23 — a Davidic righteous branch. Tabor flags the phrase that recurs: **"the days are coming."** Not "in heaven." Not "after you die." *The days are coming* — meaning a future era of this world.

The transformation is sometimes called cosmic, sometimes called a "new creation," but the meaning is consistently that *this* creation reaches an ideal at the end of the age. Earth remains the venue. The new Jerusalem is on a mountain, on the earth. The nations come *up* to it. This is the framework Jesus and Paul inherit, and the framework most modern Christians have abandoned for a disembodied-heaven framework that the Hebrew prophets would not have recognized.

### Apocalypticism — The End Is Near and Calculable

Out of post-exilic eschatology grows a more intense variant: **apocalypticism**. Not just "someday the new age will come" but "the end is *near* — and the signs can be read." The Book of Daniel is the keystone in the Hebrew Bible (chapters 2, 7, 8, 9, 11, 12). The Book of Revelation is its Roman-era recasting in early Christianity. Tabor flags the four-kingdom schema — Babylon, Persia, Greece, Rome — and Daniel's great statue with head of gold, breast of silver, belly of bronze, legs of iron, feet of clay. By the New Testament era, Rome is the fourth and final kingdom. The end has to be near.

Tabor notes that **apocalypticism never really left**. We are living through another heyday of it in the 20th and 21st centuries — environmental collapse, wars, famines, disease epidemics. He flags an upcoming course he plans to teach: *Apocalypticism Through the Ages*, tracing end-of-the-world scenarios from Daniel to the present.

### The First Clear Resurrection Text — Daniel 12 and Wisdom of Solomon

Bodily resurrection enters the Hebrew Bible late and tentatively. Daniel 12:2 is the cleanest text: **"Many of those who sleep in the dust shall awake — some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt."** The image of "shining like the stars" gives an *astral immortality* dimension — not Greek soul-flight to the stars, but a glorified condition that includes star-like radiance.

The Wisdom of Solomon (first century BCE, in the Apocrypha) shows the fusion in progress. Tabor reads two contrasting passages:
- **The old view (Wisdom 2):** "Short and sorrowful is our life... no one has ever been known to return from Hades. We were born by mere chance... our breath is smoke, reason is a spark." This is the Sadducean / Epicurean view — death is final, life is a brief animal episode.
- **The new view (Wisdom 3):** "The souls of the righteous are in the hand of God; no torment will ever touch them. In the eyes of the foolish they seemed to have died... but their hope is full of immortality." At the time of God's visitation they "shine forth" and govern the nations.

Tabor's point: the Wisdom author *intends* the second view to be the right answer. The first view is described only to be repudiated. This is what Pharisaic Judaism — and Jesus, and Paul — inherit.

### Paul's Transformation Framework — Re-Clothing, Not Escape

Paul develops the Pharisaic-resurrection inheritance into something distinctively his own. The keystone is 1 Corinthians 15. Tabor walks the framework:

- At the **parousia** (Christ's appearance), Paul expects to be alive ("we who are alive"). "Behold, I tell you a mystery: we shall not all sleep, but we shall all be changed — in a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trumpet."
- The Greek word is **metamorphosis** — the same root as caterpillar-to-butterfly. The seed dies in the ground; what comes up is not the seed but something new.
- The dead come forth *from the state of being dead*. They have been "naked" (no body) in the interim, resting in the hand of God. At the trumpet they are **re-clothed** with an imperishable body.
- The new body is *glorious, immortal, eternal, powerful* — Paul piles up adjectives because he cannot describe it concretely. It is the "complete opposite" of the lowly body of humiliation.

Crucially, this is **not soul-escape**. Paul wants to be *clothed*, not *naked* (2 Cor 5). The self needs a body. The body is your mode of existence. The Platonic ideal of the naked soul rising free is the opposite of what Paul hopes for.

### The Cosmic Transformation — Not Just Persons, the Whole Creation

Tabor flags a verse most Christians never notice: Romans 8:18-25. **The whole creation (*ktisis*) is groaning in labor pains, subject to decay, awaiting its own release.** It is not just that individuals get raised; the entire created order is being remade. New heavens, new earth. Death itself swallowed up. No more mourning, sighing, tears.

This is the framework that disappears when Christianity goes platonic. In the platonized framework, salvation is *escape from* the world — the world is the cave, the prison, the cage. In the Pauline framework, salvation is the *redemption of* the world — the same earth restored, the same creation glorified. The new Jerusalem comes *down*. The kingdom comes *on earth as it is in heaven*.

This is the eschatology popular Christianity has lost. Tabor: "It's actually here and now in this world — but a future."

### What's Next — Origen, Clement, Augustine, and the Platonization

Tabor closes by previewing the next installments. The series, originally planned at twelve videos, is now extending to fourteen. The next video (#12 of the series, video #38 in the Paul playlist) will tackle the church fathers — specifically **Origen of Alexandria** (taught by Clement, a thoroughgoing neoplatonist who tried to christianize Plato) and **Augustine of Hippo** (whose *City of God* and *Confessions* finalized the platonization of Christian eschatology).

Tabor's preview: these fathers do not want to embrace gnosticism — they affirm Genesis, they affirm the goodness of creation, they affirm bodily resurrection. But they also believe in the immortal soul. And they have to work out what kind of body gets raised. The answer they produce is "a complete sea change" from the New Testament — but it becomes the view of Christianity for the next 1700 years.

After that, Tabor promises two more videos breaking out of the ancient world entirely: what **modern science**, particularly brain science, the study of consciousness, and near-death experience research, has to say in dialogue with these ancient ideas. The series will land there.

## Misconceptions Addressed

| Misconception | Tabor's Response |
|---------------|------------------|
| "The Bible has one consistent view of the future" | The Bible is a library spanning over a millennium of authors. The ancient Hebrew view (no resurrection, sleep in Sheol) is *radically different* from the post-exilic eschatological view (new age, Messiah, renewed creation), which is different again from the late-Second-Temple resurrection view (Daniel, Wisdom of Solomon), which is different from Paul's transformation framework. Tabor's method: historical-chronological tracing, not harmonization. |
| "Biblical authors were progressively revealing one truth over time" | That assumes the final fully-revealed picture is what Christians believe today. Tabor rejects this: these are different communities holding different views in different periods, not stages of one revelation. "All change is not progress." |
| "Genesis 2:7 means humans have an immortal soul that animals don't" | The Hebrew is *nephesh chayyah* — "living breather." **The exact same phrase is used for all animals, insects, and reptiles in Genesis 1.** The KJV's "living soul" is one of the most consequential mistranslations in Bible history; it smuggled Plato's anthropology into Genesis 2. |
| "Sheol is hell — eternal conscious torture" | Sheol is a sleeping/shadow state — neither bliss nor torment. The dead exist as *shades*, kept in the hand of God, not participating but not annihilated either. Hades just means "the unseen realm." The medieval hell-imagery comes from fusing Platonic underworld scenes with Christian eschatology centuries later. |
| "The Bible promises that good people go to heaven when they die" | Not in the Hebrew Bible. Not in Jesus's teaching. Not in Paul. The Hebrew framework: you sleep in the dust. The Pauline framework: at the parousia, in the twinkling of an eye, you are re-clothed with an imperishable body — on the new earth. Heaven as the destination of the saved soul is a Hellenistic fusion that became dominant after the second century CE. |
| "Death is a friend, a graceful passage home" | Paul: "**The last enemy to be destroyed is death**" (1 Cor 15:26). Death is not welcomed; it is *defeated*. The Socrates-welcomes-death posture comes from the *Phaedo*, not from Paul. |
| "Resurrection means a corpse coming back to life" | That's resuscitation — what happens to Lazarus, Jairus's daughter, and the widow of Nain's son (all of whom die again). Pauline resurrection is *metamorphosis* — the dust-body sown, the spiritual body raised. A new mode of existence, not a tune-up of the old one. |
| "Eschatology means going to heaven at the end" | Hebrew eschatology means **a new age on the earth** — the wolf with the lamb, the earth full of the knowledge of God, the Davidic Messiah ruling. Jeremiah 30-31, Isaiah 11. The new Jerusalem comes *down*. The kingdom comes *on earth as it is in heaven*. |
| "The Bible has a flat-earth cosmology, so it has no credibility" | Tabor flags this from a viewer comment. His response: the series is *historical-descriptive*, not normative. The Hebrew authors did picture a three-decker flat-earth cosmos. That tells us how to read their *idiom*, not what we should believe about cosmology today. |
| "Necromancy is biblically endorsed" | Isaiah 8 explicitly ridicules those who consult the dead — "listening for peeps and whispers, like things that go bump in the night. Should not the living consult the living?" The Saul-and-the-medium episode in 1 Samuel 28 is anomalous and the author's stance is unclear. |
| "Daniel's apocalyptic visions can be decoded to predict the date of the end" | They've been "tried for 2500 years." Tabor: every generation thinks they're the last; the four-kingdom schema gets reapplied; the Book of Revelation recasts Daniel for Rome's era. Apocalypticism never ends — it just keeps generating new readings. |
