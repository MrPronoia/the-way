## Summary

Dr. Tabor walks through the **Pseudo-Clementine literature** — the *Homilies* and *Recognitions*, a body of second/third-century novels claiming to be written by Clement of Rome (Peter's successor) — and argues that hidden inside them is something extraordinary: **the earliest surviving Christian polemic against Paul**, preserved through a character called Simon Magus. Most scholars working in this material agree that "Simon Magus" in the Clementines is a **veiled cipher for Paul**. The criticisms leveled at him — that he rejects Torah, that he claims authority from visions instead of having known Jesus in the flesh, that he is an "apostate from the law" — line up exactly with the Ebionite critique of Paul preserved in other sources (Epiphanius, Hegesippus).

The text frames a sharp doctrinal contest. **Paul (as Simon) appeals to visions and miracles. Peter responds that signs are not proof — that even Satan can give visions, and a true prophet is recognized by his life lining up with Torah.** The Clementines build a chain-of-authority argument: Moses → the 70 elders → the high priesthood → Jesus the True Prophet → James and Peter and the Jerusalem apostles. Anyone who shows up claiming "I had a vision and Jesus told me a new gospel" — no matter what wonders they work — is to be rejected if their teaching contradicts the commandments. The criterion is essentially Deuteronomy 13 and Deuteronomy 18 applied directly to Paul's apostolic claim.

The video closes with the Ebionite ethical program the Clementines lay out: **vegetarianism, no wine, no animal sacrifice, the body as the temple, prayer and righteousness as the only acceptable offering.** Tabor connects this to James the Just (who, per Hegesippus, never drank wine or ate flesh), to the Dead Sea Scrolls community ("build me a temple of men"), and to Isaiah 11/65's peaceable-kingdom vision. He admits with a wry smile that he himself enjoys a glass of wine — he's not a good Ebionite — but the substantive point stands: this is a coherent Torah-faithful Jesus tradition that runs through John the Baptist, Jesus, James, and the Jerusalem community, and that explicitly defines itself **against** the Pauline gospel.

## Key Teachings

### What the Pseudo-Clementines Are

The *Homilies* and *Recognitions* are companion texts purporting to be from Clement, bishop of Rome and successor of Peter. They survive in Greek, Latin, Syriac, and other languages — though not complete in any one tradition. They're **forgeries** in the technical sense (Clement did not write them; they bear the marks of second/third-century pseudepigraphy), but Tabor argues forcefully that **they preserve genuine earlier controversies**. The novel-format wrapping holds inside it Jewish-Christian (Ebionite) source material that goes back much further.

The Clementines open with a letter from Clement to James in which Clement calls James "the Lord" — the title typically reserved for Jesus — and says Jesus himself put James in charge. The frame, in other words, is **Jerusalem-centered, James-headed Christianity** writing back to Rome about who has real authority. The answer it gives: listen to Peter. Reject Simon Magus.

### Simon Magus Is a Cipher for Paul

Simon Magus appears in Acts 8 as a magician rebuked by Peter. In the Clementines he is rebuked again — but the rebuke has expanded into a sustained theological attack with content that does not match the brief Acts portrait. The criticisms leveled at "Simon" in the Clementines:

- He rejects Torah and is an "apostate from the law"
- He claims apostolic authority on the basis of visions, not personal knowledge of Jesus
- He works signs and wonders that deceive
- He boasts: "I am the standing one... in me alone is the incarnate deity, for the Father dwells in me"
- He claims his visions outrank the testimony of those who knew Jesus face to face

**These are exactly the Ebionite complaints against Paul** preserved in Epiphanius and elsewhere. Tabor: most scholars working in this material agree that Simon is a "veiled criticism of Paul." Even the boast — "the Father dwells in me" — has Pauline parallels: "he who disregards this disregards not me, but the Holy Spirit speaking through me."

### The Apostolic Test: Did You Know Jesus, or Just See a Vision?

This is the central polemic, repeated several times across the texts Tabor reads:

Peter (as written): *"If our Lord had said that Satan fell like lightning from heaven, why do you speak of your visions as if they were from God? Do you not understand that evil spirits have also the power to deceive and lead astray through visions?"*

The Clementine criterion for apostleship is **personal acquaintance with Jesus in the flesh** — what Paul himself dismissively calls *kata sarka*, "according to the flesh," in 2 Corinthians. The Jerusalem position is the inverse: knowing Jesus *kata sarka* is the qualification. Anyone who arrives later with "I had a vision and Jesus revealed a new gospel to me" is automatically suspect — because Satan has visions too.

This is **Deuteronomy 13 applied directly to Paul**. Deuteronomy 13: even if a prophet gives a sign that comes to pass, if he calls you to follow gods you have not known (or, by extension, to abandon the commandments), do not listen. Signs alone are not proof.

### Chain of Authority: Moses → Elders → Jesus → James and Peter

The Clementines construct an explicit succession argument:

- Moses received the Torah
- He delivered the mysteries to the **70 elders** in whom the Spirit of prophecy was renewed
- The chain continues through the high priesthood and teachers
- It culminates in **Jesus the True Prophet** ("the prophet of truth")
- And is now carried by **James, Peter, and the Jerusalem apostles**

Notice what this excludes: independent vision-based authority. Paul is not in the chain. He's bypassing the chain. The Clementines call this out as the disqualifying move.

Tabor catches a striking parallel: the title for Jesus here, *the True Prophet / the Prophet of Truth*, lines up linguistically with the **Teacher of Righteousness** of the Dead Sea Scrolls. The Hebrew *moreh ha-tsedek* can be read either as "Teacher of Righteousness" or, following John Reeves's argument that Tabor finds persuasive, as **"True Teacher."** Same root, same conceptual frame — a community organized around an authoritative teacher figure standing in the prophetic succession.

### Deuteronomy 18 — Jesus as the Prophet Like Moses

The Clementines lean heavily on **Deuteronomy 18:15-19** — "the Lord shall raise up to you a prophet from among your brethren." The Ebionite community read this as the Mosaic prophecy of Jesus. The Holy Spirit "hastens through the ages" from Moses through the elders until it finally lands on Jesus at his baptism. (Some textual variants of Mark's baptism scene have: "Thou art my Son; this day have I begotten thee" — Psalm 2:7 — capturing the moment Jesus becomes Son by the descent of the Spirit.)

This is **adoptionist** Christology — Jesus as the prophet like Moses, recognized at baptism, not pre-existent deity. It's the Christology Paul does not share. And Deuteronomy 18 is also the text that includes the **false-prophet test**: "the prophet who shall presume to speak a word in my name which I have not commanded him to speak — that prophet shall die." The Clementines are not subtle about the implication.

### Test by Fruits, Not by Signs

Peter (as written): *"We are commanded by our Lord to judge a tree by its fruit, not by its leaves or branches — that is, not by outward appearances. And even if one should perform signs and wonders, if his life is not in accordance with the divine law, he is a false prophet. For the true prophet of God will never act contrary to the instructions of God."*

Notice: **"instructions"** translates *torah*. The criterion is Torah-conformity. A prophet's life must align with the commandments. Signs alone, visions alone, miracles alone — none of these constitute apostolic credential. Tabor concedes that on personal morality Paul actually rates well (the famous fruit-of-the-Spirit/works-of-the-flesh lists). The real charge that bites is Torah-rejection — Paul as **apostate from the law**.

### The Ebionite Ethical Program: Vegetarian, No Wine, No Sacrifice

Once the apostleship question is settled, the Clementines lay out their actual lived ethic. It is striking and consistent:

**Vegetarianism.** "I earnestly entreat you to abstain from all animal food... For in the beginning, the eating of flesh was unknown until the flood." A Genesis-1 argument: humans were originally created vegetarian (Gen 1:29). Meat-eating is a fallen accommodation, not the ideal.

**No wine.** Alcohol defiles. The temple priests abstained from wine during service (Leviticus 10, the Aaron's-sons-killed-for-being-drunk passage). And — the move that turns the priesthood-rule into universal ethic — *you are the temple now*. If you are the temple, no wine.

**No animal sacrifice.** "The sacrifice of animals, so far as it depends on man, is to be abolished. And the sacrifices which remain are prayers and giving of thanks and righteousness." Tabor connects this directly to the Dead Sea Scrolls community rule: "build me a temple of men, who will offer pure sacrifices" — meaning prayer.

**The triple abstention from Acts 15.** "Keep yourselves from blood and things strangled and from idols." This is **James's exact ruling at the Jerusalem council** — and the Clementines preserve the principle. No bloodshed. No meat with blood in it (non-kosher slaughter). No food offered to idols.

### James the Just as the Ebionite Exemplar

Hegesippus (preserved in Eusebius) records that **James the Just**:
- Never drank wine or strong drink
- Never ate flesh
- Wore only linen (priestly garb)
- His knees were calloused like a camel's from constant prayer at the Temple

The Clementines' ethical program matches James's actual practice exactly. This is not an invented sect's invented ethic — it is **the practice of Jesus's brother**, and (the Ebionites would argue) of Jesus himself, of John the Baptist before him, and of the Nazarene/Essene stream they all came from. Vegetarian. Sober. Anti-sacrifice. Body as temple. Prayer as offering.

### The Peaceable Kingdom and Isaiah 11

The vegetarian ethic isn't just personal discipline — it's **eschatological**. Isaiah 11 ("the wolf shall dwell with the lamb...") and Isaiah 65 (new heavens and new earth) describe a coming creation in which animals are no longer hurt or destroyed. "They will not hurt or destroy on all my holy mountain" — and the holy mountain is the Temple Mount. If no destruction of animals there, **no sacrifices**. The vegetarian Ebionite is already living the coming kingdom now. The peaceable-kingdom imagery — animals at peace, children playing safely with snakes — is the world they are practicing into.

### What This Tells Us About the Earliest Christian Split

Tabor's larger point: the Clementines, even granting their late literary form, **assume a pre-existing rupture between Paul's gospel and the Jerusalem community led by James**. The fight is already old by the time these novels are written down. They are evidence — alongside Galatians 2, Acts 15, the Ebionite testimony in Epiphanius, the letter of James in the New Testament — that there were always **two Christianities**: the Torah-faithful, vegetarian, James-led Jerusalem stream, and the Gentile-mission, vision-based, Torah-relaxed Pauline stream. The Clementines are the surviving theological broadside from the losing side.

## Misconceptions Addressed

| Misconception | Tabor's Response |
|---------------|------------------|
| "Simon Magus in the Clementines is just the guy from Acts 8" | The criticisms levied at Simon — Torah-rejection, vision-based authority, claim of incarnate deity, apostate status — match the Ebionite critique of Paul exactly. He is a veiled cipher for Paul. |
| "There was no early opposition to Paul; he was universally received" | The Clementines, the Ebionite testimony in Epiphanius, the Hegesippus material on James, and Galatians 2 itself all witness to a sustained, organized Jerusalem-centered opposition to Paul. |
| "Visions are a sufficient apostolic credential" | The Clementines reject this explicitly: visions can be demonic; Satan deceives; the test is conformity of life to Torah, not the spectacle of revelation. |
| "Jewish Christianity was a marginal sect" | The Clementines locate it at the center — Jerusalem, James, Peter — and treat the Pauline mission as the deviation, not the norm. |
| "Animal sacrifice was always central to authentic biblical religion" | Jeremiah 7, the Ebionite tradition, the Dead Sea Scrolls, and James's practice all point to a prophetic-priestly stream that read sacrifice as accommodation to weakness, not divine ideal. |
| "Christianity has always been a meat-and-wine faith" | The earliest Jesus tradition Tabor can reconstruct — John the Baptist, James the Just, the Ebionites — is vegetarian and abstinent. The meat-and-wine framework is later. |
