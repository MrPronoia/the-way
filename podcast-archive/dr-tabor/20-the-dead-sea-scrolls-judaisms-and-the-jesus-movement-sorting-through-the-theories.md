## Summary

This is Tabor in his element — a lecture to his "Jesus and the Dead Sea Scrolls" online course, working through the competing scholarly theories about how the Qumran community fits into the broader landscape of late Second Temple Judaisms and the John-the-Baptizer / Jesus / James-the-Just movement. The deliberate plural — **"Judaisms"** — does the heavy lifting. Tabor's first move is to dismantle the idea that there was ever a single monolithic "Judaism" in the first century. Like Christianity, Islam, and Buddhism today, late Second Temple Judaism was a contested space of competing visions, halakic interpretations, messianic expectations, and apocalyptic timelines. The Dead Sea Scroll community is one such Judaism. The Jesus movement is another. The question is what the actual relationship is.

Tabor opens with the most personal disclosure he's ever made on camera about a scholar: his decades-long friendship with **Robert Eisenman**, the renegade Dead Sea Scrolls scholar who argues James the Just IS the Teacher of Righteousness and Paul IS the Liar of the Habakkuk Pesher. Tabor spent months at Qumran with Eisenman in 1991-92 surveying over a thousand caves along the Kidron — six-person teams, climbing equipment, one kilometer each. Tabor calls Eisenman a "doktorvater" (the German academic term for "doctor-father"), placing him alongside Jonathan Z. Smith, Norman Perrin, and Robert Grant as the scholars who made him. But — and this is where the lecture gets interesting — Tabor disagrees with Eisenman on the central claim. **Eisenman wants to move the Teacher of Righteousness and the flourishing of the Qumran community into the first century CE so the Teacher can BE James the Just**, with Paul as the lying opponent. Tabor sides with **Michael Wise's** Journal of Biblical Literature article: paleographic dating, coin evidence, and the textual silence about first-century events (no Herod, no Pompey's invasion of 63 BCE handled in the Scrolls' present tense, etc.) all push the Teacher's career to the first century BCE — roughly 70 BCE for the Teacher's death.

But here's where Tabor's signature move lands: **scholars should still read Eisenman carefully — just forget the dating question.** Eisenman's substantive claims about the *character* of the movement — that it was messianic, apocalyptic, zealous for the Torah, nationalistic, "prepare-the-way-in-the-wilderness," anti-Rome — are exactly right, and the standard "Jewish-monks-in-the-desert" picture from Father Roland de Vaux and the early Catholic scholars is exactly wrong. So Tabor lands on a synthesis: the Qumran group flourished in the first century BCE under the Teacher; survived in some form into the first century CE; and the John-Jesus-James movement is a later, related stream of the same Judaism — a "kinder, gentler" Qumran-type movement with the same apocalyptic prepare-the-way-in-the-wilderness DNA but a Hillel-style halakic openness rather than Shammai-style rigidity. Jesus and James as Essene-type successors to a Qumran tradition that had been continuing through the "dark age" between Herod and the destruction of Jerusalem. This is the lineage Tabor has been tracing in his Essene → Nazarene → Ebionite work for decades. The Dead Sea Scrolls aren't a parallel sect that died out in 73 CE. They're the upstream tradition the Jesus movement is downstream of.

## Key Teachings

### Why "Judaisms" Is Plural — Late Second Temple as a Contested Field

Tabor is emphatic: scholars now write "late Second Temple Judaisms" with an intentional S because there was no monolithic Judaism in the first century any more than there is one Christianity or Islam today. The period stretches from the Maccabees (~165 BCE) down to Masada in 73 CE — though Tabor extends it to Bar Kokhba (~135 CE) because Yohanan ben Zakkai and Rabbi Akiva survive 70 and bridge into the Mishnah era at Sepphoris, Safed, and Tiberias.

In this contested space you have Pharisees (Hillel and Shammai schools), Sadducees, Essenes, Zealots, Maccabean/Hasmonean partisans, baptizing groups, apocalyptic communities, and the Dead Sea community at Qumran — all calling themselves Israel, all reading the same Torah, all with different halakic interpretations and different visions of what redemption looks like. Jesus, John the Baptizer, and James the Just are all operating inside this matrix. The Dead Sea Scrolls give us the textual archive of one of those Judaisms.

### Eisenman vs. Wise on Dating the Teacher of Righteousness

This is the lecture's spine. Two of Tabor's friends — Robert Eisenman and Michael Wise — collaborated on releasing the unpublished Scrolls in 1991-92 (they share authorship on the "scoop" volume) but disagree fundamentally on dating:

- **Eisenman** — Pushes the Teacher of Righteousness, the Wicked Priest, the Liar, and the dramatic events of the Habakkuk Pesher into the **first century CE**. Identifies the Teacher with James the Just (martyred ~63 CE), the Liar with Paul, the Wicked Priest with the Jerusalem establishment that killed James.
- **Wise** — In his JBL article (and book) lists *every* identifiable historical reference in the Scrolls and counts textual copies by paleographic decade. The flourishing peaks in the mid-first century BCE; the Teacher dies around 70 BCE; no first-century CE figure is mentioned anywhere — no Herod, no Augustus, no Pilate. **It is not possible** that a movement flourishing in the first century CE would have nothing to say about Herod's 40-year reign as king of the Jews, Pompey's 63 BCE invasion in their living memory, or the Roman occupation.

Tabor: "Michael's article is convincing, so I'm going to have a problem with Eisenman from the start." But he stresses Eisenman's value is in the *substance* of the description, not the dating.

### Why Eisenman Is Still Worth Reading — The Substantive Claims

What Eisenman gets right against the early-establishment Dead Sea Scrolls consensus:

- The Qumran group was **NOT** Jewish monks — quietist, pacifist, contemplative, Catholic-monastic in spirit. That was the 1950s Catholic-scholar projection.
- They were **messianic, apocalyptic, nationalistic, zealous for Torah, anti-Roman, preparing-the-way-in-the-wilderness**.
- Their central question matches the disciples' question in Acts 1:6: "Will you at this time restore the kingdom to Israel?" — the restoration of the Davidic kingdom on Earth, not symbolically and not in heaven.
- This profile fits the Jesus movement (Mark 1 — "prepare the way of the Lord" — directly quotes Isaiah 40, which is also the Qumran community's founding text).
- The James-vs-Paul dichotomy in early Christianity echoes the Teacher-vs-Liar dichotomy in the Pesharim and is reflected in the pseudo-Clementine literature.

So forget the dating fight: the *kind* of movement Eisenman describes is correct, and it illuminates what the Jesus movement actually was. The mainstream Dead Sea Scrolls establishment, by dismissing Eisenman wholesale, has missed this.

### The Archetypal Set-Piece — Teacher, Liar, Wicked Priest

Tabor offers a heuristic for understanding why so many different identifications of the Pesher characters get proposed. The Habakkuk Pesher and related texts give us four narrative slots:

1. **The Teacher** — the good guy, founder, righteous one
2. **The Liar** — once an insider, broke off, teaches "smooth things," opposing the Teacher
3. **The Wicked Priest** — the outside Jerusalem-establishment enemy who comes to destroy the Teacher
4. **The community** — the faithful remnant

This set-piece is so generic it can be mapped onto almost any sectarian conflict. Tabor's example: David Koresh (Teacher), Marc Breault (Liar — Koresh's brilliant student who turned), FBI/government (Wicked Priests), the Branch Davidians in 1993. The pattern fits. This is why John the Baptist gets proposed as Teacher (Barbara Thiering), James as Teacher (Eisenman), or various Hasmonean figures (mainstream). It's a fluid template, which is part of why scholarship has been so contentious.

### Jodi Magness, the Archaeology, and the Wise Convergence

Tabor walks through Jodi Magness's *The Archaeology of Qumran and the Dead Sea Scrolls* — the standard work — and how the archaeology has shifted to fit Wise's textual dating:

- **Iron Age (8th-7th c. BCE):** Some kind of desert fortress — not the sect. (Agreed.)
- **Period 1a (de Vaux):** Virtually no coin or pottery evidence. Magness argues this period of habitation is doubtful. De Vaux invented it to fit the Damascus Document's "390 years after Nebuchadnezzar" timeline — Magness says that's reading the math too literally.
- **Period 1b (~100 BCE - 31 BCE earthquake):** Abundant coin evidence. **The flourishing.**
- **Period 2 (~4 BCE - 68 CE):** Resettlement after Herod's death. The community continues but produces fewer new texts.

Wise's textual count by decade matches: 5 texts from 75-65 BCE, 9 from 65-55, **32 from 55-45**, 81 from 45-35 (32 old + 49 new), then a steep drop. Texts pile up around the Teacher's death and the prophesied end window, then drop off as the prophecies fail. The community then enters what Tabor calls a "dark age" from Herod onward — they're still there archaeologically, but producing little, possibly merging with adjacent baptizing/messianic streams. **This is the bridge to John, Jesus, and James.**

### The Qumran Strategy When Prophecy Fails

When the predicted End didn't come at the close of the 70-week / 10-jubilee Daniel timeline, the Qumran community did something unusual. Most apocalyptic groups either (a) reset the date, (b) spiritualize the fulfillment ("it already happened invisibly"), or (c) give up.

Qumran did **none of these**. Instead they quoted Habakkuk 2:3 — "if it tarries, wait for it; it will surely come" — and said: **"We were right. Our interpretations were not wrong. God has extended the time beyond anything written, and now we don't know when."**

Tabor notes this same move shows up in Acts 1:6-7. The disciples ask: "Will you at this time restore the kingdom to Israel?" — still expecting the Davidic kingdom, still apocalyptic, still concrete. Jesus's answer: "It is not for you to know the times and the seasons which the Father has set." Same strategy. **No re-dating, no spiritualization, no abandonment — just God-extended-the-clock.** Tabor calls this the "Qumran strategy" and tracks it later into Sanhedrin 98 in the Babylonian Talmud where the rabbis eventually say no more date-setting.

### Eisenman's Big Books — A Reading Plan

For students who want to engage Eisenman, Tabor lays out the canonical sequence:

1. **1984 — *Maccabees, Zadokites, Christians and Qumran*** (small, technical)
2. **1986 — *James the Just in the Habakkuk Pesher*** (the thunderbolt — claims the Teacher is James)
3. **1996 — *The Dead Sea Scrolls and the First Christians*** (combines the first two with new essays and translations; Tabor recommends this volume above all for the introductory essay where Eisenman lays it all out)
4. **1997 — *James the Brother of Jesus*** (his most famous book; 1000 pages; brutally difficult to read because of Eisenman's prose style)
5. **The abridged *James the Brother of Jesus*** (400 pages, edited by a student; far more accessible)
6. **Neil Silberman's *The Hidden Scrolls / Christianity, Judaism, and the War for the Dead Sea Scrolls*** — Silberman, a great writer, spent time with Eisenman at Tabor's urging and emerged sympathetic; covers the whole Scrolls release saga in narrative form

Tabor's recommendation: read the 1996 introductory essay, then Silberman, then dive into the abridged James book if you want more.

### Jesus, John, and James as Continuation of a Qumran-Type Tradition

This is Tabor's synthesis position. He's not arguing Jesus, John, or James were card-carrying members of the Qumran community. He's arguing they're downstream of the same apocalyptic-messianic stream:

- **Shared profile:** apocalyptic, messianic, prepare-the-way-in-the-wilderness, new-covenant language, expecting the Davidic kingdom, scriptural orientation
- **John the Baptizer:** the strongest Qumran-type evidence — wilderness, baptism, ascetic diet of "what grows of itself" (the Qumran food rule, also found in Slavonic Josephus — Tabor connects this to modern "I only eat unprocessed food" intuitions)
- **Jesus:** more open, more Hillel-style — "my yoke is easy, my burden is light" — but still in the same broader stream. Q-source material shows Jesus calling John "more than a prophet" and "none greater born of women" — he's exalting the Qumran-style ascetic even while teaching differently.
- **James the Just:** the Letter of James upholds the Torah — "whoever offends in one command is guilty of all" — sounds very Matthew 5:17. James is the bridge figure carrying the Torah-zealous strand forward after Jesus.
- **Gospel of Thomas saying 12:** "Go to James the Just, for whom heaven and earth came to be." Suggests a successor framework that fits Eisenman's Teacher-figure analogy even if the strict identification fails on dating.

So the picture is: **Qumran is the upstream tradition; John-Jesus-James are downstream streams of the same broader Judaism.** Not identical. But related, recognizable, and continuous.

### Hillel-Style vs. Shammai-Style Qumran

Tabor's most interesting personal move in the lecture. Reading the Scrolls, Tabor finds two registers: a strict, ritually-rigorous tone in some texts (Community Rule, Damascus Document, some Cave 4 material), and a high-idealism devotional tone in others (the Thanksgiving Hymns). He proposes that Qumran-type Judaism, like Pharisaism, may have had **a Hillel-style and a Shammai-style** — a kinder gentler version and a strict ritualistic version.

If so, Jesus's "my yoke is easy" isn't a rejection of the Qumran tradition — it's a Hillel-style Qumran. Same apocalyptic preparation. Same messianic expectation. Same prepare-the-way-in-the-wilderness program. But without the ritual-purity obsessions of the strict wing. This puts Jesus, John, and James in a recognizable spiritual genealogy that the Western church has spent 1700 years denying.

## Misconceptions Addressed

| Misconception | Tabor's Response |
|---------------|------------------|
| "There was one unified 'Judaism' in the first century that Jesus broke from" | False. Late Second Temple Judaism was a contested field of competing 'Judaisms' — Pharisees (Hillel and Shammai), Sadducees, Essenes, Zealots, baptizers, Qumran community, apocalyptic groups. Jesus is operating *inside* this matrix, not against it. |
| "The Qumran community were Jewish monks — quietist, pacifist, contemplative" | This is the early-Catholic-scholar (de Vaux era) projection. The Scrolls themselves show a messianic, apocalyptic, nationalistic, Torah-zealous, anti-Roman movement preparing the way in the wilderness. Eisenman is right about this against the early establishment. |
| "Eisenman is a crank who should be ignored" | Wrong twice over. Mainstream scholars dismiss him without giving him a hearing. The dating problem (CE vs BCE) is real, but his substantive description of the movement's character is largely correct and should be engaged. |
| "James the Just IS the Teacher of Righteousness" (Eisenman) | Tabor disagrees. Paleographic, archaeological, and historical-event evidence all place the Teacher in the first century BCE (~70 BCE death). No first-century-CE figures or events are referenced in the Scrolls — impossible if the flourishing were that late. |
| "The Qumran community died out in 73 CE and is unrelated to early Christianity" | Tabor: the community continues archaeologically through Period 2 (4 BCE - 68 CE), and the John-Jesus-James movement is plausibly a downstream successor stream of Qumran-type Judaism, not a parallel sect. |
| "Resurrection in Mark/Luke and in Paul are different ideas" | Tabor: the "neither marrying nor giving in marriage / sons of the resurrection / like angels" framework appears in both Mark 12 and Luke 20 — and is exactly Paul's framework in 1 Cor 15. The Jewish apocalyptic transformation tradition runs through both. |
| "Paul is the anti-Jewish accommodating pro-Roman pacifist of Eisenman's caricature" | Tabor's nuance: Eisenman is right that Paul *became* this in later church theology (Augustine, Luther). But the historical Paul in his seven undisputed letters is a Jewish apocalyptic mystic expecting the end any day, demanding strict moral conduct, threatening divine judgment. |
| "Carbon-14 dating settles the dating dispute" | Tabor: AMS/C-14 dating "doesn't tell us as much as we had hoped" because of contamination issues. Paleography remains the more reliable method, and paleographic dating supports Wise. |
| "Josephus tells us the whole truth about the Essenes" | No. Josephus is writing the *War* from Vespasian's palace in Rome — he LIKES the Essenes and protects them by hiding their apocalyptic-militant character. He omits Jesus, James, and John from the *War* (where they belong) and only mentions them later in the *Antiquities* — by which time the threat has passed. |
