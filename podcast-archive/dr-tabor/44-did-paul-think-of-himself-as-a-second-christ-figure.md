## Summary

This is the most direct, head-on treatment of the "Paul as second Christ" thesis in the entire Tabor playlist. Where Video #05 introduces the theme and Video #24 frames it through the ascent narrative, this conversation with Derek Lambert lays out the textual evidence in concentrated form. Tabor states the claim baldly: Paul understood himself as a kind of *second anointed one* — small-c christ — paralleling Jesus's mission to Israel with his own mission to the nations, sharing in Christ's sufferings, and personally identified in Isaiah 49 as the servant called from the womb. This is not a peripheral feature of Paul's self-understanding. It is the engine of his apostolic identity.

The textual anchors are tight and specific. **Galatians 1:15** — "God who called me from my mother's womb" — places Paul in the company of only two other biblical figures who claim womb-calling: Jeremiah (the prophet to the nations) and Jesus himself. Tabor then reads **Isaiah 49** aloud in the first person *as Paul*, demonstrating how naturally the servant-song fits Paul's autobiography: formed in the womb, mouth like a sharp sword, hidden in the shadow of God's hand, sent to gather Jacob *and* to be a light to the nations to the ends of the earth. This is the same passage Paul echoes verbatim in Galatians 1. Tabor argues Paul read Isaiah 49 as describing two servants: one to Israel (Jesus, the circumcision) and one to the nations (Paul, the uncircumcision). The implication: Paul saw himself as personally prophesied in scripture, set apart cosmically, with a redemptive job that Jesus could not finish alone.

The suffering passages reinforce the claim. **Colossians 1:24** ("I fill up what is lacking in the afflictions of Christ in my flesh") — which Tabor concedes is deutero-Pauline but is genuinely Pauline in idea — coheres with the undisputed **Galatians 6:17** ("I bear in my body the *stigmata* of the Lord Jesus") and **1 Corinthians 11:1** ("Follow me as I follow Christ"). Paul is not modeling humble discipleship. He is announcing that his own suffering is the redemptive continuation of Christ's, that his own body bears the marks of crucifixion, and that to imitate him is the way to imitate Christ. The conversation closes with Tabor connecting this to Jason Staples's restoration-of-Israel framework (the Gentile mission is the regathering of scattered Israel), the apocalyptic worldview Paul shared with his Jerusalem contemporaries (demons, principalities, Cosmic Redemption), and the bold conclusion: Paul thought Jesus inaugurated the redemption — Paul is finishing it.

## Key Teachings

### Called from the Womb — Paul's Self-Identification with Jeremiah and Jesus

The phrase "called from my mother's womb" appears in only three places in the biblical tradition: Jeremiah 1, applied to Jeremiah himself; Luke 1, applied implicitly to Jesus through the infancy narrative; and Galatians 1:15, where Paul applies it to himself. This is not casual rhetoric. Womb-calling is the highest form of prophetic commissioning — the claim that God set you apart before you were even formed, that your mission was decided cosmically before your conscious life began. Paul is placing himself in a category with Jeremiah and Jesus, not below them. And like Jeremiah, Paul's commission is specifically to "the nations" — not primarily to Israel, but to the Gentiles.

Tabor pauses on this carefully. "Conversion" is the wrong word for Paul's Damascus experience, he insists, because Paul didn't change religions. Paul came to a *conviction* that the crucified Jesus was the exalted Messiah — but his mission, his calling, his identity were established in the womb. The Damascus event is the moment that calling activated, not the moment it began.

### Isaiah 49 Read in the First Person as Paul

Tabor performs an extended exegetical move in the interview: he opens his Bible to Isaiah 49 and reads it aloud "as Paul," imagining Paul speaking the passage to Derek about his own mission. The passage describes a servant "called from the womb," whose mouth is "like a sharp sword," who is "hidden in the shadow of God's hand," who is "made a polished arrow in his quiver," who is sent first to gather Jacob/Israel — and then, "is it too light a thing," sent also to the nations to bring salvation "to the end of the earth." The passage also speaks of suffering, of being "deeply despised" and "abhorred by the nations."

Tabor's claim: every detail fits Paul's autobiography. The womb-calling matches Galatians 1:15. The sword imagery matches Paul's "sword of the spirit" language. The hiddenness matches Paul's three years in Arabia. The double mission (Jews first, then nations) matches Paul's settled practice of starting in the synagogue and then turning to the Gentiles. The suffering matches Paul's catalogues of beatings, shipwrecks, and imprisonments in 2 Corinthians. Paul didn't just admire Isaiah 49. **He read it as describing him personally.** And he read the servant-song tradition as containing *two* servants — one for Israel (Jesus), one for the nations (Paul).

### Filling Up What Is Lacking in the Afflictions of Christ

The Colossians 1:24 verse — "I fill up in my flesh what is lacking in the afflictions of Christ for the sake of his body, the church" — is theologically explosive. It implies Christ's suffering was *incomplete*, and that Paul's suffering is completing it. Tabor concedes Colossians is technically deutero-Pauline (most scholars don't consider it among the seven undisputed letters), but he argues the *idea* is genuinely Pauline. The students and followers who wrote Colossians were transmitting Paul's actual theology. And the same logic shows up in undisputed Paul: "I bear in my body the *stigmata* of the Lord Jesus" (Galatians 6:17); "I am being poured out as a libation upon the sacrificial offering of your faith" (Philippians 2:17); "always carrying in the body the death of Jesus, so that the life of Jesus may also be manifested in our bodies" (2 Corinthians 4:10).

Paul is not just commemorating Christ's suffering. He is *extending* it. His own afflictions are the redemptive continuation of Christ's afflictions. This is co-redemptive language, and it is at odds with later Protestant theology in which Christ's atonement is complete and sufficient at the cross. For Paul, the redemption is staged: Jesus inaugurated it; Paul is finishing it; the parousia will consummate it.

### "Follow Me as I Follow Christ" — Paul as the Replicable Pattern

The phrase from 1 Corinthians 11:1 reads as casual exhortation in modern translations, but in the context of Paul's "second Christ" self-understanding it carries more weight. Paul is not saying "imitate my generic faithfulness." He is saying: **the path to Christ runs through me.** I have walked the road; my body bears the marks; my suffering parallels his suffering; therefore to imitate me is to walk the same road Christ walked. In other contexts Paul amplifies this — "be imitators of me, brothers" (Philippians 3:17), "you became imitators of us and of the Lord" (1 Thessalonians 1:6) — placing himself between Christ and the church as the operative middle term.

Tabor's reading: this is not modesty masquerading as boldness. It is exactly what it sounds like. Paul is positioning himself as the secondary anointed figure, the second Christ (small c), the one whose life is the replicable template for what believers are called to become.

### The Apocalyptic Cosmos — Demons, Principalities, and the Powers

A long aside in the interview clarifies the worldview Paul operated in. Paul was a fundamentalist of his time, Tabor says — meaning he genuinely inhabited the demonological cosmos of first-century apocalyptic Judaism. The world is "thickly populated" with cosmic forces: principalities, powers, rulers of this age, demons, angels of Satan. When Paul's plans are frustrated, "Satan hindered me" (1 Thessalonians 2:18). When the rulers of this age crucified Jesus, they "would not have crucified the Lord of glory" if they had known the cosmic plan (1 Corinthians 2:8). The "powers that be" are real entities, not metaphors.

This matters for the second-Christ framework because Paul's mission isn't just about teaching ethics or saving individual souls. It is about **defeating the cosmic powers**, putting all things under Christ's feet (1 Corinthians 15:24-28), conquering Decay and Death themselves. Paul is participating in a cosmic war whose stakes are the transformation of the entire material order. His sufferings are not just personal afflictions — they are part of the redemptive process that conquers the powers and births the new creation.

### Jason Staples and the Restoration-of-Israel Reading

Tabor enthusiastically endorses Jason Staples's (Bart Ehrman's former PhD student) reading of Romans 9-11. Staples argues that Paul's Gentile mission is actually the prophesied **regathering of scattered Israel** — specifically the lost northern tribes who became "not my people" (Hosea 1-2) after the Assyrian deportations, assimilating among the nations. When Paul writes "the fullness of the Gentiles" (Romans 11:25), he is using the phrase from Genesis 48:19 about Ephraim becoming "a multitude of nations" — a technical reference to scattered northern-kingdom descendants returning.

The implication for Paul's self-understanding is profound. Paul is not founding a new religion replacing Judaism. He is the apostolic figure prophesied to gather the scattered Israelites among the nations *back* into the covenant — alongside a fullness of righteous Gentiles attaching to them. This is the second-stage redemption Jesus inaugurated but did not finish. Jesus went to the Jews of the southern kingdom (Judah); Paul goes to the descendants of the northern kingdom mixed among the nations. Together they complete the restoration of "all Israel" — which is the precondition for the parousia.

### Stage One Inaugurated, Stage Two in Progress, Stage Three at the Parousia

Tabor structures Paul's theology as a three-stage redemption. **Stage One:** Jesus inaugurates the redemption through his ministry, death, resurrection, and exaltation — but does not finish it. **Stage Two:** Paul carries out the regathering of scattered Israel and the fullness of the Gentiles, sharing in Christ's sufferings to complete what Christ began. **Stage Three:** the parousia, the cosmic transformation, the new heavens and new earth, the glorification of believers into the family of God ruling above the angels.

In this framework, Paul is not a missionary explaining a finished gospel. He is the active agent of stage two. His mission is the gating event. Until Paul completes the Gentile mission, the parousia cannot come. This is what makes him the most important person on earth in his own self-understanding — and what makes his suffering, his afflictions, his stigmata, his womb-calling, his Isaiah 49 commission all parts of a single coherent (and very high) self-conception.
