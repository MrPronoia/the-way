## Summary

This is Part 2 of Tabor's conversation with Paul Williams, picking up two days after Part 1 (#51) and pivoting from Paul's "Earth to Heaven" thesis to its sharpest test case: the resurrection of Jesus. The argument lands hard. **The earliest first-person testimony we possess of the risen Jesus is Paul's — and what Paul describes is a vision, not a corpse-encounter.** Paul himself places his experience as the last in a series — appearances to Peter, to James, to the 500, "and last of all, as if to one untimely born, he appeared to me." All of these, on Tabor's reading, are of a single type: visionary manifestations of glory, presence, and voice. None of them are physical resuscitation. The later gospel encounters — Thomas's finger in the wounds, Luke's Jesus eating fish — are the product of decades of apologetic embellishment as second-century skeptics like Celsus pushed back: "everybody has visions; prove it was physical."

Williams sharpens the case with two observations Tabor agrees with. First, in one of the three Acts accounts of Paul's Damascus road experience, the companions hear a voice but see nothing — exactly what you'd expect of a private religious vision, the kind reported across history by Catholics seeing the Virgin Mary or mystics encountering other figures. Second, the risen Jesus only ever appears to existing disciples. Never to Pilate. Never to a Sanhedrin member. Never to a Pharisee or Sadducee. If it had been a public, corporeal event, where is the outside testimony? And even *inside* the inner circle, Matthew 28 says plainly: "some doubted" — after seeing him. That is not how publicly resuscitated corpses behave. It is exactly how visionary experiences within a grief-shattered community behave.

Tabor then reconstructs the timeline. Paul's "transformation" framework from 1 Corinthians 15 (the word is *metamorphosis*, like caterpillar to butterfly) treats resurrection as apotheosis — the dust-body dissolving and the glorified spirit-being emerging. Drawing on the Gospel of Peter ("we wept and cried for seven days... I'm going fishing"), Tabor reconstructs the disciples fleeing to Galilee in despair, *then* having overwhelming visionary experiences of Jesus's continuing presence. Paul has his own version seven to ten years later. The gospel trajectory — Mark with no appearances at all (the Transfiguration *is* Mark's resurrection moment), Matthew's misty mountain encounter with built-in doubt, then Luke and John progressively concretizing the body — reflects apologetic pressure, not historical memory. Tabor's favorite reconstructed passage: the first ten verses of John 20. Stop reading at verse 10 and you have the most natural account — Mary alone at an empty tomb, a gardener, fear, departure. Everything after that, including Thomas, is the embellishment layer.

## Key Teachings

### The Earliest Resurrection Testimony Is a Vision, Not a Corpse-Encounter

Paul's letters are chronologically the first first-person witness we have to the risen Jesus. Paul himself describes a sequence of "appearances" — to Peter, to the Twelve, to the 500, to James, to all the apostles, "and last of all to me." Williams presses the point: Paul is *placing himself in the same category* as the others. He is the last in a series of identical-in-kind experiences. So whatever Paul saw — and one of the Acts accounts says his companions heard a voice but saw nothing — is the same thing Peter and James and the Twelve saw.

That is **categorically different** from the resurrection scenes in Luke and John. Luke has Jesus eating broiled fish. John has Thomas putting his finger into the wound and Jesus telling him "blessed are those who have not seen and yet believed." Those scenes belong to a later, embellished layer. The chronologically earliest layer — Paul's own description — is visionary.

### Why "Vision" Doesn't Mean "Just in Their Head"

Tabor pushes back on the lazy reduction that calling these experiences visionary means dismissing them as hallucinations. Paul himself, in 2 Corinthians 12 (the ascent-to-paradise passage), says: **"whether in the body or out of the body, I do not know."** Paul is not a naive enthusiast. He is reporting a genuine, indisputable, indescribable experience — light, presence, voice, glory — and he is openly agnostic about its ontological mechanics.

"If you tell me a spiritual experience you had, or even something that seemed fairly corporeal — like people talk about seeing a person that's died who just seems to appear and then disappear — I don't know if that's in your head or not. Most skeptics would say it is. But do we know all the mysteries of the universe?"

The disciples' experience, on this reading, was real to them — and overwhelming — without being a resuscitated corpse. That is what visionary means.

### The Tell: Appearances Only to Insiders, and Even Insiders Doubted

Williams names what becomes the load-bearing observation of the conversation. The risen Jesus appears **only** to existing disciples. Never to Pilate. Never to a Sanhedrin member. Never to a Pharisee or Sadducee. Never to a Roman soldier. Never to a neutral party.

If it had been a publicly resuscitated body walking around Jerusalem, where is the outside testimony? Where is Pilate's astonished report? Where is the Sanhedrin's emergency meeting? Where is Josephus on this?

Worse, even **inside** the circle of true believers, Matthew 28:17 admits: *"but some doubted."* Doubted what — after seeing the risen Jesus in person? Tabor: "Not only is it just the inner group, but even some of the inner group didn't believe it after they had seen Jesus." That is exactly what you'd expect from visionary, partial, tentative appearances within a grief-shattered community. It is exactly what you would *not* expect from a publicly resuscitated corpse.

### Resurrection as Metamorphosis, Not Resuscitation

Paul's word for what happens to the dead is *metamorphosis* — the same root behind caterpillar-to-butterfly. The dust-body (the Hebrew *nephesh*, the "breathing being" we share with animals) dissolves. What emerges is a new genus of being — glorified, immortal, powerful, *body* in the sense that you are an active being with motive existence, but not a body in the sense of two arms and two legs and a face.

Paul mixes two metaphors in 2 Corinthians 5: you live in a *tent* (Platonic echo) which you exit for a permanent building; and you wear *clothes* which you exchange for new ones. His hope is **not** to be "found naked" (disembodied) — but to be re-clothed, or even further clothed (skipping the naked/dead state entirely). It is dualistic — you are not your body — but it ends in an embodied state, just a transformed one.

### How the Empty Tomb Fits a Vision Framework (Dematerialization, Not Revival)

Tabor disagrees mildly with Ehrman and Crossan that the empty tomb is a late legend. He thinks Paul's framework can accommodate an empty tomb — just not in the way modern Christians picture it.

Picture a Herodian rock-cut tomb. A body wrapped in a shroud, laid on the *arcosolium* niche. Tabor describes finding the only shrouded first-century remains ever discovered, hiking with archaeologist Shimon Gibson in the Valley of Hinnom. He pictures Paul imagining: **some kind of energy field in the tomb, the physical remains transmogrifying or dematerializing, only some shroud residue left.** You walk in and ask "Where is he?" — not because a corpse stood up and left, but because the dust-body has been transformed into something that no longer occupies that space.

Following Crossan's provocation: in principle, early Christians could have venerated the tomb *with a corpse still in it* and said "this is testimony to the resurrection; he left his tent behind." The body is irrelevant. The glorified Christ at the right hand of God is the point. Does the glorified Jesus need lungs? A stomach? Organs to respirate? Of course not.

### The Despair-Then-Vision Reconstruction (Gospel of Peter)

Tabor reconstructs the actual timeline by reading the non-canonical Gospel of Peter against the canonical accounts. After the crucifixion, the disciples **wept and mourned for seven days.** Then Peter says, "I'm going fishing" — and the fragment breaks off.

"Why would you be weeping and crying if you're meeting with Jesus regularly and having meals with him? You wouldn't."

That fits a despair-then-vision arc, not a we-just-had-breakfast-with-the-risen-Jesus arc. The disciples flee to Galilee broken. *Then* the overwhelming visionary experiences begin — the Galilee mountain encounters of Matthew 28, the Galilee fishing scene of John 21 are the geographical fingerprint. The Jerusalem-resurrection-Sunday narrative compresses what was probably a longer, messier process. Paul has his own version seven to ten years later, shows up to the others (who are initially terrified of him), and they accept his testimony precisely because his behavior backs it up — his life completely changes.

### The Gospel Embellishment Trajectory

Tabor sketches the chronological development of how the resurrection got concretized:

- **Mark (earliest):** **No appearances at all.** The Transfiguration in Mark 9 *is* Mark's resurrection moment — a *prolepsis*, a foretaste. The young man at the empty tomb says "you will see him in Galilee, as he said." That's pointing back to Mark 9's glorified Christ, not forward to a campfire reunion.
- **Matthew:** Two appearances. The mountain encounter (Matthew 28) is **misty** — clouds wafting, "some doubted" even after seeing him. Probably set on the same high mountain as the Transfiguration. Galilee, not Jerusalem. Still visionary in tone.
- **Paul's own experience (~7-10 years after the crucifixion, earlier in writing than the gospels):** A blinding-light vision. He shows up to the others terrified of him; they accept his testimony. Says he saw Jesus, heard a voice — but admits he doesn't know if it was in or out of the body.
- **Luke (much later):** Now Jesus eats broiled fish. The corpse needs to be *demonstrably* physical because skeptics are pushing back.
- **John (latest):** Thomas can put his finger in the wounds. Jesus eats. **And yet** — Jesus also walks through locked doors and just kind of disappears, like an angelic epiphany (compare Gideon and the angel in Judges 6). The author is trying to have it both ways: physical enough for apologetics, spiritual enough to fit the early tradition.

The trajectory is unmistakable. **Vision → embellished physical → resuscitated corpse with a side of "but also spiritual."** Tabor: this is "strictly for apologetic purposes."

### Why Mark Has No Appearances: The Transfiguration as Mark's Resurrection

A subtle theological move that gets overlooked: Mark — the earliest gospel — has *no* resurrection appearances. The original ending is the women fleeing the empty tomb in fear. That isn't a mistake or a lost ending. It is a theological choice.

Mark's resurrection moment is **the Transfiguration in Mark 9.** Right after Mark 9:1 — "some standing here will not taste death till they see the kingdom of God come with power" — Jesus goes up the high mountain with Peter, James, and John and is transfigured into the glorified Christ they will see. The promise "you will see him in Galilee, as he said" at the empty tomb points back to Mark 9, not forward to a future fish-fry.

For Mark, the resurrection *is* the transfiguration. The glorified Christ is what they saw. That is the early, pre-apologetic Christology — and it lines up perfectly with what Paul describes as his own experience: glory, light, presence, voice. Not a resuscitated body.

### The Cleanest Empty-Tomb Account Is John 20:1-10

Tabor's favorite reconstruction passage: **John 20, verses 1 through 10 only.** Mary Magdalene goes alone, early, to the tomb. The stone is already rolled away. She thinks someone has taken the body. She sees a gardener. She runs away afraid. Two disciples come, see the tomb is empty, and leave.

That is the natural, undecorated account. Stop reading at verse 10. Everything after that — the risen Jesus appearing, Thomas touching wounds, John 21's breakfast on the beach — is the embellishment layer. Tabor argues those scenes were added to satisfy the apologetic need to *prove* the resurrection against second-century skeptics like Celsus who pointed out that visions don't count as evidence.
