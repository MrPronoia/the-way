## Summary

This is the keystone video in Tabor's Death & Afterlife series — the ninth installment, where he names the problem outright. Modern Christianity, anciently and today, has largely abandoned the actual eschatology of Jesus and Paul in favor of a **Platonic dualism** that says you have an immortal soul that escapes the body at death and flies up to heaven. That framework — soul-as-prisoner, body-as-cage, death-as-friend, heaven-as-true-home — is not Jewish. It is not Pauline. It is not what Jesus taught. It comes from Plato (via the entire Hellenistic religious atmosphere), and the early church fused it onto Pauline language until almost nobody could see the seam anymore.

Tabor builds the lecture around **Oscar Cullmann's 1955 Ingersoll Lectures at Harvard** — *Immortality of the Soul or Resurrection of the Dead?* Cullmann's thesis triggered hate mail when he gave it: that 1 Corinthians 15, the so-called "resurrection chapter," has been sacrificed to Plato's *Phaedo*. Cullmann argued — and Tabor agrees — that "primitive" (early) Christianity's view of resurrection of the dead is in **sharp contrast** to the Greek idea that the soul is intrinsically immortal. Later Christian theologians forged a fusion between the two, equating them, and that fusion is what most Christians inherit today as "the Christian view of the afterlife." It isn't. It's mostly Plato wearing Christian vocabulary.

Tabor walks through the contrast in detail. The Hebrew/Jesus/Paul view: humans are *living breathers* (nephesh chayyah) made of dust, mortal by nature, who at death return to dust while a shadow-self rests in Sheol/Hades awaiting the resurrection of the dead at the end of the age — a **cosmic event**, not a private soul-flight. Death is an **enemy** (the last enemy to be destroyed, per 1 Cor 15), not a friend. The body is part of the good creation. The new age is a *re-clothing*, a metamorphosis, a putting on of immortality like new clothing — not the soul finally escaping the prison of flesh. The Platonic view (which has colonized Christian funerals, hymns, and pulpit clichés): the body is a prison, the soul is intrinsically immortal, death liberates, heaven is home, the earth is exile, asceticism is the ideal. Tabor's verdict: when the preacher at the casket says "Charles is in heaven now; this is just the shell" and then walks to the graveside and reads "on that last great day the dead will rise" — those are two completely different religions, and most Christians don't notice.

## Key Teachings

### The Three-Decker Hebrew Universe vs. the Hellenistic Cosmos

The Hebrew view is a three-decker universe: Sheol (the underworld, where shadow-selves of the dead exist in a sleeping/dust state — *not* hell, not torture, not annihilation), the flat earth with its firmament and stars, and heaven above where Yahweh dwells. Eschatology adds a *time arrow* to this picture: this age, then the end-of-this-age, then the new age — kingdom of God, final judgment, resurrection of the dead.

The Hellenistic cosmos (Plato onward, roughly 400 BCE) is vastly expanded: earth at the bottom, planetary spheres ascending (moon, Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Sun), then the heavenly world beyond the seventh heaven. Souls are *points of light* that have fallen down through these spheres into the world of darkness. Humans are trapped — by the body below and by the planetary powers (which Paul calls *stoicheia tou kosmou*, the elemental spirits) above.

**The two cosmologies generate two completely different soteriologies.** Salvation in the Hellenistic world = escape, ascent, getting out. Salvation in the Hebrew/Pauline world = cosmic transformation, the new creation, resurrection-at-the-end-of-the-age. These are not compatible. The church merged them anyway.

### Nephesh Chayyah — The "Living Soul" Mistranslation

Genesis 2:7: God breathes into Adam the Breath of Life and Adam becomes a "living soul" (KJV). This is one of the most consequential mistranslations in Bible history. The Hebrew is *nephesh chayyah* — literally "a living breather." And the **exact same phrase** is used for all the land animals, the insects, the reptiles. People read the King James and think humans have an immortal soul that animals don't have. The Hebrew says no such thing. A human is a living breather. A dog is a living breather. A cricket is a living breather. We are all dust creatures. We all return to dust.

This single mistranslation has done more theological damage than almost any other — because it smuggled Plato's anthropology into Genesis 2.

### Sheol Is Not Hell — And Hades Just Means "the Unseen"

Sheol in the Hebrew Bible is not torture, not punishment, not annihilation. It is a sleeping/shadowy state — "sleeping in the dust" is the recurring metaphor in both Testaments. The dead are *shades* or *shadows* of their former selves, kept in the hand of God, awaiting resurrection. Hades is just the Greek word the Septuagint and New Testament use for the same concept — *hades* literally means "the unseen realm." Neither word originally carried any of the medieval Christian baggage of fire, devils, and pitchforks. That came later, mostly from fusing Platonic underworld imagery with Christian eschatology.

### Cullmann's Thesis — 1 Cor 15 Has Been Sacrificed to the Phaedo

Tabor anchors the whole lecture in **Oscar Cullmann's 1954-55 Ingersoll Lectures at Harvard**: *Immortality of the Soul or Resurrection of the Dead?* Cullmann argued that primitive Christianity's resurrection-of-the-dead doctrine is in **sharp contrast** to the Greek intrinsic-immortality-of-the-soul doctrine. The two were later fused by Christian theologians, and the fusion robs us of what's actually unique in early Christianity.

Cullmann took massive heat for this. Hate mail. "You've taken away our Christian hope." Cullmann's reply, often written by hand: "No, my dear friend. The Christian hope is that you sleep in the dust until the return of Jesus, who will raise you from the dead and give you this new glorified body. But you don't have it yet."

Cullmann moved in circles with Bultmann, Karl Barth, Schweitzer. He was the major Strasbourg / Basel New Testament scholar of the mid-20th century. The book is long out of print but Tabor strongly recommends finding a reprint.

### Death Is an Enemy, Not a Friend

In Plato's *Phaedo*, Socrates welcomes death. Death is a friend — the liberator who takes the soul out of the dark lower world and helps it ascend to the starry heavens. "Don't weep for me," Socrates tells his disciples; "weep for yourselves — you have to stay behind in this prison."

Paul could not be more opposite. In 1 Corinthians 15:26: **"The last enemy to be destroyed is death."** Death is not a welcome friend. Death is the final adversary that gets *defeated* at the end of the age, when this perishable puts on the imperishable and this mortal puts on immortality.

When Christian funerals talk about death as a "graceful passage home" or "going to be with Jesus in a better place" — that's Socrates, not Paul. Paul wanted to be **clothed**, not **naked**. He says so explicitly in 2 Corinthians 5: when you die you're naked, "and that is not what we want." Plato wants to be naked. Paul wants a new body.

### Re-Clothing, Not Escape — Paul's Metamorphosis

Paul's framework in 1 Corinthians 15 and 2 Corinthians 5: the body is a *mode of existence*, not a prison. When you die, your shadow-self rests in the hand of God but you have no mode of existence — you're "naked." At the parousia, the dead and the living together are **re-clothed** with the imperishable, immortal body. Paul uses *metamorphosis* — the Greek word that gives us the caterpillar-to-butterfly image, though Paul's preferred analogy is the seed: plant a seed, it dies, something different comes up. There's continuity but not resuscitation.

The point is: this is not soul-escapes-body. The Pauline self **needs** a body. The naked interim state between death and resurrection is **not the goal**. The goal is the new clothing — the powerful, glorious, immortal spiritual body at the end of the age.

### Resurrection Is a Cosmic Event, Not a Private Soul-Flight

Cullmann's first proposition (which Tabor walks through): "Resurrection of the dead and thus immortality or eternal life is based upon a **cosmic event**, not the natural dualistic state of human beings as an essential immortal soul imprisoned in a material perishable body."

This is the load-bearing point. The Platonic framework is *natural and individual*: you already have an immortal soul; it gets released at your private moment of death; it flies home. The Pauline/Jesus framework is *cosmic and collective*: nobody has immortality yet; at the parousia, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trumpet, **all the dead and the living together** are transformed in one cosmic act of new creation. Romans 8: the whole creation (*ktisis*) is in labor pains, subject to decay, awaiting its own release into a new earth where death itself is swallowed up.

You don't go to heaven when you die in Paul's framework. You sleep, and at the cosmic transformation you and the whole creation are remade together.

### The Body Is Good — Genesis 1 vs. The Cave

The Platonic view is body-denying and world-denying. The body is the cave, the prison, the muck. Asceticism is the moral ideal — feed the soul, starve the body, deny sexuality, deny pleasure, so the soul can be ready to depart upward.

The Hebrew/Genesis view is body-affirming and world-affirming. God called the creation good, good, good, very good. The first commandment in the Torah (says the rabbis, of the 613) is **"be fruitful and multiply and fill the earth."** Procreation is good. The earth is your home. The body is your mode of existence and integral to your personhood.

Tabor flags this as the deep cultural fork: most of Christianity's body-shame, sexuality-shame, world-shame, and matter-shame inherited from Plato. None of it is in Genesis. None of it is in Jesus's teaching about marriage and feasting. Even Paul's "suffering" language is about *being persecuted*, not about deliberately starving the body to feed the soul.

### The Funeral Contradiction Most Christians Don't Notice

The pastoral example Tabor drives home: at the typical Christian funeral, the preacher stands by the casket and says, "Don't weep — Charles is in heaven with Christ. His soul has gone to be with God. This is just the shell." Then everyone drives to the cemetery, and at the graveside the liturgy is **completely different**: "on that last great day, we believe the dead will come forth, immortalized in this new wonderful spiritual body."

Those are two incompatible eschatologies running in the same funeral, fifteen minutes apart. The first is Platonic immortality-of-the-soul. The second is Pauline resurrection-of-the-dead-at-the-parousia. The preacher doesn't notice. The mourners don't notice. The church has been doing this for 1700 years.

Why? Tabor's answer: **comfort**. Paul said "we who are alive" — easy enough to wait. But what if a thousand years go by? Two thousand? People want their loved ones already in heaven now, not asleep in the dust waiting. The immortal-soul comfort is irresistible. So it won.

### Where the Platonization Happened — Augustine and the Church Fathers

Tabor flags the next video (#15) as the deep dive on this, but signals the answer here: gnosticism through Augustine. Second through fifth century. By the time you get to Augustine's *City of God* and *Confessions*, the Pauline new-body cosmic-transformation framework has been thoroughly platonized into a soul-going-to-heaven framework. The church fathers had Plato in their bones (their entire intellectual culture was Hellenistic), and the fusion happened almost imperceptibly. They didn't think they were changing anything. They thought they were systematizing.

But they were following Plato more than they were following Paul or Jesus.

## Misconceptions Addressed

| Misconception | Tabor's Response |
|---------------|------------------|
| "You have an immortal soul that goes to heaven when you die" | This is Plato, not the Bible. Hebrew anthropology says you are a *nephesh chayyah* — a living breather, made of dust, who returns to dust. The shadow-self rests in Sheol; nobody has immortality yet. Immortality is something you *put on* at the end of the age. |
| "Soul and body are separate substances; the soul is the real you" | The Hebrew framework treats the person as a unity. The body is your *mode of existence*, not a costume the real self wears. That's why Paul wants to be *re-clothed*, not unclothed. |
| "Heaven is your true home; earth is exile" | This is Hellenistic and gnostic. Genesis says God made the earth and called it good. Humans were made *for* the earth — "be fruitful and multiply and fill the earth." The eschatological hope is a *new earth*, not escape from earth. |
| "Death is a friend, a graceful passage, going home" | Paul: "The last enemy to be destroyed is death." Death is the final adversary that gets *defeated* at the parousia. Christians have inherited Socrates's posture toward death and mistaken it for the gospel. |
| "Sheol = hell, where the wicked are tortured" | Sheol is a sleeping/shadow state — neither bliss nor torment. Hades just means "the unseen realm." The medieval hell-imagery comes from fusing Platonic underworld scenes with Christian eschatology centuries later. |
| "When believers die they go to be with Jesus in their new body" | They go to "be with" Jesus in the sense of resting in the hand of God — but the *new body* doesn't arrive until the parousia, the cosmic transformation. The interim is sleep. The clothing is later. |
| "Resurrection means a corpse coming back to life" | That's resuscitation (Lazarus, Jairus's daughter — all of whom die again). Resurrection (Pauline) is *metamorphosis* — the perishable putting on the imperishable. Not a corpse with a tune-up; a new mode of existence. |
| "The body is bad, sexuality is bad, asceticism is holy" | Pure Plato. Genesis 1 first commandment is "be fruitful and multiply." | "Jesus came to take you home to heaven" | Jesus and Paul both teach that *the kingdom comes to earth* (or the new earth comes) — not that humans leave earth for a disembodied heaven. The Lord's Prayer: "thy kingdom come, on earth as it is in heaven." |
| "Plato and Paul are basically saying the same thing in different words" | The fusion Cullmann attacks. They are saying *opposite* things on body, death, soul, salvation, and cosmology.
