# My First and Favorite Interview on My Book The Jesus Dynasty

**Speaker:** Dr. James Tabor (PhD, Univ. of Chicago; Professor Emeritus, UNC Charlotte)
**Interviewer:** Mike Collins (host, *Charlotte Talks*, WFAE 90.7 / WFHE 90.3 — Charlotte NPR)
**Source:** [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FgsW3SNyfsk)
**Published:** April 14, 2023 (interview originally aired the week after Palm Sunday, 2006)
**Duration:** 46 minutes
**Playlist:** Paul (#47 of 77)

## Summary

This is the 2006 NPR-affiliate interview Tabor calls his favorite presentation of *The Jesus Dynasty: The Hidden History of Jesus, His Royal Family, and the Birth of Christianity*. The book had been released the Friday before Palm Sunday — exactly 100 years after Albert Schweitzer's *Quest of the Historical Jesus* (1906) — and had landed Tabor on ABC's *Good Morning America*, *Nightline*, and *20/20*, the cover of *U.S. News & World Report*, the *New York Times* Best Seller list, eventual translation into 26 languages, and a denunciation from the pulpit of St. Paul's in New York on Easter Sunday by the Cardinal. Mike Collins of WFAE's *Charlotte Talks* gets a calmer, longer-form Tabor than the network-TV cuts, and Tabor uses the hour to lay out the actual thesis of the book: Jesus is best understood as a first-century Jewish messianic figure with a royal Davidic claim, working in tandem with John the Baptizer as the priestly half of a two-Messiah configuration drawn from Zechariah and the Dead Sea Scrolls; his physical family — Mary, his four brothers and two sisters, and especially James the brother — is central to the movement, not incidental; and his designated successor was James, not Peter and certainly not Paul.

The middle of the conversation does the heavy lifting on the parts of the book that drew the most heat. Tabor handles the paternity question carefully: historians can only investigate what historians can investigate, so the Virgin Birth is theology, not history. If Joseph wasn't the biological father (which the Gospels themselves imply), the ancient Jewish and Roman polemic naming a man called Pantera is at least worth surveying — but Tabor's actual interest is rehabilitating Mary as a real Jewish woman who raised seven children under hard conditions, who likely married Joseph's brother Clopas/Alphaeus after Joseph's early death (per Jewish levirate law), and whose other children included the James, Joses, Judas, and Simon named in the Gospels and the "two sisters" Mark mentions. Jesus's "missing years" Tabor places not in India or Egypt but at Sepphoris — the Herodian urban capital four miles from Nazareth, where Jesus would have worked as a *tekton* (better translated "builder" or "stone-mason" than "carpenter") and seen Roman wealth juxtaposed with Galilean poverty up close. His messianic agenda — drawn directly from Isaiah 11 — was six-fold: rule on David's throne, purge Jerusalem of foreign rulers, establish righteousness, separate sinners from Israel, extend the rule to the nations, gather the scattered tribes. The Triumphal Entry was prophetic pantomime fulfilling Zechariah 9, not Schonfield-style staged drama.

The last third gets to the part that triggered the cultural fight: James the brother of Jesus as the actual designated successor of the movement. Tabor argues this is not his invention — it's in the New Testament (Acts, Galatians 1-2, 1 Corinthians 15), in Eusebius via Hegesippus, in the Ebionite literature, in the early Christian historians, and only disappears because Paul's literary legacy (13 letters) eclipses James's single letter that "almost didn't make the canon." Paul, Tabor stresses, never met Jesus, came late, claimed direct revelation, and went a different theological direction than the Jerusalem leadership. The "beloved disciple" leaning on Jesus's breast at the Last Supper, Tabor speculates, was likely James — the kid brother Jesus turned his mother over to — not the fisherman John (who, after all, asks Jesus to call down fire from heaven and gets rebuked for "not knowing what spirit you are of"). A caller — Michael from Matthews — pushes back hard from a confessional Christian frame ("at the name of Jesus every knee shall bow"), and Tabor's gentle response models how he does this work: he isn't dismissing the New Testament, he's reading 90% of his evidence from it, but reading carefully and contextually. The closing reflection: "I see my work as trying to get closer to Jesus" — not destroying Christianity, but rehabilitating its forgotten Jewish founders.

## Key Teachings

### Jesus as Davidic Messiah, John the Baptist as Priestly Messiah — The Two-Messiah Configuration

The structural claim of *The Jesus Dynasty*. Drawing on Zechariah, the Dead Sea Scrolls (1QS, 4QTestimonia, the Damascus Document), the Mishnah, and the Talmud, Tabor argues Second Temple Jewish messianic expectation included **two** Messiahs operating in tandem: a priestly Messiah (sometimes called the Messiah of Aaron) and a kingly Messiah (the Messiah of David / the Messiah of Israel). The kingly figure does not stand alone — the priestly figure inaugurates and anoints him.

John the Baptizer, son of the priest Zechariah, fills the priestly slot. He's not just a "forerunner" who appears and disappears (as the Gospels' editorial framing implies) — he inaugurates the movement, declares the time fulfilled, and Jesus joins him as the Davidic figure. "All the pieces in place for this incredible messianic dynamic to work." Herod Antipas's response (arresting John, watching Jesus closely from Sepphoris) was not over-reaction — it was recognition.

This frame restores John to his historical centrality. The Gospels' rapid pivot ("here he is, here's the man, curtain down, never hear from him again") obscures what Tabor sees as the historical reality: John was Jesus's teacher, and Jesus honored him as such ("among those born of women none is greater than John").

### Jesus Saw Himself As The Davidic Messiah — Knowingly

Tabor is not arguing Jesus was a humble Galilean teacher whose followers later inflated him into Messiah. He's arguing the reverse: **Jesus consciously stepped into the Davidic role**, knew exactly what the Hebrew prophets said the Messiah would do, and acted out the prophecies deliberately. The Triumphal Entry is the clearest case:

- Zechariah 9:9 — "Lo, your king comes, lowly, riding on a donkey"
- Jesus arranges to ride into Jerusalem on a donkey, at Passover, with crowds shouting "Blessed is the kingdom of our father David"
- "Prophetic pantomime" — Jesus signaling to the culture in symbols the crowd understood, even if modern readers miss them

Tabor explicitly rejects Hugh Schonfield's *Passover Plot* reading (Jesus staged his death and survived). What he affirms is that Jesus was knowingly fulfilling a prophetic script. The temple action ("no traders in the house of the Lord") is another deliberate fulfillment. These are cultural signals — dangerous slogans to yell with Pilate sitting across the city at Passover. Jesus knew what he was doing.

### Six-Point Davidic Messianic Agenda from Isaiah 11

Tabor lays out the agenda Jesus believed the Messiah would carry out, drawn straight from Isaiah and the broader prophets:

1. Rule over Israel on the throne of David
2. Purge Jerusalem and the land of Israel of foreign rulers
3. Establish a rule of righteousness
4. Separate sinners from the people of Israel
5. Extend his rule to the Godless nations of the world
6. Gather together the scattered tribes of Israel

This is not the agenda of a man founding a new religion. It's the agenda of a Jewish messianic claimant who believes God is about to act through him to restore the kingdom. It's also — Tabor notes — what Jews are still waiting for the Messiah to do.

### Mary as a Real Jewish Mother of Seven — Not the Non-Sexual Queen of Heaven

Tabor's pastoral move on the paternity question. He explicitly does not want the conversation reduced to "did Jesus have a father?" — he wants to humanize Mary. The reconstruction:

- Mary had Jesus, biological father unknown (or named as Pantera in early Jewish and Roman polemic; Tabor surveys the evidence without endorsing)
- Mary married Joseph; bore him children
- Joseph died young — universally acknowledged in the tradition
- Per Jewish levirate law, Joseph's brother **Clopas/Alphaeus** (named in the Gospel of John and identified with the father of three of the Twelve in the Synoptic Apostle lists) married Mary
- Mary raised four boys and two girls beyond Jesus: James, Joses, Judas, Simon, and two unnamed sisters
- Three of "the Twelve" — James, Judas, and Simon — are the brothers, "children of Alphaeus/Clopas" in the Gospel lists

"I want to honor her memory historically as she was. She's not the non-sexual Queen of Heaven; she's a Jewish mother of seven children who lived a hard life and must have been extraordinarily gifted with these boys."

The Catholic and Eastern Orthodox traditions resolve the New Testament's "brothers of Jesus" references by making them cousins or Joseph's children from a prior marriage. Tabor argues the simplest reading is the right one: these were Jesus's physical siblings, and Mary remarried after Joseph's death.

### The Pantera Question — Survey, Not Endorsement

Tabor handles the Pantera tradition carefully and pastorally. The evidence:

- Multiple Jewish and Roman ancient sources name a man called Pantera as Jesus's biological father
- Tabor cites Gustaf Adolf Deissmann's 1906 work documenting this
- Pantera later becomes a Roman soldier (so at the time of Jesus's conception he would have been 14 or 15 — not yet a soldier)
- The tradition raises images of "gutter pregnancy" or assault that the sources do not actually support

Tabor's position: **historians can survey what ancient sources said, but we do not know what arrangement existed between Mary and Pantera, if any.** The Charlotte newspaper review of the book had claimed Tabor concluded Jesus's father was a Roman soldier; Tabor specifically denies making that claim. He surveys the ancient possibilities; he does not endorse one. His actual interest is rehabilitating Mary, not naming a father.

### Jesus the Tekton — Builder, Probably Stone-Mason, in Sepphoris

The "missing years" reconstruction. Greek *tekton* is conventionally translated "carpenter" but more accurately means "builder" or "artisan." Wood was rarely used in 1st-century Galilee (door frames, roof beams). Stone was the dominant building material.

Tabor's case for stone-mason:
- Jesus's parables and sayings are saturated with building imagery (foundations dug deep, blocks laid, "I will build my church")
- Nazareth was a tiny village with no work — population in the dozens
- Sepphoris, four miles north, was the Herodian urban capital of Galilee — being expanded throughout Jesus's childhood and young adulthood
- Tabor has excavated at Sepphoris with UNC Charlotte students for three seasons; the first-century foundations are still visible
- Day-laborer builders walked to Sepphoris from surrounding villages for work

Compelling implication: Jesus saw the wealth of the Herodian/Roman ruling class daily, juxtaposed against Galilean Jewish poverty. "Those who wear soft garments live in kings' palaces" — that's not abstract theology, it's an observation from a builder who walked past those palaces every working day. The class consciousness in Jesus's preaching has biographical roots.

### The Davidic Line Through Mary (Nathan, Not Solomon)

A technical point Tabor finds important. Jesus is "of David" through both parents — but the *line through Mary* is the legitimate one. The line through Joseph runs through Solomon and the Judean kings — but Jeremiah pronounced a curse on that line ("no descendant of Jeconiah shall prosper") because of their wickedness. Of the twenty-some kings of Judah, only Hezekiah and Josiah are praised; the rest were "very wicked."

Mary's line, given in Luke (in Tabor's reading — most scholars take Luke as Joseph's line, but Tabor finds the Mary-line reading defensible), runs through Solomon's *brother* Nathan. Same Davidic descent, no curse. This is the legitimate royal line — and it gave Jesus credibility with the Pharisees and the popular movement, even as the Romans hunted descendants of David (multiple Emperors, including Vespasian after the Jewish Revolt, conducted searches for Davidic descendants to eliminate Messianic claimants).

### James the Brother as Designated Successor

The thesis the book's title points to. After Jesus's death, leadership of the Jesus movement passed to **James the brother of Jesus** — not to Peter, not (for years) to Paul. The evidence Tabor draws on is biblical, not extra-biblical:

- Acts: James presides over the Jerusalem Council (Acts 15); James is the figure Paul reports to (Acts 21)
- Galatians 1-2: Paul goes to Jerusalem "to see James the Lord's brother"; "James, Cephas, and John" are the "pillars" (note the order — James first)
- 1 Corinthians 15: Resurrection appearances list includes "then he appeared to James"
- The single Letter of James in the New Testament
- Eusebius preserving Hegesippus on James as a vegetarian Nazirite who prayed in the Temple until his knees were calloused like a camel's
- The Ebionite and Nazarene literature treating James as the rightful successor

"Peter is very important but he's chief of the Twelve — James takes over more or less in the administrative place of Jesus."

The reason this is forgotten: Paul's literary legacy. 13 letters in the New Testament under Paul's name. One short letter under James's name that "almost didn't make the canon." When you weigh the textual mass, Paul wins by volume. But Tabor: "James was the guy in charge."

### The Beloved Disciple Was Probably James, Not John

A speculative but striking suggestion. The "disciple whom Jesus loved" who leans on Jesus's breast at the Last Supper, who stands at the cross while John flees, to whom Jesus hands over his mother ("Son, behold your mother") — Tabor argues this is more plausibly James the brother than John the fisherman.

Reasoning:
- Why would Jesus hand his mother over to a fisherman friend rather than to his own brother?
- John the fisherman's New Testament reputation is not actually flattering — he's "Son of Thunder," he asks Jesus to call fire from heaven on Samaritan villages ("you do not know what spirit you are of"), he and his brother lobby for the top seats in the kingdom
- The "beloved disciple" leaning on Jesus's breast is a posture of intimate family affection
- The Da Vinci Code popularized reading this figure as Mary Magdalene; Tabor proposes James the kid brother instead

This connects to Tabor's broader rehabilitation project: recover the family of Jesus — Mary, John the Baptizer (the co-worker), and James the brother — as the inner circle of the historical movement.

### Paul Came Late, Never Met Jesus, Went a Different Direction

The Paul material here is a précis of arguments Tabor develops elsewhere in the playlist (Videos #02, #04, #05, #10). The compressed version for the WFAE audience:

- Paul never met Jesus (no evidence; Paul himself never claims it; his "I received it not from man" in Galatians 1 explicitly cuts him off from the apostolic chain)
- Paul knew James — went to see him in Jerusalem ("I went up to see James the Lord's brother")
- Paul was Jewish but "not part of the movement" originally — he came to it through visions on the Damascus road
- Paul's gospel was driven by his own visions and revelations — a different theological vision than James's
- "Everything we feel Christianity is today essentially comes from Paul, not from James"
- Christianity as a Trinitarian, divine-Christ, blood-atonement religion is Paul's literary triumph, not Jesus's or James's framework

The pastoral nuance: Tabor doesn't want Paul presented as sinister. "He's driven by a vision; he had these visions, he felt he had a vision from Christ." But it is *a* version of the movement — not *the* original version.

### Historian vs. Theologian — What Each Can and Cannot Investigate

The methodological frame Tabor uses to deflect the "are you trying to destroy Christianity" attack from ABC's Martin Bashir. Bashir's question: "You don't believe in the Virgin Birth, you don't believe Jesus was God, you don't believe he died for our sins, you don't believe he was raised from the dead, you don't believe he's in heaven, you don't believe he's coming again — are you trying to destroy Christianity?"

Tabor's response: **all five of those are theology, not history.** Historians can't investigate whether someone is God, whether someone died for our sins, whether someone is in heaven. Historians can investigate documentary evidence, archaeological evidence, contextual evidence — what someone said, what people said about them, what the cultural context was.

"Did Jesus walk on water? Some students say I think he did, some say I don't think he did. As a historian, I can't say anything about that except that 40 years after the event, Mark's Gospel reports the miracle story."

At a state university — Tabor is at UNC Charlotte, a public institution — the academic role is to study religion historically, not to promote religious belief. "We wouldn't allow it with Islam if an Islamicist came in and said 'in the name of Allah the great...' — that's not a course in Islam, that's a course promoting the beliefs of Islam."

This is the cleanest articulation in the whole interview of the discipline Tabor practices: historical-critical study, not theological apologetics.

### The Caller (Michael from Matthews) and the "Are You Dismissing the New Testament?" Charge

A live caller pushes the confessional Christian objection: "you're effectively elevating your sources above the New Testament, dismissing what scripture teaches, dismissing 'at the name of Jesus every knee shall bow.'"

Tabor's response is masterclass-level pastoral and methodological at once:
1. "90% of my evidence comes from the New Testament" — read carefully, contextually, with attention to idiom and shorthand
2. The "every knee shall bow" line Michael cites is Paul quoting Isaiah — and Isaiah is talking about Yahweh, not the Messiah
3. "75% of what I say is accepted by all historians I know in New Testament studies" — this is mainstream scholarship, not Tabor's solo eccentricity
4. The disagreement is between historical-critical scholarship and confessional theology — not between Tabor and scripture

He doesn't talk down to Michael. He doesn't dismiss the question. He answers it carefully, then redirects: "If you read the book, you'll see I carefully go through these texts and give a historical-critical reading. It's not disrespectful to read the Bible with intelligence and historical perception."

### Tabor's Own Faith Has Been Strengthened, Not Destroyed

The closing personal note. Mike Collins reads an email from "Amy" — a former Catholic now reading the book — who says, "the more I find out about the Jesus who was probably much more human and radical than was previously supposed, I find that I have regained my faith, but in a different and much more meaningful form."

Mike asks Tabor: how does your own faith square with your research? Tabor's answer: it has changed over time, in a positive way.

"I see my work as trying to get closer to Jesus. Now I know certain believing Christians would say 'how could you say that, you're awful, you're a blasphemer,' but if I am more credibly reflecting what he was about — then I'm on the right side."

The line that closes the show: this is not destruction; this is rehabilitation. Recover the historical Jewish founder, the family around him, the priestly co-worker, the brother who carried it forward. The Christianity that emerges from that recovery isn't Constantine's church. But it isn't a-religion-of-nothing either. It's a religion *closer to its founder*.

## Misconceptions Addressed

| Misconception | Tabor's Response |
|---------------|------------------|
| "Tabor says Jesus's father was a Roman soldier" (Charlotte Observer review) | Tabor specifically denies making that claim. The book *surveys* ancient Jewish and Roman polemic that named a man called Pantera — who later became a Roman soldier but was 14-15 at the time of Jesus's conception. Tabor takes no position; his actual interest is rehabilitating Mary. |
| "Jesus had no brothers — Mary was perpetually virgin" | The Gospels themselves name four brothers (James, Joses, Judas, Simon) and refer to "sisters." Protestants tend to know this; Catholic catechesis often does not. At higher academic levels in the Catholic Church the brothers are acknowledged but reinterpreted as cousins or step-siblings. The simplest reading is the historical one. |
| "Joseph was Jesus's biological father" | Even if you accept the Virgin Birth theologically, Joseph is not biologically Jesus's father. If you reject the Virgin Birth, you still have to ask who was. Either way Joseph isn't biologically in the picture. |
| "Peter was Jesus's designated successor" | James was. Peter was "chief of the Twelve" but James presided in the Jerusalem mother-church. This is in Acts 15, Galatians 1-2, 1 Corinthians 15, and the early church historians — not Tabor's invention. |
| "Christianity has always taught what it teaches now" | The Trinitarian-divine-Christ-blood-atonement framework is Pauline first, then patristic. The historical Jesus and James operated within Second Temple Jewish messianism, not what later became orthodoxy. |
| "Historians are out to destroy Christianity" | "It's not disrespectful to read the Bible with intelligence and historical perception." 90% of the evidence comes from the New Testament, read carefully. 75% of Tabor's positions are mainstream New Testament scholarship. |
| "The Gospel of Judas reveals Jesus's secret plan" | "It's authentic but it's second or third century. Does it tell us about the historical Jesus? I'm not sure." Tabor leans toward the early tradition that Judas was indeed a betrayer. |
| "Jesus was a carpenter in a quaint little wood shop" | Greek *tekton* means builder/artisan, not specifically carpenter. Wood was rarely used in 1st-century Galilee (door frames, roof beams). Stone was the dominant material. Jesus's parables saturated with building/foundation imagery. He likely worked in Sepphoris — the urban Herodian capital four miles from Nazareth — as a day-laborer stone mason. |
| "Jesus didn't know he was the Messiah / fellowship-of-the-ring style reluctance" | Tabor argues Jesus consciously stepped into the Davidic role. The Triumphal Entry is deliberate prophetic pantomime fulfilling Zechariah 9. The Temple action is a deliberate fulfillment. Jesus knew the script and acted it. |
