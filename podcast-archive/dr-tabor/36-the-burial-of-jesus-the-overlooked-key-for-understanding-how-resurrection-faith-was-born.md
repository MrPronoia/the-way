## Summary

Dr. Tabor reframes the entire resurrection conversation by refusing to start where everyone else does. Don't start with "what's your view of the resurrection" (presuppositions everywhere). Don't even start with the empty tomb (Mark Goodacre is right — "empty" begs the question of what a family tomb would normally hold). **Start with the burial.** That's the one data point everyone agrees on, including Paul's earliest creed in 1 Corinthians 15:3-4 ("Christ died... was buried... was raised"). If you build outward from the burial alone, the standard apologetic case collapses and a much more plausible historical reconstruction takes its place — one in which the body and bones of Jesus could have stayed in a tomb in Jerusalem with zero threat to early resurrection faith.

The reconstruction has two moving parts. First, the burial was an emergency: Jesus died late Thursday afternoon with Passover and the Sabbath bearing down. Joseph of Arimathea (probably with Nicodemus) got Pilate's permission, grabbed the nearest unused tomb on the Mount of Olives — **not** Joseph's family tomb, the text never says that — and laid the body there temporarily, planning to move it to a permanent grave once the double-Sabbath ended Saturday night. John 19:41-42 preserves the giveaway detail Mark omits: "the tomb was close at hand because of the Jewish day of preparation." When Mary Magdalene arrives at dawn Sunday and finds the stone moved, her own report (John 20:2) is exactly what you'd expect: "They have taken the Lord out of the tomb and we do not know where they have laid him." That's not a resurrection claim. That's somebody noticing the burial party did its job.

The second move is Paul. For Paul (our earliest witness, the only first-person eyewitness), the body-in-the-tomb is a non-problem. Resurrection isn't corpse revival. It's metamorphosis — a "dust being" left behind like rotten clothing while a "life-giving spirit" comes forth in a new glorified body. The seed in the ground is not the plant that comes up. So the disciples' enemies could have walked everyone over to the tomb and shown them the bones, and Paul's framework wouldn't blink. The reason Luke and John eventually have to put Jesus eating fish and getting touched by Thomas is apologetic — by then critics like Celsus are mocking "deluded women and hallucinations," and rival traditions like Apollonius of Tyana are claiming the same kind of post-mortem appearances. The physicalized risen body is a defense move against second-century skepticism, not the original story. The original story is buried, reburied, glorified.

## Key Teachings

### Don't Start with "Resurrection" — Start with the Burial

Tabor opens by ruling two starting points out of bounds. (1) "What's your view of the resurrection?" — this drags in every theological presupposition before you've looked at evidence. (2) "What's your view of the empty tomb?" — Mark Goodacre's point: if it was a normal Jewish family tomb, "empty" is the wrong category to begin with. **The one move everybody agrees on is that Jesus was buried.** Paul says so in the earliest creed (1 Cor 15:3-4). Build outward from that and the picture changes completely. This methodological shift — burial first, everything else downstream — is the entire frame of the talk.

### Resurrection ≠ Resuscitation: Paul's "Tent and Clothing" Model

Tabor draws the same distinction he draws across his Paul playlist, but here he ties it directly to why the burial doesn't matter for Paul's framework. In 2 Corinthians 5, Paul calls the body a "tent" — temporary, mortal, going back to dust. He also uses the clothing image: the old body is shed like rotten worn-out clothes, leaving the person briefly "naked" (disembodied) before being "reclothed" with a permanent spiritual body at the parousia. In 1 Corinthians 15 the second Adam isn't another physical body — he's a "life-giving spirit." So for Paul, when you ask "where's the body?" you're asking the wrong question. The body is the abandoned tent. **The bones in the tomb are not the person.**

### The Reburial Hypothesis: An "Emergency Burial" Then a Move

Tabor's historical reconstruction: Jesus died ~3 p.m. Thursday. Passover begins at sundown, immediately followed by the weekly Sabbath — a double-Sabbath blackout window during which a body cannot be properly buried (no washing, no anointing, no seven-day mourning rites). Joseph of Arimathea, possibly with Nicodemus, gets Pilate's release of the body and **uses the nearest unused tomb on the Mount of Olives as a temporary holding spot to keep the body from predators over the holiday**. He intends to come back the moment the Sabbath ends Saturday night and move the body to a permanent grave. By the time the women arrive at dawn Sunday with their spices, the move has already happened. The "empty tomb" is empty because the body has been reburied, not because it has been raised.

### John 19:41-42 Is the Giveaway Verse Nobody Quotes

> "Now in the place where he was crucified there was a garden, and in the garden a new tomb where no one had ever been laid. So because of the Jewish day of preparation, **as the tomb was close at hand**, they laid Jesus there."

Tabor: this is the verse he "never hears discussed." Bart Ehrman, Mark Goodacre, the whole guild reads this through Matthew's lens (Matthew is the one who turns it into Joseph's own tomb, because he wants to fulfill Isaiah 53:9, "his grave with a rich man"). But Mark, Luke, and John never say it was Joseph's family tomb. John gives two reasons for the choice: (1) no time, (2) that tomb over there isn't being used. **It's a parking spot, not a destination.** Once you see this verse for what it is, the rest of the reconstruction falls into place automatically.

### Mary's Own Report Is the Real Story: "We Don't Know Where They Have Laid Him"

Tabor's pick for the earliest layer of empty-tomb tradition is the first ten verses of John 20, read as an independent piece embedded in our latest gospel. Mary Magdalene arrives, sees the stone moved, runs to Peter and the Beloved Disciple, and says exactly what a person discovering a moved body would say: **"They have taken the Lord out of the tomb and we do not know where they have laid him."** That's not a resurrection announcement. That's "the burial party came back and finished the job." Peter and the Beloved Disciple race to the tomb, see the folded linen wrappings and head-cloth left behind (exactly what a burial party tidying up a temporary spot would leave), and then — note carefully — they **go back to their homes**. No proclamation. No angel. No risen Christ in those ten verses. The resurrection embellishments start at verse 11.

### The Gospel of Peter's Ending Confirms Galilee, Not Jerusalem

Tabor walks through the Gospel of Peter (8th-century fragment, discovered 1886, but containing what may be a second-century or earlier core). The ending is the bombshell. **Eight days after the empty tomb is discovered — through the entire week of unleavened bread — the disciples are still in Jerusalem "weeping and mourning."** No appearances. No faith. No risen Christ walking around in Jerusalem eating fish. At the end of the feast they go home to Galilee, and Peter and Andrew go back to fishing on the Sea of Galilee. Then the text breaks off mid-sentence — almost certainly at the start of a Galilean appearance scene parallel to John 21. This fits Mark ("you will see him in Galilee"). It fits Paul (no Jerusalem appearances mentioned). It does not fit Luke and John's compressed Jerusalem-on-Easter-Sunday narrative.

### Mark's Empty Tomb May Be the Transfiguration in Disguise

Tabor floats a careful suggestion: Mark's resurrection theology may already be encoded earlier in his gospel — at the Transfiguration (Mark 9). The mountain in Galilee, Moses and Elijah appearing in glorified bodies alongside Jesus — that's exactly what resurrection looks like in the Pauline framework. Tabor: "the Transfiguration is almost a proleptic resurrection experience." When Mark's young man at the tomb says "you'll see him in Galilee as he told you" — Tabor wonders whether Mark is pointing back to that mountain scene as **the** resurrection imagery, not Luke and John's fish-eating apologetic. If so, Mark didn't need post-tomb appearances. He'd already given his readers the resurrection vision in chapter 9.

### Two Objections Handled: Gossip and Legends

Tabor names the two strongest objections to his reconstruction and answers both directly. **Objection 1** (from the late Jerome Murphy-O'Connor, in a personal letter): "In the gossipy society of first-century Jerusalem, word would have leaked — somebody would have pointed at the actual tomb and said 'see, he's not risen, he's right here.'" Tabor's answer: enemies do not violate Jewish tombs. That's a religious red line, not just a social one. And the disciples who knew where the body was had no problem with the location, because they didn't think resurrection meant the body was the issue. **Objection 2**: "Wouldn't there be a known landmark in the 2nd, 3rd, 4th centuries — 'this is the tomb where Jesus's bones are'?" Tabor's answer: by then everyone had absorbed the Luke-John physicalized risen-corpse framework, so the question of "where are the bones" was already incoherent for Christians. The earlier framework that made bones-in-a-tomb compatible with resurrection had been forgotten. (Tabor notes he is *not* claiming Talpiot is the Jesus family tomb — only that **if** it were, it would not threaten authentic early resurrection faith.)

### Why Luke and John Physicalized the Body — Apologetic Pressure

By the late first and early second centuries, sophisticated Greek critics were attacking Christianity for being a religion of "deluded women and hallucinations passed off as visions." Rival divine-man traditions (Apollonius of Tyana, fathered by Zeus, raised the dead, ascended bodily to heaven) were competing in the same religious marketplace. A vague tradition of Galilean visions wasn't going to win that fight. So Luke (24) and John (20:11+ and 21) physicalize the encounters: Jesus eats fish, lets Thomas finger his wounds, walks through locked doors, insists "I am not a spirit." Tabor reads this as **directly contradicting Paul's "life-giving spirit"** — written for second-century apologetics, not first-century memory.

## Misconceptions Addressed

| Misconception | Tabor's Response |
|---------------|------------------|
| "Joseph of Arimathea buried Jesus in his own family tomb" | Only Matthew says this — to fulfill Isaiah 53:9 ("with the rich"). Mark, Luke, and John all describe a *new, unused* tomb chosen because it was "close at hand." Jewish law would have prohibited Joseph from mixing an executed criminal with his ancestors. |
| "If the body were still in a tomb in Jerusalem, the disciples couldn't have believed in resurrection" | Only true if you assume resurrection = corpse revival. For Paul (our earliest witness), resurrection is metamorphosis into a new spiritual body — the old corpse left behind like discarded clothing. The location of the bones was irrelevant. |
| "The disciples' enemies would have produced the body to disprove the resurrection" | Enemies do not violate Jewish tombs — a religious red line, not just a social one. And the disciples didn't see the body's location as a problem in the first place. |
| "There should be a known 'tomb of Jesus's bones' tradition in the 2nd–4th centuries" | By then everyone had absorbed Luke and John's physicalized risen-corpse framework, so the question of where the bones were had become incoherent for Christians. The earlier framework that made bones-in-a-tomb compatible with resurrection had been forgotten. |
| "Tabor says the Talpiot tomb is the Jesus family tomb and that Jesus's father was a Roman soldier named Pantera" | Tabor: "I've never said either one of those things. What I've said is let's look at all the evidence." Both are misrepresentations of his position. |
| "Crossan is right that the burial tradition is a late invention" | Tabor disagrees. The burial sits inside Paul's earliest credal formula (1 Cor 15:3-4) — too early to be a Markan invention. The burial is the most secure historical anchor we have. |
| "The empty tomb proves resurrection" | The empty tomb has a mundane explanation: Joseph returned at sundown Saturday and moved the body to a permanent grave. The women arrived twelve hours too late. |
| "Resurrection means the same body coming back to life" | That's resuscitation (Lazarus, Jairus's daughter — they died again). Resurrection in Paul's sense is a metamorphosis into an incorruptible "life-giving spirit" — a new genus in the universe (Romans 8). |
| "Sheol in the Hebrew Bible is a place of torment" | Sheol is universal sleep — "the small and the great are there together" (Job). It's the pit, the grave. Not the hellenistic Hades, not Gilgamesh's House of the Dead. Disembodied non-existence in the keeping of God. |
