## Summary

Dr. James Tabor takes on one of the most contested questions in early Christian studies: **when does the Jesus movement stop being a form of Judaism and become a separate religion called Christianity?** This is a Biblical Archaeology Society lecture — Tabor is in exploratory mode, working through his own thinking out loud, en route to writing the book *Paul and Jesus*. He frames the question against the title of Paula Fredriksen's *When Christians Were Jews* — because everyone agrees the John the Baptizer / Jesus / James-brother-of-Jesus movement starts as thoroughly Jewish. The question is when, and how, and through whom, the break actually happens.

Tabor lays out four anchor components that define second-temple Judaism: monotheism, the chosen nation, Torah, and the land. He then walks through how each of these gets renegotiated in early Christianity — and where the decisive break happens. The current scholarly fashion, he notes, is to say Paul never broke from Judaism at all — he was a faithful Jew defending his Gentile converts' right to remain Gentile. **Tabor is convinced this is wrong.** He's resurrecting F.C. Baur's 19th-century thesis: Paul represents a fundamental break, and Paul can properly be called the founder of the Christian faith. The Romans by 110 CE are already calling it a new *superstitio* — a new religion — and you can't get there without Paul.

The mechanism of Paul's break is not what people usually claim. It's not "Paul started worshiping Jesus as God" — second-temple Judaism actually had room for divine mediators (Metatron, the logos, the figure who eats with Abraham in Genesis 18). The real break is Paul's **redefinition of the chosen nation**. In Galatians 3, Philippians 3, and Romans 2-4, Paul argues "we are the true circumcision," "we are the true Israel" — meaning the people of faith in Christ, not the ethnic-political entity the Romans knew as the Jews. Abraham was justified by faith *before* his circumcision, so the seed of Abraham was never about flesh — it was always about faith. Jews who reject the Messiah get broken off the olive tree (Romans 9-11); Gentiles who accept him get grafted in. Tabor argues this isn't replacement theology in Paul's own mind — Paul thinks he's recovering the *true* Israel, not creating a new one — but **sociologically it is a new religion**, and within fifty years the Romans recognize it as such.

## Key Teachings

### Four Components That Define Pre-Christian Judaism

Tabor's framework for what Judaism was when Jesus came on the scene:

1. **Monotheism** — One God. Paul to the Thessalonians: "you turned to God from idols, to serve the living and true God" (1 Thess 1:9). Jesus quoting the Shema: "Hear O Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is one."
2. **Chosen Nation** — Descent from Abraham through Sarah, through Isaac, through Jacob. The seed (*zera*) is literal: Y-chromosome to Abraham, mitochondrial to Sarah. Though even within Genesis this gets immediately complicated — Joseph marries an Egyptian, Rahab is a Canaanite, Ruth is a Moabite, and David descends from both.
3. **Torah** — Better translated "teaching" than "law." The broad orientation Moses preaches in Deuteronomy — including the prediction that Israel will fail, but in the last days come back.
4. **The Land** — Jerusalem as capital, Dan to Beersheba as the arena where the drama plays out. Isaiah 2: the mountain of the Lord's house will be highest, and all nations will flow to it.

Even within the Hebrew Bible itself, all four ideals get shattered. The chosen nation doesn't stay monotheist; it doesn't become a light to the nations; it gets kicked out of the land twice. The prophets project the fulfillment forward into the last days. So the question of what Judaism "really is" was already contested before anyone named Jesus showed up.

### Three Candidates for When the Break Happens

Tabor identifies the three standard scholarly positions:

- **Jesus himself** — Started something so new it can no longer be called Israelite faith.
- **Paul** — Introduced revelations that undercut the traditional claims of Judaism.
- **The church fathers** — Marcion, Justin Martyr, Tertullian; second-century formalization with language about a "third race" and a "new people."

A fourth, currently fashionable position: **neither Jesus nor Paul ever broke from Judaism**. Both remained faithful Jews; the break is purely later. Tabor rejects this — he's making the case for Paul.

### What the Romans Saw

By around 110 CE, the Roman historian Tacitus describes "the pernicious superstition" (Christianity) as something distinct from Judaism — a new religion that "broke out in Judea and has spread to Rome." Tacitus is explicit: he doesn't mean the Jews. He means this new thing built around a man crucified by Pontius Pilate.

Pliny the Younger (also early second century) writes to Trajan from Asia Minor describing his test for catching Christians: make them curse Christ and burn incense before an image of the emperor. **Notice — not curse Yahweh.** This is a new group, distinct from the synagogue across town. Jews are exempt from emperor cult (a longstanding Roman accommodation); Christians are not. The sociological separation is complete.

Tabor's logic: if Romans by 110 CE clearly distinguish Christians from Jews, the break has to have happened earlier. Tabor's candidate: Paul, writing in the 50s.

### Why "Paul Worshiped Jesus as God" Is NOT the Break

Tabor pushes back on the obvious answer. Second-temple Judaism had room for divine mediator figures:

- **Metatron** — A figure in 1, 2, 3 Enoch and rabbinic stories who sits at the right hand of God, isn't God, but represents God.
- **The logos** — Philo's framework for the figure who eats with Abraham in Genesis 18. Can't be the ineffable transcendent God, but is called God; an "earthly manifestation."
- **Yahweh and his consort** — Even within the Hebrew Bible and archaeology, hints of partner figures (not always female).

So Paul calling Jesus an exalted agent of God isn't categorically heretical within the diversity of second-temple Judaism. People assume that's the break, but it isn't.

### The Real Break — Paul Redefines the Chosen Nation

The decisive move happens in Galatians 3, Philippians 3, and Romans 2-4:

- "We are the true circumcision" (Phil 3:3)
- "We are the true Commonwealth of Israel"
- "He is not a Jew who is one outwardly... but he is a Jew who is one inwardly" (Rom 2:28-29)

**The "we" doesn't mean the ethnic-social-political entity the Romans knew as the Jews.** It means the community of believers in Christ — Jewish or Gentile. Paul doesn't deny that the ethnic Jewish people exist; he denies that they are *Israel* unless they accept the Messiah.

The argumentative wedge is Genesis 15:6 — Abraham was justified by faith *before* his circumcision in Genesis 17. So the seed of Abraham was never fundamentally about flesh and genealogy. Romans 9-11's olive tree: ethnic Jews who reject the Messiah are broken off; Gentiles who accept him are grafted in. If Jews accept the Messiah they get grafted back in, faster because they were closer to the tree.

Tabor stresses: Paul wouldn't say this is a *new* tree — he'd say it's the *true* tree, the Abrahamic tree, always defined by faith rather than flesh. But sociologically, this IS a new religion. The "we" that emerges is no longer the synagogue.

### "True" vs "New" — Paul's Own Framing

Tabor is careful with the language. Paul resists "new" — he'd say he's recovering the *true* meaning of the Abrahamic covenant, which was always about faith, never primarily about ethnicity. So he's not replacement theology in his own mind ("get rid of those old Jews, bring in new ones"). He's restorationist.

But the practical effect is the same: a religion where ethnic Jewish identity no longer maps to covenant membership. Membership runs through faith in Christ. The Torah ethics are reduced to "the law of Christ" — love one another, the fruit of the Spirit (Gal 5, Rom 15). Food laws and calendar observance become "shadows of the past" (Gal 4). The land becomes "Jerusalem above, a heavenly country" (Gal 4:26, Hebrews developing this further). All four anchor components of Judaism get re-grammared.

### The Hagar/Sarah Allegory — Paul's "Switcheroo"

Tabor lingers on Galatians 4:21-31, where Paul pulls what Tabor calls a "trickster" move:

The Jewish reader would say: "I am Isaac's seed — child of the free woman, Sarah. The Ishmaelites are the cast-out ones from Hagar."

Paul flips it: **Earthly Jerusalem, the Jews who reject the Messiah, are actually the Hagar children.** "Jerusalem now is in slavery with her children." The free woman is Jerusalem above, mother of those in Christ. "Cast out the bond woman and her son; she shall not inherit with the free."

Tabor: "The day Paul thought of this, I think he was really proud of himself — what a switcheroo." (It also becomes politically explosive after the rise of Islam, but that's centuries later.)

### Judaism's Own Identity Confusion

Tabor pauses to point out: this isn't only Paul redefining things. **Judaism itself has never had a stable answer to "who is a Jew?"** A born-Jewish atheist who hates the Bible and converts to Buddhism is still a Jew to the rabbis. A Jewish-born Catholic priest is still a Jew — a fallen Jew, but a Jew. A Moroccan Jew speaking Arabic and eating falafel looks nothing like an Ashkenazi Jew but is equally Jewish.

The category is religious + ethnic + cultural + genetic, and which one dominates shifts based on the question being asked. Conversion is the doorway in for those without Jewish mothers — but if conversion is what makes you a Jew, then it's a religion, which contradicts the matrilineal definition. Paul, in this light, is offering one resolution of an unstable category — and his resolution happens to break the inherited boundaries.

"So I kind of want to give Paul a break in the sense that it's not like 'Oh, Judaism so clear it's this, and then Paul comes along and changes it.' It's not clear."

### Paul Did Simplify — and That Simplification Is a New Religion

Tabor's careful framing of what Paul actually did:

> "What Paul did was he simplified things. In terms of defining the people of God, the Genesis 12 chosen nation now becomes very simply put: the people of faith in Christ. And it doesn't matter what their ethnic background was, their social background, or anything to do with their pedigree. It only depends upon faith in Christ. **I call that a new religion.**"

Paul would say it's the old, the true religion. But it operationally replaces:
- Ethnic descent with faith
- Torah observance with the law of Christ
- Festivals, food laws, Sabbath observance with their dismissal as shadows
- The physical land with heavenly Jerusalem

That's a new religion. Whether Paul intends it as one or not.

### The Nazarenes and Ebionites — The Road Not Taken

Tabor closes with a pointer (the recording cuts off mid-thought): the Nazarenes and Ebionites are a Jewish-Christian group following James the brother of Jesus, who adhere to a *non-Pauline* interpretation. They represent the alternative line — the Jesus movement that didn't follow Paul, didn't redefine Israel, kept Torah observance. They get systematically erased by the orthodox tradition that builds itself on Paul.

This is the lineage Tabor traces in other work — Essene → Nazarene → Ebionite — the "blue thread" that stays close to the original Jewish Jesus movement and gets buried by what becomes mainstream Christianity.

## Misconceptions Addressed

| Misconception | Tabor's Response |
|---------------|------------------|
| "Paul never broke from Judaism — he was a faithful Jew defending Gentile rights" | This is the current scholarly fashion but Tabor rejects it. Paul redefines who is Israel — ethnic Jews who reject the Messiah are "broken off." That is a break from Judaism, not a defense of it. |
| "The break is Paul calling Jesus divine" | Second-temple Judaism had room for divine mediator figures (Metatron, logos, the Genesis 18 figure). That's not where Paul breaks. He breaks on the definition of the covenant people. |
| "Paul created Christianity, and that's a *bad* thing — he ruined the true religion of Jesus" | Tabor pushes back on the standard "Paul ruined Jesus" framing. Paul *did* create something new in effect, but he understood himself as recovering the true Abrahamic faith. The judgment is more complex than the standard polemic. |
| "Judaism was a stable category that Paul disrupted" | Judaism's own identity is unstable — religious, ethnic, cultural, genetic categories don't agree. Paul is one resolution of an already-contested boundary, not a disruption of a clean line. |
| "The 'parting of the ways' happened with the church fathers in the second century" | Sociologically, yes — Marcion, Justin, the councils make it explicit. But the theological move that makes that separation possible is Paul's, decades earlier. |
| "Replacement theology = Paul's view" | Tabor is careful: Paul doesn't say the church *replaces* Israel. He says Christ-believers are the *true* Israel and always were. The difference matters even if the practical effect is similar. |
