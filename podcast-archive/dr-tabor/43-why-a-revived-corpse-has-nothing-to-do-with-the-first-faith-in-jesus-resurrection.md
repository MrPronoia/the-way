## Summary

This is the conversation where Tabor draws the cleanest possible line between the **earliest** Christian faith in Jesus's resurrection (Paul and Mark) and the **late** view that has dominated Christendom ever since (Luke and John). The headline claim — and Tabor states it bluntly — is that **the earliest believers would not have been bothered in the slightest by Jesus's flesh-and-blood corpse being left behind in the tomb.** Paul, our only first-person witness, pictures resurrection as a cosmic transformation: the dust-being shed off like an old garment, the new glorified Spirit-being emerging in its place. The corpse-walking-out-of-the-tomb image — wounds intact, fish in hand, finger in side — is not in Paul. It is not in Mark. It enters the tradition with Luke and Acts, and is fully developed in John, and Tabor argues it was developed specifically as an apologetic *against* second-century skeptics like Celsus who said "everybody has visions; prove it was physical."

The interview moves through a tight chronological sweep. **Paul (~50s CE):** death, burial, appearances. Empty tomb is roughly irrelevant to the substance of what resurrection *is*. The Greek word in the air is *apotheosis* — a mortal melts away and the immortal emerges in its new state. **Mark (earliest gospel, ~70 CE):** the women find the tomb empty, a *young man* (not an angel) tells them to go to Galilee, and the gospel ends there. Mark's actual theological statement about resurrection, Tabor argues following his teacher Norman Perrin, is the Transfiguration in Mark 9 — a *pre*-figuration of the same glorified state Paul describes. **Matthew (~80 CE):** mountain encounter, "some doubted." **Luke and Acts (~85 CE) and John (~90-100 CE):** now Jesus eats fish, walks through locked doors, has Thomas probe his wounds, and ascends after forty days of meals. The line keeps getting thicker until what was once a luminous visionary metamorphosis has been forced into the shape of a resuscitated corpse.

Tabor's offered vocabulary is the gift of the episode. **Apotheosis** for what happens to a dead person being raised (mortal melts, immortal emerges). **Metamorphosis** for what happens to a living person at the parousia (the dust-body transforms in place). **Epiphany** for the late-gospel mode where the risen Christ manifests in a temporarily physical-seeming way and then dissolves into the spiritual state again — modeled on Hebrew Bible angelic appearances like Gideon and the angel of the fire. The whole vocabulary points away from corpse-revival. The first-century Jesus-followers were experiencing — and Paul is reporting — something categorically different from the literalism the church eventually canonized.

## Key Teachings

### The Earliest Resurrection Faith Has Nothing to Do With a Revived Corpse

This is the title-claim and the argumentative spine. Paul, the only first-person witness in the entire New Testament, would not have been bothered in the least with Jesus's flesh-and-blood body being "left behind" in a tomb. Paul's resurrection language is *apotheosis* — the mortal melts away, the glorified immortal emerges. The dust-body is irrelevant the same way old clothes are irrelevant once you've changed into new ones. Paul's first-person Jesus is not a fish-eating, wound-displaying revived corpse. **He is light, presence, voice, glory — categorically a different kind of being.**

The corpse-walking-out-of-the-tomb image is not in Paul. It is not in Mark. It enters the New Testament with Luke and is fully developed in John — both written 50-70 years after the actual events, and both, Tabor argues, written specifically to answer second-century skeptics who pointed out that visionary experiences don't constitute physical evidence.

### Apotheosis vs. Metamorphosis — Two Words for Two Situations

Tabor offers the precise Greek vocabulary the New Testament writers were working with:

- **Apotheosis** — what happens to someone who has *already died*. The mortal husk melts away; the immortal glorified form emerges. Used in Greek literature for the deification of mortals.
- **Metamorphosis** — what happens to someone who is *still alive* at the parousia. The living dust-body transforms in place. Paul's word in 1 Cor 15:51-52: "we shall not all sleep, but we shall all be *changed* — in a moment, in the twinkling of an eye."

The thought experiment Tabor offers: imagine someone dies one hour before Jesus returns. Their body is still warm. Women are washing the corpse for burial. Suddenly Jesus appears. Does the corpse sit up, say "I'm hungry, let me have some food"? Or does the corpse undergo apotheosis — the dust melts away and the new glorified George appears nearby? Tabor: the second. **Paul did not care about bodies, bones, or atoms.** Whether someone was just-dead, decades-decayed, cremated, or lost at sea — all of them undergo the same cosmic process.

### Mark's Resurrection Is the Transfiguration (Mark 9)

Tabor credits his teacher Norman Perrin (University of Chicago) for this read: **the Transfiguration in Mark 9 IS Mark's resurrection theology.** Mark has no appearances. The young man (not an angel) at the empty tomb tells the women "go to Galilee, you will see him *as he said*." That "as he said" points back to Mark 9, where Jesus said "some standing here will not taste death till you have seen the kingdom of God come with power" — followed seven days later by the mountain Transfiguration, where Moses and Elijah appear and Jesus is transfigured into shining glory.

So when Mark says "you will see him in Galilee," he is not promising a campfire reunion with a resuscitated corpse. He is pointing back to the Transfiguration — the *pre*-figuration of what resurrection actually looks like. **Apotheosis on a mountain. The glorified Christ flanked by Moses and Elijah, blinding the disciples with light.** Mark 9 and 1 Corinthians 15 are describing the same kind of event.

### Matthew's Mountain — Still Visionary, Some Doubted

Matthew preserves Mark's "go to Galilee" tradition and adds an actual mountain encounter (Matt 28). Tabor flags the strangeness of Matthew's account: it is *misty*. Clouds wafting. Eleven disciples on the mountain — Judas not yet replaced. They see Jesus. And then the line Christians rarely sit with: **"some doubted."** Not before they saw him. *After.*

Tabor's read: the Matthean appearance is still essentially visionary in texture. Some of the inner circle look up and see the phenomenon and immediately accept it. Others go "uh, is that him? It sounds like him but I don't know." That is not what you would expect of a publicly resuscitated corpse on a mountainside. **That is what you would expect of a visionary epiphany within a grief-shattered community.**

### Luke and John — Apologetic Hardening Against Celsus-Type Skeptics

The flesh-and-blood Jesus — eating fish, letting Thomas touch wounds, breaking bread with the Emmaus pair — enters the gospel tradition with Luke and Acts and is fully developed in John. Tabor argues this is *not* coincidence and *not* additional historical material newly remembered. It is apologetic hardening.

By the late first century, critics like Celsus (or the "Celsus type," as Tabor puts it) were saying: "These hysterical women just saw a hallucination. Everybody has visions. Pagans have ghost-appearances of dead heroes all the time — Apollonius of Tyana, on and on. Why should yours be different?" Luke and John are writing back. **No, no — he ate fish. He was touched. He had flesh and bones.** That is why Luke explicitly says "a spirit does not have flesh and bones" — the line is directly polemical against the visionary interpretation that was already circulating.

But notice what gives the apologetic move away: even in John, the risen Jesus *walks through locked doors* and disappears. Even in Luke, he vanishes from the Emmaus table. The author wants it both ways — physical enough for the skeptics, spiritual enough not to break the earlier tradition. The fingerprint of the embellishment is right there in the text.

### Epiphany — The Late-Gospel Mode of Appearance

The technical vocabulary for what Luke and John are actually doing, Tabor suggests, is **epiphany** — the same Hebrew Bible pattern as the angel of the Lord appearing to Gideon (Judges 6). The angel eats a meal. The angel touches the cakes with his staff. The angel vanishes upward in the flame. **The angel was never a resuscitated corpse**; he manifested temporarily in a physical-seeming way and then resumed his spiritual mode.

That, Tabor argues, is the literary mode Luke and John are working in. The risen Jesus *manifests* physically for the duration of the encounter — locked-door appearance, touchable, eats food, then vanishes. Not corpse-revival. Not Pauline metamorphosis either, exactly. Apologetic epiphany — designed to give physical-seeming evidence while still preserving the spiritual reality underneath.

### Galilee vs. Jerusalem — The Tension John Tries to Resolve

Tabor flags a structural tension in the gospel tradition: **Mark and Matthew send the disciples to Galilee.** Luke keeps them in Jerusalem and explicitly forbids them to leave ("do not leave the city until the Holy Spirit comes upon you" — Acts 1). John, latest of the four, includes *both* — Jerusalem appearances in John 20, then a Galilee appearance in John 21 (the famous fishing scene and breakfast on the beach).

This is not harmless geography. It points to two different memory streams. **The Galilee stream** (Mark, Matthew, John 21) fits Tabor's reconstruction: the disciples fled north in despair, and the visionary mountain experiences happened there. **The Jerusalem stream** (Luke, Acts, John 20) fits a later compression that wants the resurrection to happen on Easter Sunday in the city, with Jesus walking around and eating meals before ascending forty days later. John tries to hold both by including a Jerusalem section and then tacking a Galilee fishing scene onto the end.
