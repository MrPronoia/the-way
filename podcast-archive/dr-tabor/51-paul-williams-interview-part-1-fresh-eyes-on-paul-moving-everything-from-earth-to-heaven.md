## Summary

This is Part 1 of a two-part conversation between Tabor and Paul Williams (Blogging Theology) centered on a single startling sentence from Tabor's book *Paul and Jesus*: that Paul transformed the original Jesus movement into a new religion that "abrogated and superseded the old by moving everything from Earth to Heaven." Williams asks Tabor to unpack what that means, and the answer becomes a masterclass in how to read Paul's letters chronologically rather than canonically. Most readers, Tabor says, open their New Testaments to Matthew and start there — but the *earliest* witness to the Jesus movement is not the gospels. It is Paul's letters, beginning with 1 Thessalonians. Reading the New Testament backwards (Paul first, gospels later) reveals that the dominant theology in Mark — and by extension Luke, Matthew, and John — is itself a derivative of Paul's vertical, heavenly framework. Strip Paul out and you get back to a thoroughly earthly Jesus rooted in Isaiah's Messianic prophecies about Jerusalem becoming the spiritual center of a renewed world.

The core move Paul made, on Tabor's reading, was to take the Hebrew Bible's earth-bound expectations — Isaiah 2, Isaiah 11, all the nations flowing to the mountain of the Lord, the Earth full of the knowledge of the Lord as the waters cover the sea — and *allegorize them upward* into the heavens. Paul says it plainly in Galatians: "Jerusalem above is what we're interested in, not Jerusalem below." In 2 Corinthians 3-4, he says Moses's glory has faded because now we have a heavenly glory, and "the things that are seen are temporal, but the things that are unseen are eternal." Tabor concedes this *could* be straight Platonism — but he argues Paul is doing something subtly different: he is preserving Jewish resurrection and apocalyptic categories while relocating their fulfillment from a renewed Earth to a transformed cosmos of new "Adams," heavenly beings of which Jesus is "the firstborn of many."

Tabor calls this "his greatest idea" — not as a moral judgment but as a structural one. Paul's vertical framework is what enabled Christianity to survive when the apocalyptic timetable failed. The Ebionites, who kept Jesus's earthly Messianic framework, "didn't do too well." Apocalyptic has, in Tabor's dry phrase, "a 100 percent failure rate so far." Paul's Hellenized, heavenly Christ was portable, cosmic, and unfalsifiable by historical events — and that is precisely what made it the religion that survived.

## Key Teachings

### Read the New Testament Backwards: Paul Is the Earliest Witness

Most readers come to the New Testament canonically: Matthew first, then Mark, Luke, John, Acts, Paul. That sequence creates an illusion of chronology — as if the gospels record the *beginning* of the movement and Paul's letters come later. Tabor argues the opposite. The earliest text we possess is Paul's letter to the Thessalonians, written years before any gospel. Reading the New Testament backwards — starting with the seven undisputed Pauline letters (1 Thessalonians, 1-2 Corinthians, Galatians, Romans, Philippians, Philemon) — gives you the actual chronological floor of the movement.

This matters because the dominant theology of Mark (and through Mark, much of the synoptic and Johannine tradition) is *itself a derivative of Paul*. To recover what the historical Jesus, John the Baptist, or James the brother actually taught, you have to peel Paul's vertical framework back off the gospels.

### Paul Is the Only First-Hand Witness to James the Brother

A practical consequence of the "Paul first" rule: Paul is our best — and only first-person — source for James the brother of Jesus. Paul writes in Galatians that he went to Jerusalem and saw James personally. Whatever else Paul is doing theologically, this single fact makes him historically indispensable. "Imagine you're reading a letter by someone who actually met James and he gives you first-hand testimony." Most of what we think we know about the early Jerusalem church we infer from Paul's letters precisely because the gospels are written later and from outside that circle.

### The "Earth to Heaven" Move: Paul's Vertical Allegorization

The thesis sentence Williams asks Tabor to unpack: Paul "moved everything from Earth to Heaven."

Hebrew Messianic prophecy — especially Isaiah 2 and Isaiah 11 — is *terrestrial*. The mountain of the Lord's house will be established in the last days. All nations will flow to Jerusalem. The earth will be full of the knowledge of the Lord as the waters cover the sea. A new temple will be a house of prayer for all peoples. A davidic Messiah will rule the renewed Earth. **This is the framework Jesus, John the Baptist, and James operated within.**

Paul reads the same texts — and *allegorizes them upward*. In Galatians 4 he distinguishes "Jerusalem above" from "Jerusalem below" and identifies his community with the heavenly one. In 2 Corinthians 3, he says Moses's glory on Sinai has faded because the Christian now beholds a heavenly glory. In 2 Corinthians 4, he writes: "We look not to the things that are seen but to the things that are unseen, for the things that are seen are temporal, but the things that are unseen are eternal." Echoes ripple into Hebrews: "We have not come to a mountain that can be touched."

The fulfillment has been relocated. The same Messianic texts, but the action is no longer on a renewed Earth — it is in the heavenly realm.

### Is This Just Platonism? Almost, But Not Quite

Tabor concedes the obvious objection: 2 Corinthians 4 reads like straight Platonism. The seen vs. unseen, the temporal vs. eternal, the body as a tent — these are the bones of Greek philosophy, not Hebrew prophecy. Hellenistic Judaism in Paul's day was already saturated with this vocabulary; you can see it in Josephus when he talks about the "immortal soul" in ways that sound closer to Plato than to the Hebrew Bible.

But Paul is not simply baptizing Platonism. He retains a hard apocalyptic backbone: the dead are still raised, the cosmos is still transformed, there is still a *bodily* outcome (just one that cannot be described in earthly terms — "as different as an acorn from an oak tree," or "a caterpillar and a butterfly"). The Greek word he uses is *metamorphosis*. The framework is Jewish-apocalyptic in its scaffolding and Hellenistic in its texture. **A vertical synthesis, not a horizontal swap.**

### Paul's "Greatest Idea": Earth as Mock-Up for a Heavenly Genus

Tabor names what he considers Paul's signature theological innovation, drawn from Romans 8: God created the material world as a kind of *prototype* — a mortal, perishing mock-up — whose purpose is to generate a new genus of heavenly beings, the children of God. The first of these is Jesus of Nazareth, "the firstborn of many."

"The Earth is almost like a worm" — a chrysalis — "for the sons of God." Existence on this dust-Earth is not the point. The point is the transformation. What is seen dissolves so that what is unseen can remain.

This framework hands Christianity an unfalsifiable cosmology. Whether or not the davidic Messianic prophecies are fulfilled on Earth no longer matters — the action has moved upstairs. Tabor calls this Paul's "greatest idea" not because he endorses it, but because he sees it as the load-bearing innovation that let the Jesus movement survive the failure of apocalyptic expectation.

### Why Paul's Framework Survived and the Ebionites' Didn't

The Ebionites — Jewish followers of Jesus who kept the original earthly Messianic framework, who saw the temple sacrifices as "temporary accommodations to human sin and weakness," who expected fulfillment *on this Earth* — "didn't do too well." Their tradition withered. Paul's portable, heavenly, allegorized Christ won.

Tabor's dry punchline: "Apocalyptic has a 100 percent failure rate so far." Every prediction of an imminent earthly fulfillment has failed. Paul's move — relocating the fulfillment to a heavenly plane — made the religion immune to historical disconfirmation. The kingdom never had to arrive on Earth because it was already operating above.

This is, in Tabor's reading, why Christianity outlasted its original Jewish-apocalyptic competitors. The cost is that the religion of the historical Jesus — the earthly, Isaiah-rooted Messianic Judaism of John the Baptist and James — got effectively forgotten.

### "We Know Christ No Longer According to the Flesh"

A telling Pauline phrase from 2 Corinthians 5: "Even though we once knew Christ according to the flesh, we know him no longer in that way." Tabor reads this as Paul explicitly distancing his Christ from the people who actually knew Jesus in his earthly life — Peter, James, those who walked with him. The "flesh" Jesus is dispensable. The heavenly Christ is what matters.

Tabor reads Romans 9-11 (the famous "Israel according to the flesh" section) through the same lens. Paul has a new system, one that supersedes the framework Jesus's own brother James was still operating within in Jerusalem. The historical, earthly, davidic Jesus has been replaced by a cosmic, heavenly Christ — and Paul knows what he is doing.

| "Paul's gospel is just early Christianity in unfiltered form" | No — Paul *transformed* the original earthly Messianic Judaism into a heavenly, vertical system. The original framework (Jesus, John the Baptist, James) was earthly and Isaiah-rooted. |
| "Paul is straight Platonism" | Almost, but not quite. He uses Platonic seen/unseen, body-as-tent language — but retains apocalyptic resurrection, transformation, and bodily outcomes (just heavenly bodies). A Hellenized Jewish framework, not a Greek one. |
