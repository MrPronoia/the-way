## Summary

This is the second installment of Tabor's "Seven Fateful New Testament Texts" series — videos that bracket what a verse *originally meant* and focus instead on **how the verse has been pulled out of context, applied universally, and used to cause real harm across two thousand years**. The fateful passage in this episode: **1 Corinthians 7:1** — "*It is good for a man not to touch a woman.*" Paul's preference for celibacy, his reluctant concession to marriage as a "remedy for fornication," and his elevation of the single life as the more spiritually devoted path. The reciprocal phrasing matters — Paul applies the same standard to women — but the consequence is the same: marriage becomes a second-tier compromise, sexuality becomes a danger to manage rather than a creational gift to celebrate, and a two-tiered system of spiritual seriousness gets seeded into Western Christianity that eventually crystallizes as priestly celibacy, monastic vows, and an aversion to the body that Augustine will later canonize.

Tabor's central distinction is between **apocalyptic celibacy** and **church-imposed celibacy**. In 1 Corinthians 7, Paul flags repeatedly why he is recommending the unmarried life: "*the appointed time has grown very short*," "*the present distress*," "*the form of this world is passing away*." Paul thinks the end is five to ten years out. If the parousia is imminent, don't start a family — Jesus's own teaching warns against being pregnant or nursing when the tribulation comes. Tabor calls this Paul's apocalyptic celibacy, and he reads it as Paul's primary frame in the chapter. But Tabor also acknowledges Paul layers in a second motive: a Stoic-flavored ascetic piety. The unmarried man "is anxious about the affairs of the Lord, how to please the Lord. But the married man is anxious about worldly affairs." This is *not* purely eschatological. It is the seed of the idea that single devotion is spiritually higher — and once the end did not come, that seed is what grew.

The video then makes its most important turn. Tabor pulls up his own translation of Genesis 2 ("The Transparent English Version") and reads the original Hebrew creation account of male and female. "*Not good*" — God says — "*the soil creature being by himself.*" Yahweh fashions a corresponding counterpart, the *isha* to the *ish*, the woman who fits the man. The Hebrew is exuberant — Adam stutters with joy: "*This one, this one, this one*" (*zot, zot, zot*) — bone of my bones, flesh of my flesh. The first command in the Bible is "be fruitful and multiply." Marriage and embodied union are celebrated, not tolerated. This is the **Hebraic root** Paul's verse cuts Christianity off from. When Hellenistic neoplatonism's aversion to the body absorbs Paul's celibate preference, you get Augustine sleeping on the floor and refusing music in case it stirs his earthly passions. You get nuns as brides of Christ. You get priests forbidden from marriage. You get billions of people taught that their natural desires to love and be loved are a weakness to be repressed. Tabor closes by recalling his years teaching at Notre Dame, where 75% of his undergraduates came from Roman Catholic backgrounds and openly told him that priestly celibacy looked like "a ticket to heaven" — but a ticket only the saintly few could afford. The two-tier system, alive and well, still doing damage.

## Key Teachings

### What 1 Corinthians 7:1 Actually Says

Paul's opening affirmation in 1 Corinthians 7: "*It is good for a man not to touch a woman.*" Tabor is careful: this is **reciprocal** in Paul — the same standard applies to women. Paul goes on: "but because of the temptation to sexual immorality, each man should have his own wife, and each woman her own husband." Paul calls this a **concession, not a command**. He wishes everyone could live as he himself lives — single. But he allows that each has "his own special gift from God" — and the gift of celibacy is one some have and some do not. Marriage, in Paul's frame here, is functionally **a remedy for fornication**: "It is better to marry than to be aflame with passion." Tabor flags how this has rightly bothered progressive readers across the centuries: is *that* really all marriage is for?

### The Apocalyptic Engine Under the Whole Chapter

Paul's reasons for recommending celibacy are not free-floating spirituality. They are eschatology. Watch the markers Tabor highlights in red:

- "*in view of the present distress*" (v.26)
- "*the appointed time has grown very short*" (v.29)
- "*from now on, let those who have wives live as though they have none*" (v.29)
- "*the form [Greek: schema] of this world is passing away*" (v.31)

Tabor connects this to Isaiah 24, the prophet's "apocalypse" — "as with the slave, so with his master; as with the buyer, so with the seller; as with the lender, so with the borrower." Paul is reading the Hebrew prophets and seeing the order of the world dissolving. Male/female, slave/free, married/unmarried — all those distinctions become irrelevant in the age to come when Jesus returns from heaven. Paul thinks he has five years, maybe ten. **Don't get married now** is the same logic as **don't seek freedom if you're a slave** — don't restructure your earthly life when the earth itself is about to be restructured.

### Apocalyptic Celibacy vs. Church-Imposed Celibacy

This is Tabor's load-bearing distinction. Paul's celibacy in 1 Corinthians 7 is **situational**. It's keyed to the imminent end. If the end doesn't come, the rationale collapses. But the church, over the next several centuries, **detaches the celibate preference from its apocalyptic frame** and reinstalls it as a permanent mark of spiritual seriousness. By the time you get to the Pastoral Epistles (1 Timothy in particular, which Tabor — like most critical scholars — treats as later and heavily edited, not authentically Pauline), widows are being "enrolled" if they've pledged themselves to Christ and remained unmarried. The metaphor of being **married to Christ** has arrived. Nuns wear wedding rings. Roman Catholic priests are *required* to be celibate. And none of this carries Paul's "the time is short" caveat anymore. The emergency measure has become the standard.

### The Second Motive Tabor Acknowledges in Paul

Tabor is fair to the text. Paul is not *only* doing apocalyptic celibacy. Read verses 32-35: "The unmarried man is anxious about the affairs of the Lord, how to please the Lord. But the married man is anxious about worldly affairs and how to please his wife, and his interests are divided. And the unmarried woman or girl is anxious about the affairs of the Lord, how to be holy in body and spirit." This is **not** strictly eschatological. This is a piety claim — that the single life enables a higher, more undivided devotion. Tabor sees this as Paul's Hellenistic borrowing — a Stoic-flavored ideal of self-control, a quasi-ascetic spirituality that *would* fit comfortably in neoplatonic philosophy and *does not* fit comfortably in the Hebrew Bible. This is the strand of Paul that survives the failure of his apocalyptic timeline and grows into Augustine.

### The Hebraic Counter-Witness — Genesis 2

Tabor reads from his own translation, the *Transparent English Version of Genesis*, to surface what Christianity walked away from:

> "Not good — the soil creature being by himself. I will make for him a help as one before him."

The Hebrew *kenegdo* — "as one facing him," "as his corresponding counterpart." Male and female as a complementary fit. After Yahweh shapes the woman from the man's side and brings her to him, Adam **stutters with joy**:

> "*Zot* — this one, this time, bone of my bones, flesh of my flesh — to *this one* will be called woman, because from a man *this one* was taken."

The Hebrew triples the word — *zot, zot, zot* — Adam is rapturous. The text concludes: "they were both nude and they were not ashamed." This is the foundational picture of sexuality in the Hebrew Bible — celebrated, not tolerated. The **first command** in the Bible is "be fruitful and multiply and fill the earth." Tabor cites the old joke: this is the only command humans have ever obeyed. The Hebrew Bible is, in his words, "very frank about sexuality." Embodied union is creational, not concessional.

### How the Hellenistic Aversion to the Body Captured Christianity

The fateful sequence Tabor traces:

1. Paul, writing under apocalyptic pressure in the 50s CE, says celibacy is "good" — with reciprocal application to men and women, and with the time-is-short caveat repeated throughout.
2. The end doesn't come. But the language stays in the canon.
3. The Pastoral Epistles (1 Timothy and others) detach celibacy from apocalypticism and reframe it as a mark of devotion.
4. Hellenistic philosophy — particularly **neoplatonism** — runs through the Mediterranean intellectual world with its sharp dualism: the body is lower, the spirit is higher; pleasure is danger; the goal is to escape embodiment.
5. **Augustine** — whom Tabor calls "the model for Christianity, this otherworldly Christianity that wants to leave the world behind and leave sexuality behind" — codifies it. Augustine sleeps on the floor. He refuses comfortable food. He won't listen to music that might stir earthly passions. His *Confessions* is one long agonized testimony of a man at war with his own body.
6. Two-tier system: clergy and monastics live the "higher" celibate life; laypeople settle for marriage as the next-best option. The aversion to embodied sexuality becomes Christianity's default texture for over a thousand years.

### Why This Is a "Fateful" Passage

Tabor's verdict: this verse has done damage at scale. Billions of people across two thousand years have been taught that their natural desires — to love, to be loved, to express union through sexuality — are weakness, danger, or sin to be repressed. The damage is twofold:

1. **Personal harm.** People living under impossible standards, suppressing what creational theology actually celebrates, often hiding the resulting wounds (sometimes catastrophically — see the Catholic abuse crises).
2. **Theological severance.** Christianity is cut off from its Hebraic root. The Hebrew Bible's frank, embodied, joyful theology of sexuality is replaced by a Greek-flavored escape-from-the-body framework. The "hallelujah chorus" of Adam meeting Eve is muted. The "be fruitful and multiply" of Genesis 1 is treated as an indignity. And the church becomes, in significant measure, a religion *against* the body — which is exactly the opposite of what its Jewish foundation was.

### Tabor's Notre Dame Anecdote — The Two-Tier System in Real Life

Tabor closes the video with a personal coda. He taught New Testament at Notre Dame straight out of graduate school. Roughly 75% of his undergraduates came from Roman Catholic backgrounds. When the conversation in class turned to celibacy and the priesthood, students would open up about uncles or brothers or relatives who had taken vows. The phrase Tabor kept hearing: **"that is a ticket to heaven."** The implicit theology: God especially rewards those who sacrifice the most, who deny themselves the most, who hurt the most. And the speakers knew they weren't going to do it themselves — celibacy was for the saintly few. Tabor flags this as the lived consequence of the fateful verse: a system that produces both unreachable holiness ideals and quiet self-condemnation in everyone who can't reach them.

## Misconceptions Addressed

| Misconception | What the Text Actually Says / Tabor's Reading |
|---------------|-----------------------------------------------|
| Paul taught that celibacy is universally preferable to marriage as a timeless Christian ideal | Paul recommended celibacy specifically *in view of the present distress* and because *the appointed time has grown very short*. | Paul ordered Christians not to marry | Paul explicitly says: "I say this *by way of concession, not of command*"; "if you marry, you do not sin"; "if a girl marries, she does not sin." Paul recommends, he does not require |
| The Bible has always taught that single celibate devotion is spiritually higher than marriage | The Hebrew Bible — the foundational scripture Jesus and Paul both inherited — celebrates embodied sexual union as creational good. Genesis 2: "not good" for the human to be alone. Genesis 1: be fruitful and multiply (the first command). The "celibacy is higher" framework is post-Pauline and Hellenistic |
| Roman Catholic priestly celibacy comes directly from Paul's teaching | It comes from the *extension* of Paul's apocalyptic preference into a permanent mark of holiness, mediated through the Pastoral Epistles, neoplatonism, and Augustinian theology. Paul himself made the recommendation contingent on a five-to-ten-year apocalyptic timeline that did not materialize |
| Marriage in Christian theology is primarily a remedy for sexual sin | This is Paul's framing in 1 Corinthians 7:2 — "because of the temptation to *porneia*." It is not the Hebrew Bible's framing. Genesis 2 frames marriage as creational completion, not damage control |
| Paul was anti-woman and anti-sex | The text is more complex. Paul applies celibate preference *reciprocally* — to men and to women — and treats them as moral equals on this point. | The Pastoral Epistles (1 Timothy etc.) reflect Paul's own developed thought | Most critical scholars, Tabor included, treat the Pastorals as **non-Pauline or heavily edited** — written by later admirers extending Pauline ideas into church discipline. | Augustine's *Confessions* represents mainstream Christian thought about the body | Augustine represents the *Hellenized* form of Christian thought — heavily shaped by neoplatonism's body/spirit dualism.
