## Summary

This is Tabor and Robyn Faith Walsh in conversation — two scholars who have worked together repeatedly (see #30 where she interviews him on Mark-as-Pauline) — recorded as a teaser for Bart Ehrman's *New Investigations of the New Testament 2024* conference on Paul, where they were both featured speakers. The first 15 minutes are conference setup (Walsh's bio, her *Origins of Early Christian Literature* book on Cambridge, her two MythVision courses with Derek Lambert, the conference roster — Paula Fredriksen, Joel Marcus, Jason Staples, Ehrman himself). The substantive scholarly conversation begins around the 20-minute mark when Tabor pitches Walsh his "Johnny Carson softball" question: what does the broader Greco-Roman / archaeological / philosophical-context approach to Paul give us that the standard theological "open your study Bible and read Romans" approach misses?

Walsh's answer is the spine of her book: Christianity was not a Big Bang. The standard model — Jesus dies, rises, and the message just spreads because it's self-evidently true — is sociologically incoherent. What we actually have in Paul is an elite-literate first-century Jewish writer producing texts in a Greco-Roman literary culture, deploying middle-Platonist and Stoic frameworks fluently, in conversation with other intellectuals who were doing similar work (Philo most obviously). Paul is "an excellent middle Platonist for his time period." His *pneuma* is not Trinitarian Spirit-as-personified-actor but a Stoic physical substance. His talk of the moon and the third heaven and degrees of *doxa* (glory) in 1 Corinthians 15 is not poetic flourish — it's cosmology, with technical philosophical referents you can look up in Plutarch and Lucian. Walsh reports she has a Harvard Theological Review article in proofs on exactly this: Paul talking about the moon as a clearinghouse for souls, ether circling around it, where most middle Platonists thought the soul went after death. This is Paul on the actual ancient-physics terms his audience would have recognized.

The other major scholarly move — credited to Walsh's dissertation advisor Stanley Stowers — is the **subjective vs. objective genitive** problem. In Paul's Greek, when he writes about Moses's *pistis* and Abraham's *pistis*, every translation renders it "faithfulness of Moses, faithfulness of Abraham" — their fidelity to God's plan, the predicate of covenant. But when the identical construction appears with Jesus, suddenly translators flip it: "faith *in* Jesus" rather than "faithfulness *of* Jesus." That's not a grammatical fact; it's a theological decision baked into the English by translators who already believed Pauline soteriology required individual belief in Jesus as its mechanism. The Greek itself reads Jesus's *pistis* the same way it reads Abraham's. The whole "justification by faith" controversy of the Reformation may sit on top of a translator's thumb on the scale. Tabor and Walsh close with their favorite Pauline passages — both pick 1 Corinthians 15 for its cosmology, with Tabor adding Romans 8 (the cosmos itself "subjected to futility," running down like the second law of thermodynamics, awaiting transformation) — and a riff on the philosophical seriousness with which Paul treats space, heavenly spheres, and the dangerous journey "up there."

## Key Teachings

### Walsh's Framework — Christianity Is a Literary Production of Greco-Roman Elite Culture

The thesis of Walsh's *The Origins of Early Christian Literature: Contextualizing the New Testament Within Greco-Roman Literary Culture* (Cambridge, 2021). The standard "Big Bang" model — Jesus rose, the message spread, communities formed, gospels emerged from oral tradition — is sociologically implausible. What we actually have is a set of literate elite writers producing texts for other literate elite readers within Greco-Roman literary conventions. Paul is not transcribing the simple faith of fishermen. He is operating inside a sophisticated intellectual culture where Jewish scripture, Platonic philosophy, Stoic physics, and Roman politics are all available raw material, and where the prestige move is to claim that whatever the Greeks said well, the Hebrews said first and better.

Walsh: "It's not just that Jesus is born, died, is resurrected, and therefore everybody just automatically understands and when Paul comes to the door you answer and you say yes Paul I'm going to abandon my whole life and follow what you're saying about a Judean peasant who was executed. That doesn't make a great deal of sense to me as a social formation."

### Paul as Middle Platonist

Walsh's headline claim for the conference: "Paul is an excellent middle Platonist for his time period." That doesn't mean Paul was a Greek philosopher who incidentally wrote about Jesus. It means Paul was educated in a Jewish-Hellenistic environment where engagement with Platonic and Stoic ideas was the basic literacy of anyone "who wants to hobnob in those kind of intellectual ideas." He uses platonic categories. He uses Stoic cosmology. He combines them in a recognizably mid-first-century philosophical idiom. To read Paul as just a "Pharisee son of a Pharisee" without the middle-Platonist layer is to read half the document.

Tabor's frame: when you look up at the sky, you don't think the moon is made of cheese, but if you didn't know modern cosmology you might. Paul had a cosmology — an actual map of the heavens, the spheres, the third heaven, the ether around the moon. Romans 8's "creation subjected to futility" is Stoic physics, not poetic metaphor.

### Pneuma Is a Stoic Physical Substance, Not Trinitarian Spirit

The single most-cited example in the conversation of how mistranslation has flattened Paul. *Pneuma* (πνεῦμα) in Greek philosophy — and Walsh insists in Paul's Greek too — is a physical substance. It is the active stuff that circulates in the cosmos and in the human body. It has properties. It does work. Stoic *pneuma* is what holds matter together; it has tension; it has degree.

When Paul uses *pneuma* — "you have received the Spirit of adoption," "the Spirit groans within us," the resurrection body as "spiritual body" — he is not describing the third person of the Trinity. He is describing a physical-cosmic substance with technical properties. Once you read Paul (and Mark, and John) with the Stoic-physical *pneuma* in view, "First Corinthians 15 makes a heck of a lot more sense than the Trinitarian model that you get later on where it just becomes kind of this personified actor in human events." Walsh: "That is not how it works in Paul. It is not how it works in Mark."

This is the same critique Tabor has made about Mark's resurrection (see #30): the original Jesus-movement framework is transformation into a *pneumatic* body — a body made of *pneuma*-stuff — not a resuscitated corpse and not a disembodied ghost.

### The Subjective vs. Objective Genitive — "Faith in Jesus" Is a Translator's Choice

The most consequential Greek-grammar point in the conversation. Credited to Stanley Stowers (Walsh's PhD advisor at Brown). In Pauline Greek:

- *pistis Mōuseōs* — "faith/faithfulness of Moses" (subjective genitive: Moses's own faithfulness)
- *pistis Abraam* — "faith/faithfulness of Abraham" (subjective: Abraham's own faithfulness)
- *pistis Iēsou Christou* — should grammatically read "faith/faithfulness *of* Jesus Christ" (subjective: Jesus's own faithfulness)

Translators consistently render the Moses and Abraham constructions as the subjective genitive (their faithfulness toward God) but flip to the objective genitive when Jesus appears (our faith *in* Jesus). Walsh: "Paul can be talking about how the faithfulness of these actors in human history — their faith in God's plan — is the predicate for a covenant. Works for Moses, Abraham. But for some reason when you get to Jesus, your translation is going to change it to faith *in* Jesus. That's a theological decision."

The implication: Pauline soteriology may not be primarily about individual believers believing in Jesus. It may be about Jesus's own faithfulness as the predicate for the covenant — the same way Abraham's faithfulness was. The entire Reformation argument about *sola fide* may have been an argument over a translator's choice.

### Romans Is Paul's Spiritual CV — He's Asking the Romans to Sponsor Him

Walsh's framing of Romans, the latest of Paul's authentic letters and the one written to a community he has not founded and never met. "He is basically presenting what I've called his spiritual CV. It's like his resume. He's trying to say to these Romans this is why you should sponsor me and bring me to Rome to talk to you more about these ideas."

This reframes Romans entirely. It's not the systematic theological treatise the later church reads it as. It's Paul putting his best foot forward to a community he wants resources from — diplomatically, carefully, presenting the "best of Paul" because he needs them to fund his Spain mission (Romans 15). The intensity, the structure, the rhetorical buildup of chapters 1-3 — that's not a calm doctrinal exposition; that's an apostle auditioning. Knowing this lets you read Romans with the right ear: it's persuasive rhetoric to strangers, not catechism for the already-converted.

It also dissolves the "every Pauline letter implies a cohesive Pauline community that takes Paul as gospel" assumption. The Roman *ecclesia* is "Jesus-curious people, whatever term we want to use" — a community that has coalesced without Paul, that Paul is trying to win over, that may not actually buy what he's selling.

### The Antiquity Argument — Moses Said It Before Plato

Walsh on the rhetorical move shared by Paul, Philo, and Josephus: whatever you Greeks find admirable in Plato or the Stoics, Moses said it first and better. Antiquity equals authority in the ancient Mediterranean. Josephus's *Antiquities of the Jews* (Genesis 1:1 forward to Alexander) is structured around this claim. Philo of Alexandria makes the same move. Paul, when he engages middle Platonism, is not converting Plato into a Jew — he's claiming that Plato got it from the Jews, who got it directly from God.

"Whatever you think is great about Platonism, whatever you think is great about any of these philosophers or these moral systems or cosmology, what have you, whatever you think is great was already in the Jewish scriptures. Moses already said it before Plato. This is far more ancient. And in this period — and I would say probably today — there's a degree to which this is still the case: the more ancient something is, the more you could claim it."

Tabor adds that Josephus argued this explicitly with the Greek philosopher Apion: "Your people were living in caves when we were intellectually... you know, he does say that." The point: when Paul deploys philosophical categories, he's working *within* a literary culture where the prestige move is to baptize Greek philosophy as derivative of older Jewish revelation.

### Paul Doesn't "Convert" to Christianity — That Word Isn't in His Vocabulary

Both scholars agree (citing Paula Fredriksen) that "conversion" is the wrong English word for what happens to Paul on the road to Damascus. Paul does not convert *from* Judaism *to* Christianity. Christianity as a distinct religion does not exist yet in Paul's lifetime. What Paul thinks is happening: the God of Israel has acted again, sent the long-awaited Messiah, and is now incorporating Gentiles into the existing Jewish covenant story. "He sees this as a Jewish movement but one that's incorporating the Gentiles. God — the God of Israel — has now acted again, and you guys need to catch up on his latest activities."

This matters because the entire later Christian self-understanding — Paul as the founder of a new religion that supersedes Judaism — is a backward projection. Acts wants you to believe Paul converted. Paul's own letters don't read that way. He's a Jewish apostle to Gentiles, working out what Gentile inclusion in a Jewish messianic movement looks like.

### Mark Is "Pristine" Pauline Christianity — But Pauline-Jewish, Not Pauline-Christian

A briefer riff that connects to Tabor's main thesis (developed at length in #30). When you understand Paul as middle Platonist working in Jewish messianic categories, not as proto-Trinitarian Christian, the implication for Mark changes. Mark is reflecting Paul's actual theology — Adam Christology, *pneumatic* resurrection body, Stoic cosmology, the suffering-servant ethic — not the later Trinitarian-Christian theology that gets read back into him.

The conference paper Walsh is presenting on Paul-as-middle-Platonist intersects directly with Tabor's Mark-as-Pauline thesis from #30: if Mark is downstream of Paul, and Paul is middle Platonist, then Mark is also middle Platonist in its underlying conceptual world, even when the genre is biographical narrative rather than letter.

### Paul's Cosmology — The Moon, the Third Heaven, the Stars and the Glory

Walsh's favorite Pauline passage and the subject of her forthcoming *Harvard Theological Review* article: 1 Corinthians 15's discussion of *doxa* (glory) and the heavenly bodies. Paul talks about the moon a lot. He talks about the stars. He talks about the third heaven (2 Corinthians 12). He uses *doxa* as a quasi-technical philosophical term, not just "shininess." Most middle Platonists, Walsh notes, thought the soul after death "is going to shoot up into the atmosphere" — to a clearinghouse around the moon, made of the same ether substance that circles the moon.

If you read 1 Corinthians 15 with that cosmology in view, Paul is not waving his hands poetically — he is making a technically informed claim about where transformed bodies go and what they are made of. Tabor adds: "He's scientific in his own way. You got to know your way around those heavenly spheres. You can't just sort of head up there. It's dangerous." This is the same Paul who wrote *Paul's Ascent to Paradise* (Tabor's dissertation) — the mystic who went up and came back changed.

### Mark 7's Ritual Purity, Not Dietary Law — Same Point Walsh Confirms

In passing, the same point Tabor hammers in #30 surfaces again: when Paul (and Mark) deal with food laws and purity, they are dealing with **ritual purity** in a Mediterranean context where every cult had purity protocols (Walsh nods to the Athena cult parallels Tabor has on his blog), not "kosher vs. non-kosher." Paul's "the kingdom of God is not food and drink... touch not, taste not, handle not" is the same Stoic-Pauline ethical-interiority move that Mark 7 dramatizes. The misreading "Jesus declared all foods clean" is a translator's gloss; the Greek does not say it.
