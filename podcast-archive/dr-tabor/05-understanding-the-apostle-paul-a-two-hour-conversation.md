## Summary

This is Tabor's definitive long-form unpacking of his book *Paul and Jesus*. In contrast to the more biographically-oriented conversation with Christopher Enoch (Video #10), David LeBlanc — a Hebrew Bible / Kabbalah scholar transitioning out of Christianity into Jewish thought — pushes the theological conversation harder. The result is the densest single Tabor lecture on Paul's actual gospel: what it *is*, why it diverges from Jerusalem-Nazarene Christianity, and where Tabor parts ways with everyone from N.T. Wright to Robert Price.

The core methodological move (which Tabor repeats from Video #04 but expands here): **read the New Testament backwards**. Start with the seven undisputed Pauline letters (Galatians, 1 Thessalonians, 1-2 Corinthians, Philippians, Philemon, Romans) — all written in the 50s CE, all predating every gospel. Don't read Matthew and harmonize Paul into it. Read Paul first, in his own voice, and then notice that **Paul never met Jesus** — a fact most Christians have never consciously processed. Paul's gospel is his own ("my gospel," he repeatedly says), received "by revelation of Jesus Christ," not transmitted from the Jerusalem apostles. He says explicitly in Galatians 1-2 that the pillars (James, Peter, John) "added nothing to me" and that what they are "means nothing to me." This is not collegial Christianity; Paul claims direct divine authority over and above the people who actually walked with Jesus.

Then the theological core. Paul's gospel is not "believe in Jesus and your sins are forgiven and you go to heaven when you die." That popular summary, Tabor insists, is alien to Paul. Paul's gospel is **cosmic salvation** — the transformation of the entire material order, in which mortal flesh-and-blood humans are metamorphosed into "life-giving spirits" (1 Cor 15) and the cosmos itself is birthed into a "new heavens and new earth." Christ is the firstfruits, the prototype, the first of a coming family of glorified sons of God who will rule with him over angels and powers. Romans 8's "cosmos groaning in labor pains" is the central image. Philippians 2 is best read as **Adam Christology**: Jesus, like Adam, was in the form of God; unlike Adam, he didn't "grasp" at equality but humbled himself — and was therefore exalted to become the cosmic head of redeemed humanity. The "blood of Jesus" is not the mechanism of individual sin-forgiveness in isolation; it's how the cosmic transformation was inaugurated. The Last Supper, on Bruce Chilton's reading Tabor endorses, was Jesus rejecting the Passover lamb in favor of bread and wine as anticipations of the Messianic banquet — not cannibalistic mystical participation in his body.

Closing themes worth flagging: Tabor reads Romans 1-3 as a **hypothetical setup-then-takedown** (Romans 2 sounds Jewish-Yom-Kippur-friendly: judgment by works, written-on-the-heart Torah for Gentiles, "doers of the Torah will be justified" — then Romans 3 pulls the rug: nobody actually qualifies, so we need the blood). He dismisses Robert Price's Dutch-critical hypothesis (that all Pauline letters are 2nd-century Marcionite forgeries) on internal-rhetorical grounds — Tabor has read the seven letters in Greek 100+ times and the "tells" of authentic Paul are unmistakable. He endorses Albert Schweitzer's *Mysticism of Paul the Apostle* as "the greatest book on Paul ever written" — the apocalyptic-mysticism reading is the master key. And he closes with the Schweitzer challenge: **what Paul most expected didn't happen.** The parousia didn't come. The cosmos isn't transformed. We're still in the present age. So how do you read Paul honestly without either flattening him into modern Evangelicalism or dismissing him as a failed apocalypticist? That tension is the real homework Paul leaves us with.

## Key Teachings

### Read the New Testament Backwards — Start with Primary Paul

Tabor's method, repeated and elaborated. The standard reading order (Matthew → Mark → Luke → John → Acts → Romans → ...) is chronologically backwards. The seven undisputed Pauline letters were written in the 50s CE, decades before any gospel. To understand the Jesus movement *before* it becomes Christianity-around-Paul, start there:

1. **Galatians** — earliest or near-earliest; Paul's autobiographical apologia
2. **1 Thessalonians** — earliest or near-earliest; pure parousia eschatology
3. **1 Corinthians** — community problems; the cosmic resurrection chapter (15)
4. **2 Corinthians** — defensive; Paul's mystical experiences (chapter 12)
5. **Philippians** — Adam Christology (chapter 2); late-Paul reflection
6. **Philemon** — single-page letter about Onesimus the slave
7. **Romans** — the formal treatise, last in time and most systematic

These seven can be dated 49-58 CE. Everything else — Ephesians, Colossians, 2 Thessalonians, the Pastorals, Hebrews — is deutero-Pauline or pseudonymous and shouldn't establish Paul's core theology.

### Paul Never Met Jesus

The fact most Christians have never processed. Tabor reports lecturing on this around the world and watching audiences register surprise. Paul met the resurrected Jesus in visions (his own claim), but he never sat at Jesus's feet, never heard his teachings firsthand, never walked the dusty roads of Galilee with him. Everything Paul knows about Jesus's earthly life came secondhand — and Paul is striking in how little he engages it. He almost never quotes Jesus. He almost never references Jesus's parables, his Sermon-on-the-Mount ethics, his temple critique. The historical Jesus is largely absent from Paul.

This matters because: if you've been catechized to assume "Paul carries on what Jesus taught," you've been catechized to merge two figures who never met and whose theological emphases are dramatically different.

### "My Gospel" — Paul's Independent Authority Claim

In Galatians 1-2, Paul makes a striking series of claims:
- "I did not receive [my gospel] from man, nor was I taught it"
- "I received it by revelation of Jesus Christ"
- "When God revealed his Son to me... I did not consult flesh and blood"
- "I did not go up to Jerusalem to those who were apostles before me"
- Even after meeting them: "What they are means nothing to me"
- "The pillars (James, Peter, John)... added nothing to me"
- "If we or an angel from heaven preach any other gospel... let him be anathema [accursed]"

Tabor reads these statements as Paul claiming **direct apostolic authority equal or superior to the original Twelve and James**. Not collegial humility. Paul positions himself as the recipient of a divine revelation that the Jerusalem leadership doesn't share — and he is willing to consign opponents (including potentially angelic messengers) to gehenna over it.

This is the key context for understanding why Paul's letters work the way they do. He's not a missionary explaining the shared faith. He's a prophet contending for *his* gospel against rival versions.

### The Galatians 2 / Acts 15 Question

The Jerusalem meeting. In Galatians, Paul says he went up "by revelation" with Titus (uncircumcised Greek believer) as "show and tell." The question on the table: do Gentile believers need to convert to Judaism (circumcision, full Torah obligation) to be "saved"?

Paul reports: the pillars agreed Gentiles don't need to convert. Right hand of fellowship extended. Paul to the uncircumcised, Peter to the circumcised. Operationally, this looks like a Noahide-laws-plus-Jesus framework for Gentile believers — basic morality, no idolatry, no eating blood, no sexual immorality (which has specific Torah definitions), and faith in Jesus as Messiah.

But Tabor's careful read: this agreement covers the *operational* question of Gentile inclusion. It does NOT cover Paul's deeper theological framework — cosmic salvation, the imminent parousia, Paul's "mystery hidden from the ages," the Adam Christology of Philippians 2. Whether James, Peter, and John shared all that, we don't know. They may have agreed on Gentile inclusion while operating from a quite different theological universe.

### Romans 1-3 as Setup and Takedown

Tabor's pedagogically useful reading of the hardest two chapters in Paul:

**Romans 1** describes God's wrath against general sinfulness and idolatry — sets up the universal-human-problem frame.

**Romans 2** is the "hypothetical" Jewish-friendly chapter. Paul lays out a judgment-by-works framework that any first-century rabbi would affirm:
- "It is not the hearers of the Torah who will be justified, but the doers"
- God is no respecter of persons
- To those who patiently do good — glory, immortality
- To those who do evil — wrath
- "When Gentiles who do not have the law do by nature what the law requires... they are a law to themselves... the work of the law written on their hearts"
- "A real Jew is one inwardly, circumcision is of the heart"

If you stopped reading at Romans 2, Paul sounds exactly like rabbinic Judaism on Yom Kippur — works matter, heart matters, all humanity is judged on its actual deeds.

**Romans 3** is the rug-pull. "None is righteous, no not one." Paul quotes Psalms and Isaiah to establish total depravity. Now the works-based framework can't save anyone, because no one passes the test. The mechanism becomes the blood of Jesus as propitiation. Christianity diverges from Judaism precisely here — not in Romans 2's framework, but in Romans 3's "but nobody qualifies, so we need the blood."

A rabbi reading Paul, Tabor says, would agree wholeheartedly through Romans 2 and then say, "Yes, we all need grace — but I don't see why the blood of Jesus is required when Judaism has always taught grace and forgiveness through repentance and God's mercy."

### Paul's Mistranslations of Habakkuk 2:4 and Genesis 15:6

(Same content as Video #02, recapped briefly here.) Paul's two foundational misreadings:

- **Habakkuk 2:4** — Paul renders "he who through faith is righteous shall live" (justification by faith). Original context: a righteous person waits trustingly for God's delayed promise. Not how to *get* righteous; how a righteous person endures.
- **Genesis 15:6** — Paul renders "Abraham believed God and it was reckoned to him as righteousness" (Abraham justified by faith before circumcision). Better translation: Abraham *counted God* as righteous to fulfill the promise of a son.

Both verses are loadbearing for justification-by-faith theology. Both are misreadings. Tabor notes this is "what stopped Luther in his tracks" — the Habakkuk misreading became the foundation of Protestant theology.

### Cosmic Salvation, Not Just Personal Forgiveness

This is Tabor's headline correction of popular Christianity. The standard Evangelical summary — "believe in Jesus, get your sins forgiven, die and go to heaven" — is **not Paul's gospel**. Paul's gospel is:

- The entire material cosmos is in bondage to decay (Romans 8)
- The cosmos is "groaning in labor pains" — birthing a new creation
- Christ is the firstfruits of a glorified humanity who will be transformed (1 Cor 15)
- Believers will be metamorphosed at the parousia from corruptible flesh to incorruptible "life-giving spirit" bodies (1 Cor 15:45)
- This new humanity will rule angels and powers, sitting at the right hand of God with Christ
- Eventually death itself will be destroyed and God will be "all in all"

The forgiveness-of-sins piece is a *prerequisite* for participation in cosmic transformation — not the goal. The goal is the new creation. Read this way, "die and go to heaven" isn't even close to Paul's framework. Paul doesn't think you go to heaven when you die; he thinks the dead are "asleep" until the parousia, at which point the dead are raised and the living are transformed *together* (1 Thess 4).

### Philippians 2 as Adam Christology

Tabor argues for an Adam-Christology reading of the famous Philippians 2:5-11 "Christ hymn":

- "Christ Jesus, being in the form of God" — like Adam, created in God's image
- "Did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped" — unlike Adam and Eve, who grasped the fruit
- "Emptied himself, taking the form of a servant" — the humility Adam didn't have
- "Was obedient unto death" — reversing Adam's disobedience
- "Therefore God highly exalted him" — Christ as new Adam, head of a new humanity
- "Have this mind in you which was also in Christ Jesus" — the path believers are also called to walk

The standard Christological reading (Christ as pre-existent divine being who descends to earth) is one option but, Tabor argues, an Adam-Christology reading is at least as well-supported textually and fits Paul's framework better. Christ isn't a uniquely-divine god-man category; he's the prototype of the transformed humanity all believers will become.

This is the single most important Christological move in the playlist so far. It strips Paul of the Trinitarian / Incarnational metaphysics later imposed on him.

### The Last Supper as Anti-Passover-Lamb Statement (Bruce Chilton's Reading)

Tabor endorses Bruce Chilton's reconstruction of the Last Supper. The standard reading is that Jesus instituted a new mystical participation — eat my flesh, drink my blood, this is my body. Chilton's reading: Jesus rejects the Passover lamb. The disciples ask about the lamb. Jesus says: this bread, this cup — this is what we need. Not the lamb. The Last Supper is anti-sacrifice, anti-Passover-lamb, anticipating the Messianic banquet rather than initiating a new sacrificial cult.

This fits broader patterns in the Jesus tradition: John the Baptizer's vegetarianism, James the brother of Jesus as a vegetarian, the Essene anti-sacrifice tradition, the "I desire mercy, not sacrifice" thread from Hosea through Jeremiah through Jesus.

The standard Eucharistic theology (real presence, transubstantiation, Catholic/Protestant debates over substance) all builds on **Paul's** version from 1 Corinthians 11, which Paul says he received "from the Lord" — not from the Jerusalem community. Tabor allows that Paul may have meant either direct revelation or transmitted tradition, but the cannibalistic-mystical reading is Pauline, not Jesus-historical.

### Paul as a "Second Christ" Figure

A distinctive theme of this interview that doesn't get as much play elsewhere. Tabor argues Paul presents himself as a kind of secondary Christ figure:

- "I fill up what is lacking in the afflictions of Christ" (Colossians 1:24)
- "I bear in my body the stigmata [marks] of the Lord Jesus" (Galatians 6:17)
- "Follow me as I follow Christ" (1 Cor 11:1)
- The Isaiah 49 self-identification — "called from my mother's womb"
- The boast about being beaten, shipwrecked, stoned, left for dead (2 Cor 11) — suffering as participation in Christ's suffering

This isn't humility-language; it's a claim to be participating in the redemptive process *with* Christ — almost as a co-redemptive figure, the apostle to the Gentiles whose own sufferings extend Christ's mission. Paul as the first follower of Christ in a sense that matters cosmically, not just biographically.

### Jason Staples' Reading of Romans 9-11

Tabor enthusiastically endorses Jason Staples (Bart Ehrman's student, now at NC State) on Romans 9-11. Staples argues Paul's Gentile mission is actually the prophesied **regathering of scattered Israel** — the lost tribes of the northern kingdom who became "not my people" (Hosea), assimilated among the nations. When Paul says "all Israel will be saved" and "the fullness of the Gentiles come in" (Romans 11), he's working with Genesis 48's "fullness of the Gentiles" language about Ephraim's descendants among the nations.

The Gentile mission isn't a replacement of Israel. It's the restoration of scattered Israel plus a "mighty force of righteous" from the nations who attach themselves to the one God of Israel. This is Tabor's preferred frame for Paul's apostolic self-conception — not "founder of a new religion replacing Judaism," but "apostle of the eschatological regathering of all Israel and the nations."

### Albert Schweitzer's Apocalyptic-Mystical Paul

Tabor's recommendation: Schweitzer's *Mysticism of the Apostle Paul* (1930), "the greatest book on Paul ever written." Schweitzer reads Paul as apocalyptic-mystic at the core. Without the Dead Sea Scrolls (which weren't discovered until 1947), Schweitzer intuited that Paul's framework was Jewish apocalypticism with mystical participation — believers united with Christ in an eschatological process of transformation.

Tabor's *Paul's Ascent to Paradise* (his 1986 dissertation, revised and re-released as the book this interview also discusses) extends Schweitzer's framework by focusing on Paul's actual mystical experiences (Damascus road, the 2 Cor 12 ascent to paradise, the Galatians 1-2 revelations) as the *generative source* of Paul's theology — not abstract systematic reflection.

### The Dismissal of Robert Price / Dutch-Critical Mythicism

Robert Price (mythicist scholar) holds that all Pauline letters are 2nd-century forgeries — Marcionite productions retrojected onto a possibly-fictional 1st-century apostle. Tabor respects Price personally and reviewed Tabor's *Jesus Dynasty* negatively, but on Paul, Tabor is firmly negative: he has read the seven letters in Greek "at least 100 times" since age 17 and the *internal* tells of authentic 1st-century authorship are unmistakable. The rhythms, self-interruptions, undigested-detail moments, the specific Greek idioms — these don't read as second-century product.

A 2nd-century forger working backwards would have produced cleaner theology, fewer self-contradictions, less of the embarrassing autobiographical detail. Paul's letters have that "ring true" quality that even mythicists like Price generally accept (mythicists tend to be more comfortable denying Jesus's historicity than Paul's).

### Tabor's Eden Reading (Quick Aside Worth Flagging)

At the very end of the conversation Tabor mentions a separate project: an 8-part series with Jono Vandor on Genesis. Tabor's heterodox reading: **the eating of the forbidden fruit was not a fall** — it was supposed to happen. Moses says in Deuteronomy that "your little children, who do not yet know good and evil," will inherit the land — implying that *knowing* good and evil is a developmental telos, not a transgression. Paul says in Romans 7 "before I knew the Torah, I was alive; when the commandment came, sin sprang to life and I died" — Adam-and-Eve language.

This Eden reading aligns with Tabor's process-theist self-identification and his broader cosmic-transformation framework. The expulsion from Eden isn't punitive; it's developmental. Humanity is being birthed into the maturity required for cosmic redemption.

### The Schweitzer Challenge — What Paul Expected Didn't Happen

The closing reflection. Paul expected the parousia within his lifetime. He calibrated everything — celibacy, slavery, marriage advice, women's silence, calendar disputes, dietary leeway — to that imminent horizon. The horizon didn't arrive. Two thousand years later we're still in the "form of this world" Paul thought was passing away.

So how do you read Paul honestly? Options:
- **Preterist** — claim it all happened in 70 CE (Jerusalem destruction = parousia). Tabor: not what Paul meant; Paul never references 70 CE because he was probably dead by then.
- **Futurist** — "a day with the Lord is a thousand years." Tabor: the text doesn't read that way.
- **Mythologize** — Paul was wrong about the timing but right about the cosmic principle. This is the historical-critical position.
- **Dismiss** — Paul was a failed apocalypticist; ignore him.

Tabor's preferred move: read Paul as historically situated, in his moment, with the apocalyptic urgency that made every directive provisional. Then ask what survives translation: the cosmic vision of redemption, the Adam Christology, the call to embodied transformation rather than mere belief. The accidents (women silent, slaves stay slaves, marriage discouraged) were calibrated to a horizon that didn't arrive. The substance — humans transformed into glorified sons of God, the cosmos itself redeemed — that's what's worth retaining.

## Misconceptions Addressed

| Misconception | Tabor's Response |
|---------------|------------------|
| "Paul met Jesus" | Paul never met the historical Jesus. He met visions/revelations of Christ. He never sat at Jesus's feet, never heard him teach in Galilee, never broke bread with him. |
| "Paul's gospel is the apostles' gospel" | Paul claims direct revelation from Christ, independent of the Jerusalem apostles. "What they are means nothing to me" (Gal 2). He may have agreed with them operationally about Gentile inclusion, but the deeper framework — cosmic salvation, Adam Christology, the parousia mystery — is "my gospel." |
| "Paul's gospel is 'believe and go to heaven when you die'" | Paul's actual gospel is cosmic salvation — the transformation of the entire material order, with believers being metamorphosed into glorified life-giving spirits at the parousia. The popular summary is alien to Paul. |
