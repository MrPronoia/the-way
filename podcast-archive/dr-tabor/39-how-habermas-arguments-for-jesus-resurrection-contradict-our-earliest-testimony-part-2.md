## Summary

Part 2 picks up where Part 1 left off: if Paul's earliest testimony describes a visionary "lifting up" rather than a resuscitated corpse, where did the physical-body resurrection story come from? Tabor's answer is a step-by-step trajectory through the gospels in chronological order. **Mark** ends at the empty tomb with no appearances and points the disciples to Galilee. **Matthew** adds embellishments (earthquake, angel, soldiers, mountain encounter) but still keeps the appearances visionary — "some doubted." **Luke** is where the corpse starts eating fish. **John** has it both ways — flesh-and-bones for Thomas, walking through locked doors for everyone else. The trajectory is unmistakable: visionary lifting-up → embellished physical → resuscitated corpse with a side of "but also spiritual."

The structural insight of Part 2 is Tabor's "two tombs" reconstruction. Every source agrees Joseph of Arimathea handled the burial, but only **Matthew** says it was Joseph's *own* tomb — and Matthew adds that one word to fulfill Isaiah 53's "buried in a rich man's tomb" prophecy. **John** explicitly says it was a *nearby unused tomb* — an emergency burial before the High Sabbath. Tabor argues the obvious follow-up nobody talks about: Saturday night, as soon as the festival ended, Joseph's burial party would have *moved the body* to a permanent tomb. The women arrive Sunday morning to the emergency tomb and find it empty — not because Jesus walked out, but because the burial party relocated him the night before. John 20:1-10 fits this reconstruction perfectly: Mary Magdalene says "they have taken the Lord and we don't know where they laid him." That's not a confused report of a resurrection — that's a literal description of what happened.

The visionary experiences then begin in Galilee in the days and weeks that follow — first Peter, then the others, then years later Paul. Tabor's underlying claim: **the empty tomb and the visionary appearances are both real, but neither one is what evangelical apologetics claims it is.** The body was moved. The appearances were visionary "presence" experiences in a grief-shattered community. The later physical-encounter stories — Thomas's wounds, the breakfast on the beach, Jesus eating fish — are apologetic embellishments built decades later in response to skeptics like Celsus who pointed out that visions don't count as evidence.

## Key Teachings

### Where Did the Physical-Body Resurrection Idea Come From?

Tabor walks the gospels in chronological order to answer Habermas's framework. **Mark** (earliest) ends at 16:8 — women find the tomb empty, flee in terror, say nothing. No appearances. The three "longer endings" of Mark are later scribal additions; even Habermas would grant this. **Matthew** adds embellishments — earthquake, angel rolling back the stone, soldiers dropping like dead men, a mountain encounter in Galilee — but still keeps the appearances visionary in tone, noting that "some doubted" even after seeing the risen Jesus. **Luke** is where the corpse starts eating fish: "touch me, see my bones, I'm not a ghost." **John** combines both registers — Thomas touches wounds (apologetic concretization) but Jesus also walks through locked doors (epiphany-style appearance/disappearance). The trajectory is visionary → embellished → resuscitated corpse with a layer of "but also spiritual."

### The Two-Tombs Reconstruction

The hinge of Part 2. Every source agrees Joseph of Arimathea handled the burial. But notice what each source actually says:

- **Mark:** Joseph put him in a tomb. *Doesn't say it was Joseph's tomb.*
- **Luke:** Joseph put him in a tomb. *Doesn't say it was Joseph's tomb.*
- **Matthew:** Joseph put him in *his own new tomb*. (Matthew adds the word "own" to fulfill Isaiah 53's "buried in a rich man's tomb" prophecy.)
- **John:** Joseph put him in a *nearby unused tomb* — explicitly because the High Sabbath was beginning and they had to bury him fast.

John's chronology, Tabor argues, is superior. This was an **emergency burial** in a tomb of opportunity right near the crucifixion site, blocked up against predators. The plan would always have been to *move the body to a permanent tomb* as soon as the festival ended — Saturday night at sundown when the High Sabbath was over, or first thing Sunday before sunrise. The women arrive Sunday morning to the emergency tomb and find it empty because the burial party already relocated the body the night before.

### Mary Magdalene's Actual Words Confirm This

The single most overlooked passage in resurrection reconstruction, per Tabor, is **John 20:1-10**. Read it without theological glasses on:

> "Mary Magdalene came to the tomb early while it was still dark and saw the stone had been taken away. So she ran and went to Simon Peter and the other disciple and said, **'They have taken the Lord out of the tomb, and we do not know where they have laid him.'**"

That is not a confused or pious report of a resurrection. That is a literal description of *exactly what happened* if the burial party moved the body. The disciples run, see the empty tomb, see the discarded linen wrappings (bloody grave clothes — you don't take those with you), and *believe what they see* — that the tomb is empty. John 20:9 makes this explicit: "they did not yet know that he must rise from the dead." Their first response was not "he's risen" — it was "someone has moved him."

### The Gospel of Peter — Eight Days of Weeping

Tabor pulls in the non-canonical Gospel of Peter as corroboration. After the crucifixion, the disciples don't celebrate or share meals with a risen Jesus. They **weep and mourn** for the entire eight-day festival period:

> "It was the final day of unleavened bread, and many were leaving and returning home as the feast was over. But we, the twelve disciples of the Lord, wept and were sorrowful, and each one, sorrowful for what had come to pass, departed to his home. But I, Simon Peter, and my brother Andrew, having taken our nets, went off to the sea. And there was with us Levi..." *— fragment breaks off*

This is wildly incompatible with Luke and John's narratives of meetings, meals, and physical touch happening Easter Sunday onward. **Peter says they wept for eight days, then went fishing.** That's the despair-then-vision arc Tabor reconstructs — broken disciples retreat to Galilee, and only later begin to have the overwhelming visionary experiences of Jesus's "presence" that became the foundation of resurrection faith.

### Galilee vs. Jerusalem — A Geographic Contradiction the Gospels Can't Resolve

Mark, Matthew, and the Gospel of Peter all point to **Galilee** for the appearances. The young man at the tomb says "go to Galilee, you will see him there as he said." Matthew has the eleven travel to a mountain in Galilee. The Gospel of Peter has Peter going fishing on the Sea of Galilee. **Luke and John flip this entirely** — they have the disciples explicitly told *not* to leave Jerusalem for 40 days until Pentecost. The geographic contradiction is real and unresolvable. Tabor's read: Mark/Matthew/Peter preserve the earlier tradition (Galilee visionary appearances after a despair period). Luke is rewriting it to put bodily-encounter appearances in Jerusalem to satisfy second-century apologetic needs.

### Why the Flesh-and-Bones Apologetics Started

Tabor frames this bluntly. By the late first and early second century, critics like Celsus were saying: *"Some hysterical women thought they saw him at a tomb. Some deluded grief-stricken disciples thought they saw him. You're building a religion on that?"* It was devastating. So Luke and John add concrete physicality — Jesus eats fish, Thomas touches wounds, *"feel my bones, I'm not a ghost."* It's the only way to argue back against a Celsus-type skeptic. **The flesh-and-bones imagery is apologetic concretization, not historical memory.** Tabor jokes (but seriously notes) that early Church Fathers actually debated whether the risen Jesus had to go to the toilet during his 40 days of physical appearances — which is the kind of problem you only get into if you've smuggled resuscitation imagery into a resurrection framework.

### The 500 Witnesses Look Like a Marian Apparition

When Paul cites 500 witnesses (1 Cor 15:6), Tabor notes that's **not 500 independent eyewitness accounts** — it's one person reporting that 500 people all saw something at once. The analog he reaches for: appearances of the Virgin Mary in modern Catholic history. Crowds of people looking up at a sky and seeing/hearing something simultaneously. That's a well-documented religious phenomenon. It is *not* the same as 500 individual witnesses you could independently cross-examine. Tabor: "I'm not trying to trivialize it, but we know this pattern in the history of religions."

### The Romans Did Allow Burial Under Pilate

On the Ehrman/Crossan objection that crucifixion victims were typically left to rot or thrown in pits: Tabor disagrees. Under Pilate's specific circumstances — Tiberius watching from Capri, Sejanus running the empire, Pilate already in trouble for past disturbances and not wanting to cause a Passover riot — granting Joseph of Arimathea (a Sanhedrin member with connections) custody of the body to prevent a disturbance among Jesus's followers is exactly the kind of pragmatic concession Pilate would make. Craig Evans has argued this position in detail. Tabor agrees with the contour: Jesus was given an honorable (though hasty) burial because Joseph used his standing to request it and because pacifying the population during Passover week was politically necessary.

### What This Reconstruction Means for Christian Faith

Tabor closes with a striking point: *this reconstruction does not destroy Christian faith — it restores Paul's actual framework.* A Christian could fully accept everything Tabor lays out and still believe Jesus was raised in the Pauline sense — transformed into the "life-giving spirit" who appeared to Peter, James, the Twelve, the 500, and finally Paul himself. **Paul is not writing 1 Corinthians 15 to tell you about a resuscitated corpse.** He's describing a *metamorphosis* — dust-Adam transformed into life-giving spirit. Christ as firstfruits; many to follow. The Tabor reconstruction *fits Paul's theology better than the gospel-driven apologetic does*. The body in the tomb (wherever it ended up — possibly Talpiot, possibly elsewhere) is theologically irrelevant. "God doesn't care about bones. Bones are not resurrection."
