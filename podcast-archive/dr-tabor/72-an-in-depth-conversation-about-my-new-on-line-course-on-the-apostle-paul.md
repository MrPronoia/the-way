## Summary

This is the launch conversation for Tabor's MVP-Courses online course *Quest for the Historical Paul: Apostle or Apostate?* — recorded with Jacob Berman of History Valley. The first half is Tabor selling and unpacking the course: what's in the 50-page PDF guide, why he hands students a printed copy of just the seven undisputed letters (so everyone is working from the same translation — the Revised Standard Version, slightly more literal than the NRSV), how the twelve recorded lectures are structured around close textual exegesis rather than secondary-literature surveys, and what happens in the monthly six-month Zoom seminar that ships free with enrollment. The first upcoming Zoom (the day after this interview) is on "Did Paul Abolish the Law?" — Tabor flagging the recent scholarly trend (Paul-within-Judaism) that emphasizes Paul never left Torah, just offered one more interpretation of it alongside Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes, and the Dead Sea Scrolls community.

The second half turns substantive. Jacob asks the core methodological question: how do scholars decide what is authentic Paul versus interpolation, even within the seven letters? Tabor walks through the standard candidate passages — the brutal anti-Jewish polemic in 1 Thessalonians 2 ("they killed the Lord and the prophets"), the women-be-silent instruction in 1 Corinthians 14, the praise of Roman government in Romans 13, and the strange jumbled treatment of meat-sacrificed-to-idols across 1 Corinthians (free-and-liberal in one section, demon-haunted in another). Tabor's posture: **let Paul be Paul if possible — don't reach for the interpolation scalpel just because a passage offends modern sensibilities.** He pictures Paul pacing the floor dictating, ranting, backing off, contradicting himself — and he wants students to hear that messy, embodied, tempest-in-a-teapot voice rather than a sanitized doctrinal Paul. He opens Galatians 1 to demonstrate: Paul launches "an apostle sent neither by human commission nor human authority" before he's even said hello, then catches himself, throws in a grace-and-peace, and immediately returns to "I am astonished you are so quickly deserting." That self-interrupting rhythm is, for Tabor, the unmistakable signature of authentic first-century Paul.

Closing themes: Tabor's four-source separation method (early letters → later letters → Acts → legendary Paul, e.g. the *Acts of Paul*), the perennial chicken-and-egg "did Paul create Christianity?" question (Tabor leans Pauline-innovation, citing the silence of the Didache and the Epistle of James on anything resembling Pauline doctrine), and a striking revision to his earlier teaching: Tabor now doubts the traditional story that **Paul was beheaded under Nero in Rome around 64-68 CE.** First Clement (written in Rome decades after the supposed event) says Paul was released and went to Spain — and doesn't say he came back to Rome to die. The beheading tradition only emerges later (Irenaeus, then the *Acts of Paul*). Tabor floats his "movie scene" speculation: Nero's advisor reviews cases, sees the procurator's letter that Paul did nothing, releases him, and Paul heads to Spain — possibly hearing later about the 66 CE revolt and choosing not to return. Did Paul die still expecting the parousia? Did he ever lose faith when the kingdoms of the world failed to be overthrown? That question — the Schweitzer challenge in a different form — is the homework the course leaves with its students.

## Key Teachings

### Course Methodology — Why Just the Seven Letters

Tabor's structural decision for the course is to focus exclusively on the seven undisputed Pauline letters rather than attempting the full 13-letter canonical Paul (plus Acts of Paul and other apocryphal material). The reasoning is pedagogical: to get into the **core of Paul's central and earliest ideas**, you have to bracket the deutero-Pauline overlay. He prints all seven letters as a 50-page single-spaced packet handed to every student so the class works from a single shared translation (RSV) — preventing the chaos of five different students reading five different versions when Tabor says "this isn't translated right." Students are encouraged to leave blank pages in their packets and use it as a working notebook.

The order Tabor moves through them: 1 Thessalonians, Galatians, the Corinthian correspondence (1-2 Cor, which he treats as a multi-letter corpus), Romans, Philippians, Philemon. He's explicit that this isn't a hard claim about chronological order (Galatians' date in particular is unsettled) — it's the pedagogical sequence that lets the themes emerge from the text.

### The Last Two Lessons Are Q&A — Don't Read Ahead

A deliberate course-design move worth noting: the final two of the twelve lectures are Q&A sessions where Derek Lambert poses questions arising from the course material. Tabor recommends students **not** listen to these early. "Let's study it and then let's talk about the issues." The course is a close textual exegesis first, scholarly debate second. He doesn't want students arriving at the seven letters already pre-shaped by interpretive controversies.

### What the Monthly Zoom Seminar Is

The Zoom meetings — last Sunday of each month for six months after enrollment — function like a graduate seminar. Tabor lectures for ~45 minutes on a topic arising from the course, then opens it for ~75-90 minutes of Q&A. Around 100 students typically attend live, many keep cameras on, and Tabor explicitly cultivates the relational dimension: students sometimes connect via the chat for follow-up conversation. He compares this to Bart Ehrman's biblical studies academy — Tabor has built his own informal one, with quite a few repeat students taking multiple courses (Mark, Jesus and the Dead Sea Scrolls, now Paul).

The first Zoom topic (December 29, 2024): **"Did Paul Abolish the Law?"** Tabor flags this as the current scholarly trend — Paul-within-Judaism scholarship reframing Paul not as a departure from Torah but as one more sectarian interpretation of it. What does Paul mean by "the law"? What is the "Torah of Christ" he references? Does Paul Christ have his own Torah-text, or is "Torah" used as "teaching" in a broader sense?

### Authenticity Decisions — Let Paul Be Paul

Jacob's pointed question: how do you decide what's authentic Paul even within the seven letters? Tabor walks through the four classic candidate passages where scholars have proposed interpolation:

1. **1 Thessalonians 2** — the "they killed the Lord and the prophets" anti-Jewish tirade ("God's wrath has come upon them at last") that sounds out of character with Paul's love for his fellow Jews elsewhere.
2. **1 Corinthians 14** — the women-be-silent instruction citing Genesis ("even the Torah teaches this"), which scholars have suspected as a Pastoral-style interpolation working backwards into Paul.
3. **Romans 13** — the praise of government as God's agents bearing the sword, which sits uncomfortably with Paul's apocalyptic expectation of Rome's overthrow.
4. **The internal contradictions of 1 Corinthians on meat sacrificed to idols** — sometimes free-and-liberal ("an idol is nothing"), sometimes apocalyptically strict ("temples are full of demons, you cannot sit at table in an idol's temple").

Tabor's posture: don't reach for the interpolation scalpel just because a passage offends. **His default is to let Paul contradict himself**, because the seven letters as a whole reveal a "tempest in a teapot" personality — ranting and raving, then loving and gentle; praising, then upset; angry, then conciliatory. The contradictions are a feature, not a textual problem.

He tells students what the major scholars have argued (e.g., "many critical scholars say Paul did not write 1 Thessalonians 2") but refuses to take a dogmatic position himself. He'd rather flag the issue than excise the verse.

### Galatians 1 as the Demonstration of Authentic Pauline Style

To show students what authentic Paul *sounds like*, Tabor opens Galatians 1 in the interview. The opening lines:

> "Paul, an apostle, sent neither by human commission nor by human authority, but through Jesus Christ and God the Father who raised him from the dead, and all the members of God's family who are with me..."

Tabor's point: this is not how letters opened in the ancient world. Normally you'd say "grace and peace to you, I think of you often" — *then* establish your credentials. Paul leads with a defensive apostolic claim before he's even said hello. Tabor imagines the stenographer interrupting: "Paul, don't you want to say hello to them?" Next verse: Paul throws in a "grace and peace" — and then immediately reverts to the defensive mode, "I am astonished that you are so quickly deserting the one who called you."

This self-interrupting, ranting-then-recovering rhythm is, for Tabor, the unmistakable internal evidence that these letters are authentic first-century Paul rather than second-century forgery. A polished forger working backwards would have produced a more harmonious opening.

### The 1 Corinthians 1 Baptism Moment as the Smoking Gun

Tabor's favorite single passage demonstrating authentic Paul's voice — 1 Corinthians 1, where Paul is ranting about Corinthian factionalism ("I belong to Paul, I belong to Apollos, I belong to Cephas, I belong to Christ — has Christ been divided?"). Then Paul declares:

> "I thank God that I baptized none of you except Crispus and Gaius, so that no one can say you were baptized in my name."

Then mid-sentence: "I did baptize also the household of Stephanas... beyond that I don't know whether I baptized anyone else."

Tabor's reading: Paul is pacing the floor dictating, on a rhetorical roll, when the stenographer interrupts — "Paul, I hate to interrupt, but didn't you baptize Stephanas's household? I'm pretty sure you told me that you did." Paul: "Okay, okay, okay, put that in." Modern translations put the correction in parentheses to show the seam. **This is the kind of undigested autobiographical detail no forger inserts.** The gospels never do this. Mark has one wink at the reader ("let the reader understand"). Paul does it constantly, and Tabor reads that messy texture as the strongest internal evidence for authenticity.

### The Four-Source Separation Method

Early in the course Tabor teaches students to **separate out the four sources of Paul** and not bleed them into each other:

1. **The Seven Early Letters** (primary Paul, the only foundation for establishing Paul's actual views)
2. **The Later/Disputed Letters** (Ephesians, Colossians, 2 Thessalonians, the Pastorals — supplementary, possibly Pauline-school, not foundational)
3. **The Book of Acts** (sympathetic but theologically distinct — useful to supplement biography but never to override Paul's own words)
4. **Legendary Paul** (the *Acts of Paul*, where Paul is described as short and stocky and baldheaded; the 2 Peter tradition that Paul's letters are "scripture"; later hagiographical material)

The failure mode in pop-Christianity is that all four sources are blended into a single Paul. Tabor's discipline: when establishing what Paul actually thought, you cite from layer 1 only. Layer 2 can corroborate but never override. Layer 3 supplies geography and chronology but not doctrine. Layer 4 is interesting cultural memory but historically unreliable.

### The Already / Not-Yet Tension as Explanatory Frame

Tabor offers a specific theological frame for understanding Paul's apparent contradictions on gender, sexuality, and social ethics — the "already / not yet" tension developed in his book *Paul and Jesus*. Paul lives proleptically in the new creation where there is no male and female, slave and free (Galatians 3:28). But he also recognizes that the present age has not ended — there is still male and female, there are still slaves and free, there are still synagogue norms and household codes. So he oscillates.

The women-be-silent passage in 1 Corinthians 14 might be explained this way: Paul gets ahead of himself in the eschatological vision, then pulls back when he thinks about the actual social shape of the assemblies. He's worried about "decency and order" — synagogue-style propriety — when James or Peter might walk in and see how his Corinthian house churches operate. So a Paul who personally lives ahead of his time still imposes synagogue-style segregation when visitors from Jerusalem are looking over his shoulder.

This doesn't fully resolve the tension. Tabor doesn't pretend it does. But it explains why the same Paul can write Galatians 3:28 and 1 Corinthians 14 — and why neither needs to be excised as interpolation.

### Romans 13 — Paul Accommodating Roman Eyes

On the praise of government in Romans 13, Jacob asks if Tabor accepts it as authentic. Tabor: yes, with a contextual frame. Paul may have been writing knowing his letter to the Romans would be **read by people whose loyalty included Rome itself**. The verse functions as Paul's calling card — "I'm not a rebel, I'm not preaching insurrection, all governments are appointed by God." It's the public-relations move of an apocalypticist who knows the eschaton is coming but doesn't want to be crushed by Rome before it arrives.

Crucially, Tabor notes Romans 13's praise of government sits *next to* apocalyptic material — "the night is far spent, the day is at hand." Paul preserves both registers: outward loyalty to Roman order, inward expectation of its imminent overthrow. Both are authentically Paul.

### Did Paul Create Christianity? — The Chicken-and-Egg Question

The deepest question Tabor reserves for the sixth and final Zoom session of the course: did Paul create Christianity? Two broad framings:

1. **Paul innovates and adds things Jesus would be horrified by.** This is the "Jesus's authentic teaching got hijacked" thesis associated with much liberal scholarship, the Ebionites historically, and movements like The Way today.
2. **Jesus in heaven is inspiring Paul; Paul's revelation completes what Jesus began.** This is the orthodox-Christian harmonizing thesis — Paul is the thirteenth apostle receiving direct revelation.

The test case Tabor returns to: **the Last Supper.** In 1 Corinthians 11, Paul says "I received this from the Lord" — bread as body, wine as blood, the eucharistic words. Did Paul receive this from Peter or another disciple who was actually there? Or did he receive it directly by visionary revelation from the risen Christ? If the latter, then the entire eucharistic tradition flows from Paul, not from the historical Jesus's Last Supper.

Tabor's evidence for the Pauline-innovation reading:
- The **Didache** (early non-Pauline Christian manual) doesn't have Paul's doctrines.
- The **Epistle of James** "doesn't seem to be a shred of Pauline Christianity in that entire book — doesn't even seem Christian to most people."
- The **Ebionite and Nazarene materials** preserved in the Pseudo-Clementines transmit a non-Pauline Jewish-messianic Jesus tradition.

Tabor's conclusion in the interview: "I have a sneaking suspicion — more than a suspicion, I believe, or I've concluded — that the book of Acts smooths all of that over and basically domesticates it. It's sort of like okay, Jesus hands the baton to Peter who hands it to Paul, and then James comes in in chapter 15 and chapter 21 and says oh, we're all together, we've all agreed, this is the way everything is. And that's why I'm glad we have Paul's letters — because I don't get that sense when I read Paul's letters over against this other material."

### Tabor's Revised Position on Paul's Death

The most striking single moment in the interview. Tabor revises his earlier teaching about Paul's death:

> "You know, I never did [accept the beheading account]. I'm not even sure if I got it right in the course. But I think in one of the subsequent sessions Derek and I did, I might have clarified... Recently I was working on the deaths of Peter and Paul in Rome and the archaeological evidence and the tombs, and I've been to both tombs and studied them quite a bit. And I began to realize that it might be one of the things that we all say — but if you ask what is our earliest evidence, it's pretty late."

The evidence:
- **1 Clement** (written in Rome decades after the supposed beheading) says Paul was released and went to Spain. Implies Paul and Peter both suffered eventually, but doesn't say Paul came back to Rome to be beheaded.
- The earliest source explicitly claiming the Nero beheading is probably **Irenaeus** (late 2nd century), with the *Acts of Paul* providing details.
- The book of Acts itself, in its final section, has procurators and Agrippa repeatedly saying "what has this guy even done?" — possibly a motif of Paul's innocence, but also possibly historical memory of a Roman acquittal.

Tabor's "movie scene" speculation: Nero's advisor reviews case files, sees the procurator's letter saying Paul has done nothing, declares it a waste of imperial time, releases Paul, and Paul heads to Spain to complete his apostolic mission. (Romans 15-16 has Paul speaking of completing the Gentile mission "to the limits of the Roman world" — Spain is explicitly named.)

The further speculation: did Paul later hear about the 66 CE Jewish revolt, recall Mark 13's "when you see the desolating sacrilege of Daniel, the end is coming," head back toward the homeland — and then have to grapple with the fact that **nothing he expected actually happened**? The kingdoms of the world were not overthrown. Rome thrived. The "good emperors" (Marcus Aurelius and the philosopher-rulers) arrived. The parousia did not.

Would Paul, at the end, still have been able to say "wait for it, if it tarries it will surely come" (Habakkuk 2:3, echoed in 2 Peter)? Or would he have finally said "I don't know what to think"? The course leaves this open.

### Why Tabor Wants the Mess

A methodological cri de coeur that gives the interview its tone. Tabor:

> "If a book drops down from heaven and was dictated by an angel and it's all perfect, and now I've got the word of God — I wouldn't have got a PhD in a book like that. I want the messiness. If we had one gospel I would be so upset, I would be bored. I'd be bored to tears. We got like an inspired account that the angel Gabriel gave and it's perfect — it's like, oh, it's so boring. I want the mess and the tumble of reality, of history. Because I'm a historian of religion. So I have to say thank God for Paul — because we get the raw edge of somebody writing in the early days of the movement."

This is Tabor's answer to fundamentalist Paul-is-perfect inerrancy *and* to skeptical "Paul is too crazy to take seriously" dismissals. Both flatten the text. The seven letters preserve a living human voice — pacing, ranting, contradicting himself, catching himself, doubling back. That is the historical Paul. Anything smoother is editorial.

### Paul as Most Influential Western Figure

Tabor restates his standing claim — Paul is "by some measures, the most influential figure of Western history." Justification: most of what modern Christians think about life after death, gender, sexuality, the role of women, the meaning of life, the structure of salvation — comes from Paul, not from Jesus. The Sermon on the Mount is widely known but rarely operationally controlling. Paul's specific doctrines (women subservient to men, women asking their husbands at home, slaves staying slaves, government as God's agent) are the operational substrate of Western Christian culture.

Tabor mentions the 2024 American election and Project 2025 explicitly: "half the things people were debating went back to Paul. Had you ever thought of that? All these issues people are bringing up... they're Evangelical Christians and they think the United States is a Christian nation. So all kinds of reasons to take Paul — it'll help you read your daily newspaper."

## Misconceptions Addressed

| Common Belief | What Tabor Says |
|---------------|-----------------|
| Paul was beheaded by Nero in Rome around 64-68 CE | The earliest evidence (1 Clement) says he was released and went to Spain. The beheading tradition is late (Irenaeus, *Acts of Paul*). Tabor now doubts the standard story. |
| Paul's letters appear in chronological order in the Bible | They appear in size order (Romans longest, Philemon shortest). Most readers don't realize this. |
| All 13 (or 14) "Pauline" letters can be cited equally to establish Paul's views | Only the seven undisputed letters should ground Paul's actual positions. The Pastorals and others are deutero-Pauline at best. |
| Mary's wishful "Paul never said anything offensive" — passages like 1 Cor 14 must be interpolations | Possibly — but Tabor's default is to let Paul contradict himself rather than excise verses. He was a tempest in a teapot, not a systematic theologian. |
| Paul handed off doctrine that came from Peter and the disciples | Paul claims direct revelation. The Last Supper account in 1 Cor 11 says "I received from the Lord" — not from Peter. May be Paul's own innovation. |
| The book of Acts is a neutral history of early Christianity | Acts smooths over real conflict between Pauline and Jerusalem (James/Peter) Christianity. It domesticates tensions Paul's letters reveal. |
| Paul was a "convert" who left Judaism for Christianity | Most current scholarship rejects "convert" language — Paul saw himself as offering one more interpretation of Torah, not changing religions. |
| Paul abolished the Law/Torah | Trending scholarly view (Paul-within-Judaism): Paul never left Torah; he just interpreted it differently than Pharisees, Sadducees, or the Dead Sea Scrolls community. |
| The *Acts of Paul* gives us the historical Paul ("short, stocky, baldheaded") | This is legendary Paul — 2nd-century apocryphal material with no claim to historical accuracy. |
| 2 Peter claiming Paul's letters are "scripture" means Paul thought of them that way | Paul never says his letters are scripture. The 2 Peter framing is later canonical projection. |
