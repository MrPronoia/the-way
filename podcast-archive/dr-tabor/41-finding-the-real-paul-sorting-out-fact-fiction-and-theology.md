## Summary

This is one of Tabor's cleanest and most accessible single-session presentations of his "quest for the historical Paul" framework — pitched, in conversation with Paul Williams of Blogging Theology, to a religiously serious cross-traditional audience (Williams is a Muslim convert; Tabor explicitly nods to that throughout). The conversation has the feel of a survey lecture: Tabor opens his leather-bound RSV Bible as show-and-tell, lays out the "Four Pauls" taxonomy, walks through his three-rule source-hierarchy method, then spends the remaining hour applying it to the highest-stakes question — what Paul's actual gospel was, and how the Jamesian (Jerusalem) gospel got eclipsed.

The methodological core is **read the New Testament backwards**. Don't start with Matthew and read forward into Paul. Start with 1 Thessalonians — the earliest Christian document on the planet — and work outward. Recognize that the 13 letters bearing Paul's name are not equally Paul's: only seven are undisputed (1 Thessalonians, Galatians, 1-2 Corinthians, Romans, Philippians, Philemon). Ephesians and Colossians are "the crown jewels of Pauline theology" but are almost certainly rewritten / interpolated. The Pastorals (1-2 Timothy, Titus) are buyer-beware pseudonymous. And the Paul of Acts is the legendary, polemical, harmonized Paul — Luke's hero, but not the Paul of the authentic letters. Tabor's three rules: (1) never accept Acts over Paul's own account in the seven letters; (2) cautiously consider Acts when it agrees with Paul and shows no bias; (3) treat Acts's independent information as interesting but not interpretively authoritative.

The theological core is the contrast between **the religion of Jesus** and **the religion about Jesus**. Tabor reads the Last Supper of 1 Corinthians 11 ("this is my body... this is my blood") as deeply un-Jewish — drinking blood is one of the absolute taboos of Genesis 9 and the Torah, and James's Jerusalem council in Acts 15 explicitly forbids it for Gentile converts. So how did this practice originate? Tabor's answer: Paul received it not from the Jerusalem apostles but from a mystical-visionary encounter with the risen Jesus ("I received from the Lord," 1 Cor 11:23, paralleled by Galatians 1's "the gospel I preach I did not receive from men"). Mark's Gospel — written after Paul, with Paul "heavily in his head" — then back-projects Paul's Last Supper formula onto the historical Jesus. Luke, oddly, deletes Mark 10:45 ("the Son of Man came to give his life as a ransom for many") and tells the parable of the Pharisee and the tax collector in which the sinner goes home justified **by humility and repentance alone** — no atonement mechanism required. This, Tabor argues, is the religion of Jesus.

The closing movement is the Jamesian recovery. James the brother of Jesus — the actual head of the early movement, "airbrushed out of history" at the Vatican where Peter and Paul stand on the steps and James is missing — preserves the Torah-observant, Hebraic, non-atonement-based stream. The Letter of James in the New Testament has no Christian theology in it whatsoever beyond two passing references to "Jesus Christ"; a Muslim could read it and call it one of the greatest ethical treatises ever written. Martin Luther called it an "epistle of straw" and reportedly printed it at the back of the New Testament in different typeface. The sayings-of-Jesus source (Q) preserves the same stream: Sermon-on-the-Mount ethics, prayer in the closet, mercy not sacrifice. The Didache (late 19th-century discovery) preserves a Eucharist with **no body and blood of Christ** — clear evidence that an early Christian stream existed that hadn't been Paulinized. Tabor closes with the Gospel of Thomas saying: "When you are gone, where shall we go?" Jesus answers: "Go to James the Just, for whom heaven and earth came to be." If that verse were preached from pulpits, the entire shape of Christianity would shift.

## Key Teachings

### The Four Pauls of the New Testament

Tabor's foundational taxonomy. The 13 letters bearing Paul's name resolve into four distinct tiers, and reading the New Testament naively (front to back) blurs them into a single composite "Paul" that doesn't exist:

1. **Primary / Early Paul** — the seven undisputed letters: 1 Thessalonians, Galatians, 1-2 Corinthians (probably six letter fragments stitched into two), Romans, Philippians, Philemon. Written in the 50s CE. These are the authentic voice.
2. **Secondary / Deutero-Paul / Later Paul** — Ephesians, Colossians, 2 Thessalonians. "The crown jewels of Pauline theology" — most pop-Christian theology gets built from these. But they've been overwritten / interpolated. Tabor uses "secondary" as a less inflammatory term than "inauthentic."
3. **The Pastorals** — 1-2 Timothy, Titus. "Buyer beware." Almost certainly pseudonymous. Letters about appointing bishops and deacons, handling widows, marriage rules — institutional church concerns that don't match early Paul.
4. **Legendary / Tendentious Paul** — the Paul of Acts. "Knocked off his horse on the road to Damascus" (a horse Acts never mentions). The same author who wrote Luke wrote Acts, and he loves Paul as a hero — but it's *his* theologically-constructed Paul, not the Paul of the authentic letters. Tabor also calls this "polemical Paul." Acts is "the press release" after the Jerusalem meeting; Galatians is what Paul actually thought.

If you don't sort these out, you'll harmonize all four into a single figure who doesn't exist anywhere in history.

### The Three-Rule Source-Hierarchy Method

Tabor's tight three-step rule for working with Pauline sources:

1. **Never accept Acts over Paul's own account in the seven early letters.** Worked example: Galatians 2 and Acts 15 both describe the Jerusalem meeting. Galatians 2 is Paul's first-person account — heated, defensive, "what they are means nothing to me." Acts 15 is the harmonious press release: Peter and Paul united, James presiding, no friction. Don't go by the press release.
2. **Cautiously consider Acts when it agrees with Paul's letters and shows no obvious bias.** Acts's itinerary information (Ephesus, Corinth, etc.) can be useful — Paul alludes to these locations in the letters.
3. **Treat Acts's independent information as interesting but not interpretively authoritative.** Example: Acts says Paul was born in Tarsus; Jerome says Paul was actually born in Gischala in northern Galilee and exiled to Tarsus as an infant after the post-Herodian revolts of 4 BCE. Paul himself never says Tarsus. Acts also has Paul claiming Roman citizenship — Paul himself never claims it. Either could be true; neither should override Paul's own testimony.

The discipline of this method is that any claim about Paul's actual views must be grounded in Tier 1 — Primary Paul. Everything else is supplementary, corroborating, or testable against Tier 1.

### Read the New Testament Backwards — Start with 1 Thessalonians

Tabor's pedagogical move that "shocks" students raised to read Matthew first. The chronological reading order — not the canonical reading order — is:

- **First (50-52 CE):** 1 Thessalonians — the earliest Christian document on the planet. Not the great theological treatise; that's a good thing, because what it shows you is an apocalyptic movement convinced the end is imminent and Paul will live to see it.
- **Then the rest of the seven letters** in the 50s.
- **Then Mark** (~70 CE), written after Paul has died, with Paul heavily in the author's head.
- **Then Matthew and Luke** using Mark plus Q.
- **Finally John** — the latest, most theologically developed, most Christologically high.
- **Then Acts** — Luke's second volume, written probably 80-90 CE, presenting a harmonized post-hoc reconstruction.

If you read in that order, you watch the movement develop in real time. If you start with Matthew, you read the finished product as if it were the origin point — and Paul becomes a footnote inside the church's later self-understanding rather than the apocalyptic mystic he actually was.

### Paul's Gospel Came by Revelation, Not from the Jerusalem Apostles

The most consequential single claim in Paul's letters. In Galatians 1:11-12: "The gospel I preach is not according to man. I did not receive it from man, nor was I taught it — but by a revelation of Jesus Christ." In 2 Corinthians 12, Paul describes being caught up to the third heaven. In 1 Corinthians 11:23 (the Last Supper passage): "I received from the Lord that which I also delivered to you."

Most scholars softpedal "I received from the Lord" by reading the Greek verb (*paralambano*) as "passed down by tradition" — i.e., Paul got it from James and Peter. Tabor rejects this reading on Paul's own grounds: *Paul never says he got it from them*. He says explicitly the opposite. He claims direct visionary access — "let's have a little talk with Jesus" intimacy — and that this access **supersedes** the testimony of those who walked with the earthly Jesus.

This is the structural problem of Pauline Christianity. The Jerusalem apostles knew Jesus firsthand for years. Paul never met him in the flesh. But Paul claims his visionary access provides a *more authoritative* gospel ("though we once knew Christ after the flesh, we no longer know him that way" — 2 Cor 5:16). This is supersessionism applied not just to Judaism but to the original disciples.

### Why Drinking Blood Is Un-Jewish — and the Last Supper Problem

The single hardest piece of evidence that Pauline Christianity diverges from anything the historical Jesus could plausibly have taught. In Genesis 9, the prohibition on consuming blood is part of the universal Noahide covenant — older than Sinai, binding on all humanity, grounded in the sacredness of life itself. Throughout the Hebrew Bible, blood is life; you cannot consume what you cannot create. Even meat must be properly drained (kosher) before consumption. The Jerusalem council in Acts 15 — chaired by James — explicitly imposes the no-blood rule on Gentile converts (no blood, nothing strangled, no idol-meat, no sexual immorality).

Now read 1 Corinthians 11 and Mark 14: "Take, eat — this is my body. Drink — this is my blood, shed for the remission of sins." Even read symbolically, the framework is alien to anything James, Peter, or a Torah-observant Jesus would endorse. Williams pushes the point in this conversation: if James the head of the Jerusalem community was Torah-observant (Josephus and other independent sources confirm this), the Last Supper as Paul reports it would have been an abomination to him.

Tabor's reconstruction: Paul's Last Supper formula did not come from the Jerusalem apostles. It came from Paul's visionary tradition ("I received from the Lord"), got embedded in 1 Corinthians, then Mark — written after Paul's death and saturated with Pauline theology — back-projected it onto the historical Jesus. The Didache (late 19th-century discovery) preserves a Eucharist tradition that has **no body and blood of Christ at all** — clear evidence that early Christian streams existed without the Pauline ritual.

### Luke's Conspicuous Deletion of Mark 10:45

One of the most interesting interpretive cruxes in the conversation, raised by Williams. Mark 10:45 ("the Son of Man came... to give his life as a ransom for many") is the climactic statement of Mark's atonement theology — Tabor compares it to "an orchestra hitting a crescendo." It's the secret of the entire Gospel of Mark.

Matthew uses Mark and keeps the verse. Luke uses Mark and **deletes it**.

This is the puzzle. Luke is traditionally identified as Paul's companion (the "we" passages in Acts). Mark is heavily Pauline. If Luke shared Pauline atonement theology, he should keep the most Pauline verse in the synoptic tradition. Instead he removes it.

Tabor's resolution: Luke loves Paul as a legendary hero (Acts is "the Paul show") but Luke does not actually share Paul's atonement theology. Luke's own soteriology is best seen in the parable of the Pharisee and the tax collector (Luke 18:9-14), found only in Luke. The tax collector goes home **justified** — Luke uses the exact technical term — not by faith in Jesus's blood, not by atonement, but by humility and repentance. "He who humbles himself will be exalted." That's Luke's gospel. It's the religion of Jesus, not the religion about Jesus.

This is also why the parable matters so much: it's the closest the New Testament gets to preserving a non-Pauline soteriology in the canonical gospels. Ask a Pauline Christian "how was this man justified?" and they default to "faith in Jesus's death and resurrection." But the parable doesn't say that. The parable says humility before God.

### Romans 1-3 — How Paul Builds a New Religion

Tabor's pedagogical walk-through of Paul's master treatise. The structure is:

- **Romans 1** — the wickedness of pagan humanity; God's wrath is just. Any first-century Jew would nod along.
- **Romans 2** — the hypothetical Jewish-friendly chapter. It's not the hearers of the Torah who will be justified but the doers. Gentiles who keep the Torah in their hearts may be ahead of Jews who recite but don't practice. Hillel and the good rabbis would all agree.
- **Romans 3** — the rug-pull. "None is righteous, no not one. All have sinned and fallen short of the glory of God." Universal depravity, established by quoting Psalms and Isaiah. Then: "but now the sacrifice of Jesus Christ and his blood is an atonement for your sins." The blood becomes the only access point to God.

This is "where a new religion is born" — Tabor's phrase. Not because the idea originated in Romans 3 (Paul had been preaching it for years) but because Romans is the treatise version. From here forward, Christianity diverges decisively from the Hebraic tradition. The God of Psalm 51 (David's penitent psalm — "have mercy on me, blot out my transgressions") becomes a God who requires the sacrifice of his firstborn before he will forgive. The Hosea / Amos tradition ("I desire mercy not sacrifice"; "shall I offer my firstborn for the sin of my soul?") is reversed. Where Israel's God forgives through repentance and mercy, Paul's God forgives only through the blood of Christ as mediator.

Tabor stresses: this introduces an **intermediary** between God and humanity for the first time in Abrahamic religion. The Hebrew Bible repeatedly says God is near to all who call on him. Paul says no — you must go through Christ.

### Romans 9-11 — Supersessionism in Authentic Paul

A point Tabor flags as "doesn't sit well ecumenically" with modern theologians. The supersessionism that becomes Christianity is already in **early, authentic** Paul — not just a later misreading.

Romans 9-11 frames Jews who don't accept Christ as branches "broken off" from the olive tree. Paul commends them — they have the covenants, the Torah, the zeal — but they've rejected their Messiah. Gentiles who accept Christ are grafted in. Jews who reject remain broken off, "branches dying on the ground." If they receive Christ, they can be grafted back. The "my way or the highway" theology is not invented at Nicaea or by the Reformers. It's in Paul's own pen.

This matters because well-meaning modern theologians sometimes argue that Paul stayed Torah-observant and pro-Jewish and that Christian supersessionism is a later corruption. Tabor: Paul *is* the source of supersessionism. It's right there in Romans 11.

### James the Just — Airbrushed Out of History

The Vatican test. Walk up the steps of St. Peter's Basilica and look at the colossal statues: Peter on the left, Paul on the right. Where is James? **He's not there.** Ask anyone at the Vatican about James and the answer is "James who?"

But James the brother of Jesus was the head of the Jerusalem movement. Acts itself can't fully hide this — Acts 15 has James presiding over the Jerusalem council, hammering down his decision after everyone else has spoken: "It is my judgment that..." That's not Peter speaking. That's not Paul. That's James, head of "the Nazarene rabbinic community," issuing the binding ruling.

Luke — the same author — works systematically to minimize this. When he copies Mark's list of Jesus's brothers (James, Joseph, Simon, Jude), he leaves out the names — just "the brothers." When Mary appears in Acts 1, "Mary and the brothers" without identification. The reader of Luke-Acts would never know James is Jesus's brother and head of the movement.

The result is that 2,000 years of Christian self-understanding centers on a Peter-Paul united front that James's existence directly contradicts. The Pope claims succession from Peter, not James. The "vicar of Christ" doesn't claim succession from Jesus's actual brother — because if he did, the entire Catholic claim to apostolic succession would have to be relocated to Jerusalem-Nazarene-Ebionite-Jamesian lines that died out under Roman pressure and Constantinian consolidation.

### The Religion *of* Jesus vs. the Religion *about* Jesus

Tabor's framing of the fundamental fork. Most contemporary Christians, even in mainline churches, **don't actually believe Pauline atonement theology** at the level of operational ethics. Walk into a Methodist or Episcopal congregation in America and ask them what Christianity is about, and the answer is usually: love your neighbor, help the poor, treat widows and orphans, walk humbly. That's the Sermon on the Mount and the Letter of James. That's the religion *of* Jesus.

But walk past the evangelical Crusade for Christ booth on the same campus and the question is: "If you died tonight, do you absolutely know you would go to heaven?" That's Romans 3. That's the religion *about* Jesus. That's "all have sinned, justified by the blood, sign the sinner's prayer in the pamphlet."

The first is essentially reformed Judaism — Jesus's actual program. The second is the cosmic-blood-atonement framework Paul built and the church canonized. They're not the same religion.

Williams pushes Tabor here on Matthew 5:17 — "Do not think I have come to abolish the Torah." If Christians took the Sermon on the Mount seriously as Jesus's actual program, they'd be at the local Orthodox synagogue asking to be admitted. Tabor agrees: Jesus did not come to found Christianity. There's no historical evidence for that and virtually no historian holds it. Jesus came to renew Israel from inside. What Paul founded was something else.

### The Three Surviving Witnesses to Pre-Pauline Christianity

Tabor names three sources that preserve the original Jamesian / Jesus-movement stream before Pauline theology overwrote it:

1. **The Letter of James** — read it carefully. Two passing references to "Jesus Christ"; everything else is straight Hebraic ethical instruction. Don't be a respecter of persons. Don't seat the rich man up front and the poor man in the back. Don't claim faith without works. A Muslim, a Jew, or a deist could read it as their own sacred text. Luther called it an "epistle of straw" and reportedly printed it at the back of his New Testament in smaller typeface — because it lacks the Pauline themes of salvation through blood and Christ's death.
2. **Q (the sayings of Jesus shared by Matthew and Luke but not in Mark)** — Tabor sidesteps the technical debates about whether "Q" is the right term. The point: there's a body of Jesus sayings that is *not* in Mark. Mark is heavily Pauline. So this material represents a pre-Pauline Jesus stream. And what's in it? The Sermon on the Mount. Pray in your closet. Give alms in secret. Bless those who persecute you. That's the religion of Jesus.
3. **The Didache** — the "Teaching of the Twelve Apostles," lost for centuries, rediscovered in 1873. It has a chapter on the Eucharist with **no body and blood of Christ**. Just thanksgiving over bread and cup. Clear evidence that a stream of early Christianity existed which had not been Paulinized — and which preserved a Eucharistic practice consistent with Jewish law.

The Gospel of Thomas can be added as a fourth — particularly the saying "Where shall we go when you are gone?" / "Go to James the Just, for whom heaven and earth came to be." (The Lamed Vavnik tradition — the world is preserved by the merit of the righteous few.)

### What If the Movement Had Stayed Jamesian?

A thought experiment Tabor and Williams build out at the end of the conversation. If the Jesus movement had continued on the Jamesian course rather than the Pauline one — what would it look like?

- A stream of Judaism with messianic intensity but Torah-observant
- Open to Gentiles (Jesus and John the Baptist both engage Roman centurions and soldiers without converting them to Judaism)
- Centered on the Sermon on the Mount: humility, mercy, justice, repentance, prayer, alms
- Possibly more expansive in welcoming non-Jews than Paul realized, but without requiring a new theology of atonement
- Recognizing Jesus as great prophet and Messiah — perhaps a figure resembling later Islamic conceptions of Jesus
- Would have had to grapple with the failure of the parousia, but the apocalyptic urgency (the judge stands at the door — James 5:9) was less totalizing than Paul's "the form of this world is passing away"

This is the road not taken. The Ebionites — "the poor ones," James's actual followers — survived for centuries as a small minority, dismissed pejoratively by church fathers like Epiphanius for having "poor opinions of Christ" (not making him God, not making him a blood savior). The "poor ones" name connects to Jesus's own "Blessed are the poor" and Isaiah 66's "to this one I will look — to him who is poor and contrite of heart and trembles at my word."

## Misconceptions Addressed

| Misconception | Tabor's Response |

| "All 13 New Testament letters are equally Paul's" | Only seven are undisputed (1 Thessalonians, Galatians, 1-2 Corinthians, Romans, Philippians, Philemon).
