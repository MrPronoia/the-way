## Summary

This is Tabor's sweeping overview of the first forty years of the Jesus movement — the four decades between John the Baptizer's appearance in the Jordan Valley and the destruction of the Temple in 70 CE — read through the lens of his book *Paul and Jesus*. Tabor opens by reading the preface of that book aloud: he has spent his career investigating **the silence between two clauses of the Apostles' Creed**, the jump from "born of the Virgin Mary" to "crucified, died, and buried." The earliest Christian creed skips Jesus's life entirely. Tabor argues that this birth-and-death frame is essentially Paul's creation, and that the actual first forty years of the movement looked nothing like the religion built later on Paul's name.

The center of the lecture is a five-claim thesis Tabor lays out near the top and unpacks across the hour. (1) **Paul never met Jesus** — his sole claim to authority is visionary, what we'd today call clairvoyant. (2) If you define Christianity by the Nicene Creed, **Paul is its founder, not Jesus** — because Jesus himself preached repentance and humility before God, not creedal confession of his own death. (3) **You should read the New Testament backwards.** First Thessalonians is the earliest document; by the time you get to the gospels they're already heavily Paul-influenced. The original Jesus material lives in Q (the sayings shared by Matthew and Luke against Mark) and in the Letter of James. (4) Q and James are **non-Christological in the Pauline sense** — no cross, no atonement, no blood, just ethical prophetic Judaism in the line of Amos, Micah, and Hosea. (5) **Paul is radically apocalyptic** — every social instruction he gives is calibrated to a midnight he expects within his own lifetime.

From these claims Tabor reconstructs the actual relationships. James the Just is in charge of the Jerusalem movement, not Peter — even the book of Acts has to admit this (Acts 15 is James standing up and saying "this is what we're going to do"). Paul arrives late, claims direct revelation from a heavenly Jesus, and tells the "so-called pillars of the church" their flesh-knowledge of Jesus means nothing to him. The Letter of James, whether autograph or circle-of-James, can have the name Jesus removed from its two mentions and read perfectly — try that with any letter of Paul. Paul and James are not just emphasizing different things. They are running on different operating systems.

The lecture closes with Tabor defending the most controversial chapter of *Paul and Jesus* — what he calls **The Battle of the Apostles**. Tabor stands with F.C. Baur (the 19th-century Tübingen scholar) in arguing Paul actually broke with the Jerusalem leadership toward the end of his life. Most modern scholars dismiss Baur as long-since refuted, but Tabor read Baur for himself and was convinced. Paul's final letters describe abandonment ("no one stood with me"), and 2 Corinthians' polemic against "super-apostles" who claim to be "Hebrews" and "ministers of Christ" Tabor reads as targeting the Jerusalem establishment itself. The first forty years end with the movement already fragmented — and 70 CE's destruction of the Temple cleared the field for Paul's letters, not James's tradition, to become the foundation of the religion that called itself Christianity.

## Key Teachings

### The Silence Between Two Clauses of the Apostles' Creed

Tabor's framing device for the entire lecture is the preface to his book *Paul and Jesus*. The Apostles' Creed jumps from "born of the Virgin Mary" directly to "crucified, dead, and buried, and on the third day rose again." It skips Jesus's life. **Christmas and Easter** become the religion. The thirty years between — the teachings, the sermons, the kingdom proclamation, the prophetic ethics — are silent.

Tabor's case: this birth-and-death framing is essentially Paul. The Letter of James never mentions Jesus's birth, never mentions blood on a cross, never mentions atonement. Q never mentions them either. The gospels narrate them but their **core teaching content** is ethical, not creedal. Christianity as a religion of "believe the death and resurrection" is Paul's invention, not the movement's original shape.

### Paul Never Met Jesus — His Authority Is Visionary, Not Witness

Claim #1 of the book. Paul never sat across from Jesus, never broke bread with him, never walked the Galilee with him. He might have glimpsed him from a distance during the crucifixion if he was in Jerusalem — that's the most we can say. **His sole claim to authority is clairvoyant apparition.** He hears Jesus's voice, gets revelations, ascends to paradise.

Tabor puts the consequence sharply: "Paul never met Jesus, so how do we know how to credit that visionary experience? Whereas James or Peter would say I spent countless hours with Jesus face to face, knowing him, touching him." Paul himself acknowledges this gap in 1 Corinthians 9 and Galatians 1, where he leans hard on "I got my message straight from Jesus — not from man, not from any apostle." If even an angel from heaven preaches a different gospel, Paul says, **anathema** — let them be cursed, what we'd today render "go to hell with that."

People who like Paul's visionary authority love the book. People who distrust it also love the book. Tabor isn't trying to settle whether the visions were real. He's just trying to **get Paul right**.

### Read the New Testament Backwards

Tabor's third claim: most people read Matthew first because it's first in the canon, and never notice that the gospels are already saturated with Paul's theology by the time they're written. **First Thessalonians is the earliest document we have.** Paul's letters precede every gospel by decades.

If you want to study what the Jesus movement actually was, you have to read backwards. Start with Paul (50s CE), then Mark (~70 CE), then the Q sayings that Matthew and Luke share against Mark, then the Letter of James (whose dating is contested but whose **content** is pre-Pauline in flavor), then the Didache (Greek monastery discovery, 19th century, contains sayings of Jesus with the same ring as Q). Only then go to Matthew and Luke and finally John — knowing that by John you are in heavily Pauline-influenced territory.

The "Christianity" most readers think they're finding in the gospels is in many places **as much Paul as it is Jesus**.

### Q and James Are Pre-Pauline Prophetic Ethics

The center of gravity of Jesus's actual teaching, Tabor argues, lives in two non-Pauline streams:

- **Q** — the ~250 sayings of Jesus that appear in Matthew and Luke but not Mark. (Don't like the name Q? Fine, don't call it anything. The set of sayings exists.) Core content: love your enemies, turn the other cheek, don't pray to be seen, the Sermon on the Mount material. **Not the heart of Paul's gospel.**
- **The Letter of James** — whether or not it's an autograph from James the brother of Jesus, its content reads like the Hebrew prophets. "What does the Lord require of you but to do justice, love kindness, walk humbly before God" (Micah 6:8). Pay your workers a fair wage. Care for widows and orphans. Don't be partial to the rich.

Tabor's bombshell test: **take the word "Jesus" out of the two places it appears in the Letter of James, and the letter reads perfectly well without him.** James 1:1 and James 2:1 are the only Jesus references in the entire letter. There's no cross, no blood, no resurrection apologetic, no incarnation. It's prophetic Judaism. Now try removing Jesus from any Paul letter — they collapse into nothing. **I rest my case**, Tabor says.

This is not because James didn't know his own brother. It's because the Jesus James is honoring is the **ethical teacher** in the line of Amos and Micah, not the cosmic Christ whose blood atones for sin.

### James Is Running the Movement — Even Acts Admits It

A scholarly question Tabor presses through the lecture: **who is in charge in Jerusalem?** The book of Acts is written by an author sympathetic to Paul (traditionally called Luke, but the work is anonymous), and yet the author cannot avoid the embarrassing fact: James, not Peter, is the decisive voice.

The clincher is **Acts 15**. The biggest crisis in the movement's history — whether to accept Gentiles without circumcision — and how does it resolve? Peter stands and speaks. Paul stands and speaks. **No vote is taken.** James stands up and says: "this is what we're going to do." It's a ruling, not a debate. James is the chairman of the board.

Tabor notes the writer's discomfort. Acts 1:14 mentions "Mary and Jesus's brothers" without naming them, even though Mark 6:3 names them in order (James, Joseph, Simon, Jude). Acts 15 introduces James without calling him Jesus's brother. Acts 21 has Paul arriving in Jerusalem and going directly to James — again, no "the brother of Jesus." Why? Because by the time Luke is writing, the perpetual-virginity-of-Mary theology is already pushing the brothers out of view. But the author cannot delete the fact that James is in charge.

### The Acts 21 Episode — James Demands Paul Prove He Keeps Torah

Acts 21 is the most revealing moment in the entire book. Paul comes up to Jerusalem; James greets him with: "We've heard rumors that you're not keeping Torah even though you're Jewish." James demands Paul **demonstrate publicly** that he still observes the Law — pay for some Nazirite-vow men to complete their sacrifices in the Temple so people can see Paul in the Temple supporting Torah-keeping Jews.

Tabor reads this as historically plausible. James and the Jerusalem community are still Torah-observant Messianic Jews. They've granted Gentiles a pass on the "yoke of the Torah" (matching the rabbinic principle that **the righteous of all nations have a place in the world to come**), but Jewish followers of Jesus are still expected to keep Torah.

Paul goes along with it. Why? Tabor refuses the word "hypocrite." Read 1 Corinthians 9: "To the Jew I became as a Jew... to those under the law I became as one under the law... to those without the law I became as one without the law." Tabor's take: Paul is **situational**. He believes the whole order is passing away in five minutes anyway, so don't rock the boat. Don't offend Jews, don't offend Gentiles, don't offend the Church of God. It's strategy, not duplicity. But it absolutely isn't what James would do.

### Paul Has Hellenistic Cosmology — the Romans Are Pawns

Berman asks why Paul never names the Romans as Jesus's killers — always blaming "the rulers of this age" or Satan or, in 1 Thessalonians 2, the Jews. Tabor's answer: Paul's worldview is Hellenistic-cosmological. The Mediterranean cosmos of Paul's day has **seven planetary spheres** populated by principalities and powers — archons, demonic forces — and the lowest planetary realm is the material world ruled by what Paul calls "the god of this world" (2 Corinthians 4:4).

In Paul's frame, Pilate and the Romans are just **pawns in a cosmic game**. The "rulers of this age" (1 Corinthians 2:8) who crucified the Lord of Glory aren't really the Romans — they're the spiritual forces behind the Romans. Every event Paul attributes to these powers: the "thorn in the flesh" is a "messenger of Satan"; his travel plans get blocked because "Satan hindered me"; he tells the Corinthians to "deliver" the incestuous man "to Satan."

This isn't a Roman-collaborator Paul (against the mythicist reading). It's a Paul whose categories are so saturated with cosmic dualism that **human agents barely register**. Romans 16:20: "The God of peace will shortly crush Satan under your feet." That's who Paul thinks killed Jesus.

### Paul's Apocalyptic Clock — Five Minutes to Midnight

The single most underrated key to reading Paul: **it's 11:55 PM and midnight is the parousia.** Paul's letters can't be understood unless you keep this clock running. 1 Corinthians 7:29 — "the appointed time has grown very short." 1 Corinthians 7:31 — "the form (schema) of this world is passing away." Paul thinks he will live to see it. He's probably 50 years old when writing.

This drives **every social ethic in Paul**:

- Don't bother getting married — there won't even be male and female much longer
- If you're a slave, just be a slave — there won't be slavery much longer
- Don't worry about business or property — the form of this world is passing
- Live as if you have nothing, rejoice as if not rejoicing, mourn as if not mourning

So when modern readers ask "did Paul condone slavery?" — Tabor: "Well, yeah, he tells slaves if you're a slave just be a slave. But if it's five minutes till midnight, yeah, I can be a slave." Paul is delivering **interim ethics** for a five-minute window, not a 2000-year religion. Extending Paul's marriage and gender instructions across 2000 years is, in Tabor's framing, a category mistake.

### The Christ Faction in 1 Corinthians 1 — The Original Jesus People?

A subtle observation Tabor draws out. 1 Corinthians 1:12 has Paul listing factions in Corinth: "I follow Paul, I follow Apollos, I follow Cephas, I follow Christ." Tabor notes how strange the last one is. Aren't they all supposed to follow Christ?

Two readings on the table:

1. **The James faction** — these are people loyal to the original physical Jesus, the Jesus James knew, the prophetic ethical Jesus, as opposed to Paul's heavenly visionary Jesus.
2. **The hyper-charismatics** — these are people who claim direct ecstatic revelation from Christ ("I follow Christ because I talked to him this morning"), maybe the most arrogant of the bunch.

Tabor finds both plausible. The first reading fits the broader pattern: in the first 40 years there's already a real division between the **physical-Jesus tradition** (preserved by James, Peter, the family, the Q sayings, the Letter of James) and the **visionary-Jesus tradition** (Paul). Both think they're following Jesus. They don't agree on much else.

### The Battle of the Apostles — Standing with F.C. Baur

The most controversial chapter of *Paul and Jesus*, and Tabor leans into it at the close. **Ferdinand Christian Baur** (1792-1860, Tübingen school) argued that Paul and James/Jerusalem broke definitively in Paul's lifetime — that the New Testament cannot be read as a harmonious early church but as a record of an unresolved conflict. The standard modern position is that Baur was refuted long ago and Paul's opponents in 2 Corinthians are some sort of gnosticizing Judaizers, not the Jerusalem church itself.

Tabor: "I made the mistake of actually reading FC Baur and being convinced by his arguments — which I guarantee you none of these people have done." Tabor reads 2 Corinthians 11-12 (the "super-apostles" passage, "are they Hebrews? so am I; are they ministers of Christ? so am I, and I am a better one") as Paul going **directly after the Jerusalem establishment**. And Paul's later letters speak of abandonment: in Philippians and 2 Timothy he says nobody stood with him, that he was alone, that people deserted him.

The picture Tabor paints: by the end of Paul's life, the visionary apostle who never met Jesus has parted ways with the brother of Jesus who actually ran the movement. **Acts whitewashes this**. Paul's own letters preserve it if you read carefully.

### What Changes at 70 CE — Why Paul Wins the Library

The lecture ends implicit but the logic is plain. The first forty years had two centers: the **Jerusalem community under James** (Torah-observant Messianic Jews, the family, Peter, John, the brothers, the Q tradition, the Letter of James) and **Paul's Gentile mission** (Hellenistic cities, visionary authority, cosmic-cross theology).

In 62 CE, James is killed (Josephus, *Antiquities* 20.9.1 — high priest Ananus has him stoned). In 64 CE, Paul is somewhere in the West (possibly Spain per 1 Clement; possibly already dead). In 70 CE, the Temple is destroyed and the Jerusalem community is shattered. **The James tradition loses its institutional home.** The Pauline congregations in Greece, Asia Minor, and Italy survive, and Paul's letters survive with them.

When the gospels are composed (Mark ~70, Matthew/Luke 80-90, John 90-110), they are composed in a world where the Pauline framework is already ascendant. The original 40-year movement under John, Jesus, Peter, James becomes the **back half of the silence between virgin birth and resurrection**. By the time the Apostles' Creed is composed in the second century, the silence is permanent.
