## Summary

Dr. Tabor narrates a slide-illustrated reflection on a Mediterranean cruise he led for the Biblical Archaeology Society, retracing — not Paul's "missionary journeys" but, in his preferred framing, **Paul's trips**. The keyword for the episode is **the seafaring Paul**: Paul as a Roman-era traveler being moved by ship across the same waters Tabor has now sat above for forty years studying. The cruise hit Naples and Pompeii, Athens, Corinth, Ephesus, Crete, Cyprus, Rhodes, Santorini, and Rome. Tabor walks the audience through what he saw, what he felt, and what new historical pieces clicked into place at each location. The most striking feeling is geographic — Paul reduced from a doctrinal abstraction to a man crossing scary winter sea-lanes in a grain ship under loose Roman guard, headed for a Caesar he may or may not have ever reached.

The historical thesis tucked inside the travelogue: **Paul almost certainly did not die in Rome at the end of the Acts narrative.** Tabor reads First Clement (c. 96 CE) as preserving an earlier tradition that Paul was released and traveled to "the farthest points west" — meaning Spain, which Paul himself names as his goal in Romans 15. The two-year house arrest of Acts 28 was lenient, the procurators in Caesarea had treated him as a movement leader worth keeping in conversation (and possibly worth a bribe), and the Acts account simply stops without reporting a death. Tabor will develop this further in his Paul course. The Pompeii section delivers a separate hypothesis: he proposes on camera in Simcha Jacobovici's *Pompeii* film that **Revelation 18's "Babylon falls in one hour" lament reflects the eruption of Vesuvius in 79 CE** — the cargo list (gold, silver, jewels, fine linen, purple, silk, cinnamon, incense, wine, oil, wheat, cattle, slaves) matches what archaeologists pulled from the ash, and the "ships in the bay weeping at the smoke of her burning" matches what Pliny the Younger actually saw from his boat in the bay of Naples that August.

The longest and most personal sections are Athens and Corinth. Tabor sits on the bedrock steps of the Acropolis, takes off his shoes, places his bare feet on stones Zeno, Socrates, Plato, and Diogenes walked, and meditates on **"Athens and Jerusalem" as the two currents of Western civilization**. He stands in front of the Corinth *bēma* — the actual judgment seat where Gallio dismissed the case against Paul — and feels Paul's First Corinthians come alive ("don't take each other to Roman courts" turns out to be a survivor talking, not abstract pastoral advice). At Ephesus he encounters a city better preserved than Pompeii (without the volcano — just centuries of slope erosion burying its terraces), reads the great theater of Acts 19's mob scene, and sees the famous many-breasted Artemis statues reinterpreted in current scholarship as covered in **queen bees** rather than breasts or testicles — fertility iconography drawn from the beehive's life-giving queen. The episode ends with an invitation to next year's smaller cruise and Tabor's recurring confession: he had been to all these sites before, but walking Paul's actual geography by sea, with weeks at a time on a moving ship, changed him in a way that flying never had.

## Key Teachings

### Paul Probably Did Not Die in Rome — First Clement Says He Went West to Spain

Tabor's emerging historical position, signaled here and promised in more detail in his Paul course: **the Acts narrative stops because Luke ran out of source material, not because Paul died.** The two-year house arrest of Acts 28 was deliberately loose — friends came and went, Paul carried on his work, and the procurators in Caesarea (Felix and Festus) had treated him as a movement leader worth talking to, not a dungeon prisoner. (The "Paul's dungeon" tourist site in modern Caesarea, Tabor flatly states, is a recently discovered sewer with chains in it — not where Paul was kept.)

First Clement, written from Rome around 96 CE, is our earliest external witness to Paul's fate. Clement says Paul "appeared" (came) to Rome but was then "released" and traveled to "the farthest points west." Paul himself, in Romans 15, names Spain as his intended destination. Tabor's reading: Paul made it. He was released, headed west to finish the work, and the death-in-Rome tradition is later martyrology layered on top of an Acts ending that simply isn't a death scene.

This matters because the standard Christian narrative compresses Paul's life into a martyrdom story that fits the church's interest in apostolic suffering. Take the martyrdom away and Paul looks more like what he was: a Roman citizen carrying on an itinerant mission across the empire, under loose supervision, who eventually did finish his run somewhere — but probably not where tradition says, and probably not when tradition says.

### The Grain Ship to Rome — Paul Sailed in the Forbidden Season

The voyage in Acts 27-28 reads stranger when you know the sea-lane economics. Sailing the Mediterranean after roughly mid-September was forbidden by Roman practice — too dangerous. But Nero had issued a **double-bounty edict** for any ship captain willing to bring Alexandrian grain into Rome through the winter, because Rome's winter grain shortage was politically dangerous. Independent captains took the gamble: make it to harbor and you collect double or triple. The ship Paul boarded near Rhodes was one of these grain ships — late-season, captained by someone betting against the weather for the payout.

Acts confirms the timing: Paul has been in Jerusalem for Pentecost, then "the fast" (Yom Kippur) is mentioned as already past. Yom Kippur ranges from late September to October depending on the year. Whatever year Paul sailed — Tabor surveys 58, 59, 60 as candidates — he was on the water in the exact window when no rational captain would sail without Nero's premium on offer. The shipwreck at Malta and the long detour south of Crete fit the season exactly.

### Revelation 18's "Babylon Falls in One Hour" May Refer to Vesuvius (79 CE)

Tabor's flash idea on the third day of filming Simcha Jacobovici's *Pompeii* documentary. He withheld it until the cameras were rolling. Revelation 18 is poetic — many modern translations set it in meter because it's clearly a hymn-fragment inserted into the apocalypse, possibly from a pre-Christian source. The standard reading takes "Babylon" as Rome (a code Christians used elsewhere). Tabor wants to refine this: Rome did *not* fall in one hour, ever. But on August 24, 79 CE, **Pompeii and Herculaneum did**.

The cargo list in Revelation 18 maps onto the Pompeian archaeological record with disturbing precision:

> "cargo of gold, silver, jewels and pearls, fine linen, purple, silk and scarlet, all kinds of scented wood, articles of ivory, costly wood, bronze, iron, marble, cinnamon, spice, incense, myrrh, frankincense, wine, oil, fine flour, wheat, cattle, sheep, horses, chariots, slaves — that is, human souls."

The Pompeii museum displays exactly these categories. And: "the merchants of the earth weep and mourn for her since no one buys their cargo anymore" — Pompeii was a primary cargo port serving Naples. "The shipmasters stood far off and cried out as they saw the smoke of her burning" — that is Pliny the Younger, on a boat in the Bay of Naples on August 24, 79, watching exactly that. Pliny the Elder, his uncle, was killed on the shore in the same eruption.

For first-century followers of Jesus, Vesuvius would have landed as **the first judgment falling on Rome** — the opening of the apocalyptic sequence they'd been waiting for. Nero (already considered the final evil ruler of Daniel/Mark 13/2 Thessalonians) had died only eleven years prior. The temple in Jerusalem had fallen nine years prior. Now the heart of Roman opulence was burning. Many would have believed Jesus's return in the clouds was weeks away.

### Pompeii's Opulence Was Built on Jewish Slavery After 70 CE

A historical detail that lands harder once you're standing in the brothel district. The Arch of Titus (celebrating Rome's conquest of Jerusalem in 70 CE) was already built when Vesuvius erupted nine years later. The Colosseum had been built with Jewish slaves and Jewish bounty. Pompeii's prostitution houses — clearly marked from the street with phallic Chevron carvings pointing the way for male visitors — were likely staffed in part by Jewish women captured in the 70 CE war.

Tabor lingers here without sensationalizing: "pious Jewish women that even cover themselves in the view of men are now forced into this kind of slavery." It's a piece of the Roman context that rarely gets named — what the wealth of late-first-century Roman cities was actually made of, and where the bodies in the stone-bedded brothel cells came from.

### Athens and Jerusalem — The Two Currents Tabor Has Spent His Life Tracing

The Athens section is more personal than historical. Tabor climbs to the Acropolis, finds the original Bronze-Age bedrock steps (set on stone that hasn't moved since Plato), sits down, takes off his shoes, and puts his bare feet on the stone. "Zeno the founder of Stoicism — *stoa* means the porch. Whether he taught right at the Acropolis or not — how many times did Socrates pass this? How many times did Plato, Diogenes, Lucian, all the people that we study?"

The frame he uses is the classical formulation: **Athens and Jerusalem** as the two currents of Western civilization. Athens carries the philosophical, scientific, rational stream. Jerusalem carries the prophetic, ethical-monotheist stream. Add Christianity and you have the engine of world Christendom. Add the Enlightenment and the scientific age — what Tabor here names as the modern epochal turning point, the discovery of **zeros and ones (digital coding) and the structure of DNA** — and you have the modernity we're all currently living inside. He has spent forty-five years studying the cross-currents of these two cities; sitting on the bedrock made it land in his body.

The point isn't antiquarian. It's that the New Testament is written *into* this Athens-Jerusalem dialectic. Paul on Mars Hill (Acts 17) is the most famous staging of it — a Jewish apostle quoting Greek poets to Stoic and Epicurean philosophers about the unknown god. Tabor walks the wooden-and-metal steps that now cover the original bedrock of the Areopagus and pictures the scene.

### The Corinth Bēma — Paul Stood Right Here, and First Corinthians Now Reads Differently

The site that moved Tabor most. The *bēma* at Corinth is the judgment seat where Gallio (proconsul of Achaia, brother of Seneca) dismissed the case the Corinthian Jewish community brought against Paul. Acts 18 reports Gallio's famous brush-off: "If it were a matter of crime or wrongdoing I would hear you, but since it is a matter of words and names and your own law, see to it yourselves. I refuse to be a judge of these things."

Standing in front of the bēma, Tabor connects this back to **First Corinthians**: Paul telling the Corinthians, "Why do you take each other to court? Don't you know the saints will judge angels?" That reads as abstract pastoral advice until you realize Paul is writing from personal experience. He had been dragged before Gallio at this exact spot. He knew what it was to stand below the judgment seat in front of a Roman magistrate who didn't care about your theology. "Don't do this to each other" turns out to be a survivor's counsel, not a moral aphorism.

The same bēma platform pattern recurs in Jerusalem at the *Gabbatha* pavement — the platform where Pilate sentenced Jesus, recently rediscovered against the Western city wall (not the Wailing Wall). Shimon Gibson was present at the discovery as a teenager working with Magen Broshi. On Tabor's 1-to-5 scale of historical confidence, the Jerusalem Gabbatha is "1 to 3" — very high. Today, ignorant Jerusalemites hold rock concerts on it.

### The Corinth Synagogue Inscription — "Synagogue of the Hebrews"

The Corinth museum now displays a stone lintel reading **"Synagogue of the Hebrews"** — the inscription that hung above the door of the very synagogue Acts 18 says Paul taught in for the first three weeks of his eighteen-month stay in Corinth. Tabor had seen photos but had never seen the stone itself before this trip.

What unfolds at the synagogue is the same pattern Paul ran in Athens at Mars Hill and that gets him in trouble city by city across the empire. As a visiting rabbi from Jerusalem — possibly trained under Gamaliel — he gets a respectful three-week hearing. Then he reaches the apocalyptic-messianic claim: a sky-man is coming, he is the son of David per Isaiah 11, judgment is imminent. The community has people who already believe roughly that. *Then* Paul names Jesus of Nazareth, crucified twenty years ago by Rome, as the figure — and they kick him out.

The museum also holds first-century menorah carvings showing the variations in diaspora menorah design (synagogues like Beth Alpha and Sephoris would have similar floor-mosaic menorahs surviving from the third and fourth century).

### Ephesus — Better Preserved Than Pompeii, Without the Volcano

Tabor's biggest takeaway is the **archaeological revelation of Ephesus** as Turkey has now developed it. He calls it the most fabulous archaeological site in the Mediterranean — better preserved than Pompeii because the city sat in a valley and centuries of soil washed down and buried the upper terraces. Excavators have peeled back one slope and found private homes with **marble pillars, fountains, frescos on every wall, intact opulence**. The whole site sat under the soil, no eruption required.

Acts 19 says Paul spent two years here (and longer, by other readings). He was writing First Corinthians from Ephesus. The great theater — where the riot of Acts 19 happens — is the site of the silver-shrine controversy: Demetrius and the silversmiths fear Paul's preaching is hurting the trade in silver Artemis statuettes. The mob runs Paul's companions into the theater, and (in Acts's framing) the Roman authorities calm the "ekklēsia" — Tabor flags the wordplay: the Greek word the New Testament uses for "church" is the same word Acts uses for the rioting civic assembly. Same word, two functions.

### The Artemis of Ephesus — Queen Bees, Not Breasts (Current Scholarship)

The many-breasted Artemis statues from Ephesus have long been the canonical image of pagan fertility religion. Older scholarship: those are breasts. More recent scholarship (mentioned in Tabor's tour guide's lecture): they're **bulls' testicles**. The latest reading: **they're queen bees**. The statue is covered with bee imagery — bees carved into the dress, bees in the iconography. The queen bee symbolizes fertility because she alone fertilizes the entire colony and the colony exists to serve her.

The interpretive frame matters: the goddess of Ephesus is the same fruitfulness figure who appears in Egypt as Isis, in Mesopotamia as Inanna, in Greece as Aphrodite. The "great Diana of the Ephesians" the rioters defended in Acts 19 was the local manifestation of a Mediterranean-wide fertility-mother figure — not a generic idol but the patron deity of an entire urban economy whose silversmiths, sex-trade operators, temple servants, and pilgrim hostels all ran on Artemis revenue. Paul's preaching threatened the **economic base** of the city, which is why the riot was a riot and not a theological debate.
