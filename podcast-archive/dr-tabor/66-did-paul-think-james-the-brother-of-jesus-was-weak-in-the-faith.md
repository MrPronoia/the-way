## Summary

Dr. Tabor poses a question he says he's never quite framed this way before: when Paul writes about "the man who is weak in the faith" in Romans 14, is he aiming a quiet shot at **James the brother of Jesus** and the rest of the Jerusalem community? The setup matters. James was the actual leader of the Jesus movement after the crucifixion — Peter and John were his right and left hand, according to Paul's own admission in Galatians 1. But James has been almost completely erased from Christian memory. Walk up the steps of the Vatican today and you see Jesus and the twelve apostles at the top, Peter and Paul on either flank. The brother of Jesus, who actually ran the movement, is nowhere. Tabor's students, when he brings James up, either don't know Jesus had brothers or confuse him with one of the other New Testament Jameses.

Romans 14:1 opens: "As for the man who is weak in faith, welcome him, but not for disputes about opinions. One believes he may eat anything, while the weak man eats only vegetables." Paul then tells the strong not to despise the abstainer and the abstainer not to judge the eater. On the surface this looks like generous accommodation across diverse practices. But the framing — **"the man who is weak in faith"** — defines the vegetarian as weak. And every source we have on James says he was a vegetarian. Hegesippus, preserved in Eusebius, presents James's vegetarianism as a high spiritual practice, a return to Eden where only green herbs and tree fruit were given for food. John the Baptist was almost certainly vegetarian too (Tabor argues the Greek "locusts" is a confusion with a word for honey-cake, supported by the Slavonic Josephus). Tabor thinks Jesus himself was likely vegetarian. So when Paul calls vegetarians "weak in faith" — and adds that the strong can drink wine while the weak abstain (James didn't drink wine; John the Baptist was a Nazirite who didn't drink wine) — he is, by definition, locating James and the entire Jerusalem stream on the weak side of the line. Pairs directly with playlist #11, where Tabor lays out the broader battle of the apostles.

## Key Teachings

### James the Brother of Jesus Was the Actual Leader — and Was Erased

Tabor frames the discussion by reminding viewers who James actually was. The succession runs **John the Baptist → Jesus → James**, with Peter and John as James's lieutenants (Galatians 1). James was "the pillar." Today the Vatican shows Jesus, the twelve apostles, Peter, and Paul — James is missing. The brother who actually led the movement has been deleted from the public memory. Most of Tabor's students don't even know Jesus had brothers, or they confuse this James with James the fisherman or one of the other New Testament Jameses.

### Romans 14:1 Defines the Vegetarian as Weak in Faith

The crucial verse, RSV:

> "As for the man who is weak in faith, welcome him, but not for disputes about opinions. One believes he may eat anything, while the weak man eats only vegetables."

Paul then instructs the strong not to despise the abstainer and the abstainer not to judge the eater. Surface read: generous accommodation. Structural read: **the opening clause has already defined vegetarianism as the weak position**. The category is set before the accommodation begins.

Paul extends the same framework to drinking wine and observing holy days. The "strong" can eat meat, drink wine, treat all days as alike. The "weak" abstain from meat, abstain from wine, observe specific days.

### Hegesippus on James: Vegetarian as Spiritual Practice

Tabor's best ancient source is **Hegesippus**, preserved in Eusebius — a Church Father close to the Holy Land, "one of our best sources we have for Judeo-Christianity." Hegesippus records that James was a vegetarian and presents it not as weakness but as a high spiritual practice — a return to the Eden diet, where Genesis 1 gives only the green herb and the fruit of the tree for food, and only after the flood is blood permitted. Some call this the "Daniel diet" (Daniel 1) — those who want to draw very close to God eat only vegetables.

Josephus also reports on James, particularly his death, but Hegesippus is the source for the dietary practice.

### Vegetarianism Across the Jesus-Movement Lineage

Tabor's read of the broader pattern:

- **John the Baptist** — Almost certainly vegetarian. The Greek "locusts" (akris) is likely a confusion with another word meaning "honey-cake" (egkris) — better fit with the diet, and supported by the Slavonic Josephus, an alternative to the standard Greek Josephus.
- **Jesus** — Tabor thinks vegetarian (he has a separate video on "Was Jesus a Vegetarian"). The fish question gets handled there; in this short he just asks listeners to stay with James for now.
- **James** — Vegetarian per Hegesippus, also abstained from wine.
- **John the Baptist (again)** — A **Nazirite** (the Hebrew separated-one vow, *not* "Nazarene"), which includes abstaining from wine.

The pattern: the John-Jesus-James lineage Tabor consistently identifies as the original movement is also the lineage that practiced the vegetarian/Edenic diet and abstained from wine. The exact practices Paul codes as "weak."

### Was Daniel Weak in Faith? Was Adam?

Tabor's reductio: if vegetarianism is weakness, then **all of humanity before the flood**, who according to Genesis ate only vegetables, was weak in faith. **Daniel** — who ate only vegetables specifically to draw close to God — was weak in faith. The prophetic vision of the messianic kingdom in **Isaiah 11** ("they shall not hurt or destroy in all my holy mountain"; the wolf lies with the lamb, the bear with the cow) is a vision of the world *returning* to that vegetarian Eden state. By Paul's classification, the kingdom of God is populated by the weak.

### The Implicit Critique: Paul Telling Jerusalem Not to Condemn Him

Paul closes the Romans 14 passage by telling the "strong" not to condemn the "weak." Read the other direction: he's also telling the weak not to condemn the strong. If the weak include James and the Jerusalem community — practicing the Edenic diet, abstaining from wine, holding to specific holy days — then Paul is implicitly saying: *don't pass judgment on those of us who eat what we like.* Given Acts 21, where James confronts Paul with rumors that he's been telling Jews to abandon Torah practice, the Romans 14 framing reads as Paul's pre-emptive theological defense — wrapped in the language of accommodation but with the categories already loaded against the Jerusalem side.

## Misconceptions Addressed

| Misconception | Tabor's Response |
|---------------|------------------|
| **"Romans 14 is just Paul being generous and accommodating"** | The opening clause locks in the category before the accommodation begins. Paul *defines* the vegetarian as "weak in faith." The accommodation is real but it sits on top of a settled hierarchy. |
| **"James was a minor figure — Peter and Paul were the main apostles"** | James was the actual leader of the post-crucifixion movement. Peter and John were his lieutenants. The historical record has been visually and culturally edited (e.g., the Vatican). |
| **"Vegetarianism in the early Jesus movement was a fringe practice"** | All the best ancient sources point to John the Baptist, James, and probably Jesus practicing some form of the Edenic / Nazirite / vegetarian discipline. The fringe practice was Paul's eat-anything position relative to the original lineage. |
| **"Locusts means insect locusts — John the Baptist ate bugs"** | Likely a Greek-word confusion between *akris* (locust) and *egkris* (honey-cake). The Slavonic Josephus supports the honey-cake reading. |
| **"Nazarene and Nazirite are the same thing"** | They are not. *Nazirite* (Numbers 6) is the separated-one Hebrew vow that includes abstaining from wine. *Nazarene* refers to Nazareth. John the Baptist was a Nazirite. |
| **"Paul respected the Jerusalem community as full equals"** | Romans 14 is one of several spots where Paul's actual valuation leaks through. Calling them "weak" — even diplomatically — is theological positioning. |
