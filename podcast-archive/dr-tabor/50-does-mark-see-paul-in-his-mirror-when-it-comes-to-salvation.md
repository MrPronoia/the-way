## Summary

This is the soteriology companion to Video #30 ("Is Mark's Gospel Mainly a Mouthpiece for Paul's Ideas?"). The recording is an audio clip from a Zoom session with students in Tabor's "Creating Jesus — Why Mark's Gospel Was Forgotten" online course. The driving question: when Mark talks about salvation — what it means to "have eternal life," "enter the kingdom," be "saved" — is he reflecting Paul's framework or running his own track? Tabor's working method is "let Mark be Mark" — read the gospel as a self-contained composite and ask what its own soteriology looks like before importing other texts. Then compare the result to Paul.

The piece moves through Mark's salvation grammar piece by piece: the hundredfold-in-this-life passage and the persecution-then-glory frame of Mark 10; the gospel-to-all-nations mission of Mark 13 (which lines up with the "mystery now revealed to all nations" doxology of Romans 16); the Daniel 7 son-of-man-coming-in-clouds Christology of Mark 13 and Mark 14 (paralleled by Paul's 1 Thessalonians 4 parousia); the Gehenna-versus-resurrection binary of Mark 8 and Mark 9; the transfiguration as a *proleptic* glimpse of the kingdom; the "binding the strong man" exorcism eschatology of Mark 3; and the resurrection-into-angel-like-bodies passage of Mark 12 (which Luke clarifies as "those worthy to attain *that age*"). Tabor's verdict: Mark's view of "the age to come" is not a millennial earthly kingdom with Jesus on a throne in Jerusalem. It is the cosmos being dissolved and a new heavenly age beginning — exactly Paul's framework in 1 Corinthians 15 and Galatians 4 ("the Jerusalem above is the mother of us all").

Then the climax: Mark 10:45 — "the son of man came not to be served but to serve and give his life as a ransom for many." Tabor argues this is the most Pauline verse in Mark and the place where Mark's soteriology either is or is not what evangelical Christianity has made of it. He raises the question deliberately: does Mark really teach substitutionary blood atonement in the "nothing but the blood of Jesus" sense? Maybe. Maybe not. The "ransom for many" allusion goes back to Isaiah 53, which Tabor reads as *corporate* — the Suffering Servant is the little flock of righteous ones whose suffering brings the kingdom, not a single individual paying a price for cosmic sin debt. And he leaves the discussion open: even *Paul*, he is no longer sure, holds the Lutheran-Augustinian transactional reading. Paul certainly teaches that Jesus's blood was poured out for forgiveness, but he also teaches in Romans 8 that glorification is conditional on participation — "provided that we suffer with him." Mark looks the same way. Salvation in Mark is not signed-sealed-delivered. It is repentance, baptism, keeping the commandments, forgiving enemies, following on the cross-road, and arriving at resurrection. The blood is part of the framework, but so is the discipleship.

## Key Teachings

### Mark's Salvation Map: Hundredfold Now, Eternal Life in the Age to Come

The framing passage is Mark 10:28-31 — Peter says "we have left everything and followed you," and Jesus answers with a two-stage promise: in *this* time, a hundredfold of houses, brothers, sisters, mothers, children, and lands *with persecutions*; in the *age to come*, eternal life. Mark therefore has a clear "two ages" structure. There is the present age (the time of mission and persecution) and a coming age (eternal life). The "many first will be last and last first" line caps the saying.

Tabor's method here is important: "Let Mark be Mark." Take Mark as a composite work and assemble its own internal soteriology before importing Q, Paul, or Matthew. When you do that, Mark turns out to have a remarkably consistent picture — and the picture matches Paul's two-ages eschatology almost exactly.

### The Gospel to All Nations — Mark 13 and Romans 16

Mark 13:10 is one of the loudest tells of where Mark stands in the development of the movement: "the gospel must first be preached to all the nations" before the end comes. This is the Pauline mission program reflected in narrative form. A student in the Zoom session asks whether "the mystery" of Mark connects to the closing doxology of Romans (Romans 16:25-26): "the mystery held in silence through times and ages but now made manifest through the prophetic scriptures and made known to all the nations for the purpose of the submission of faith."

Tabor's read: Mark and Paul share the same framework. The mystery is the kingdom of God going out to *all* the nations — not just Israel. The end does not come until the message has gone everywhere. The endurance of believers under persecution during that interval is itself part of the salvation pattern. Mark is writing from inside that interim period.

### Mark 14 + 1 Thessalonians 4 — The Son of Man Coming in the Clouds

When Mark's Jesus stands before the high priest in Mark 14:62 and finally identifies himself, he uses Daniel 7 language: "I am, and you will see the son of man seated at the right hand of power and coming with the clouds of heaven." This is paralleled in Mark 13:26-27 — after the tribulation, the son of man comes in the clouds with great power and glory, sends out the angels, and gathers the elect from the four winds.

Tabor pairs this directly with Paul in 1 Thessalonians 4:16-17: "the Lord himself shall descend from heaven with a shout, with the trumpet of God, and the dead in Christ shall rise first... then we who are alive shall be caught up together with them in the clouds." Same scene. Same Daniel-7 substructure. Same eschatological resolution. Paul's parousia and Mark's son-of-man-coming are the same event, told twice.

Tabor flags one subtlety: Daniel 7's son of man is *corporate* — the saints of the most high collectively receive the kingdom. Jesus claims the role (and Paul claims it for Jesus) but the role itself is supposed to be shared. That is why Jesus in Mark 8:34 "calls the multitude with his disciples" and tells them "if any man would come after me, let him take up his cross" — the cross-road is participatory, not vicarious.

### The Gehenna-Versus-Resurrection Binary

Mark's salvation map is sharp at the edges. The alternative to entering the kingdom is being cast into Gehenna — the trash-dump valley outside Jerusalem, the Markan metaphor for being thrown out of the messianic banquet. Mark 9:43-48 piles up the imagery: cut off the hand, the foot, pluck out the eye — anything to avoid being cast into Gehenna where the worm does not die and the fire is not quenched.

Mark 8:36-38 frames the binary in transactional terms: "What does it profit a man to gain the whole world and forfeit his life? For what can a man give in return for his life? Whoever is ashamed of me and my words in this adulterous and sinful generation, of him will the son of man be ashamed when he comes in the glory of his father with the holy angels." Confessing Christ before the world — being unashamed under persecution — is one of the salvation criteria.

Tabor: Mark's god is not just "God is love." There is real judgment in Mark, and it is harsh.

### Mark 3 — Binding the Strong Man

A student question pulls Tabor into Mark 3:27 — "no one can enter a strong man's house and plunder his goods unless he first binds the strong man." This is Mark's exorcism eschatology. Jesus's casting out of demons is not just compassion-healing — it is the opening assault on the kingdom of Satan. The demons themselves recognize it: "I know who you are... have you come to punish us before the time?" The kingdom of God is coming with power because the kingdom of Satan is being dismantled.

Tabor connects this to Paul's "God of this world" language in 2 Corinthians 4:4 and to Paul's claim that "the saints will judge the world... we will judge angels" (1 Corinthians 6:2-3). Mark and Paul share the same cosmology: the present age is under the dominion of evil powers, and the messianic age comes through the overthrow of those powers.

### The Transfiguration as Proleptic Kingdom

Mark 9:1 — "truly I say to you, some standing here will not taste death before they see the kingdom of God come with power" — is followed *six days later* by the transfiguration. Tabor reads the transfiguration as the proleptic answer to Mark 9:1: Peter, James, and John get a preview of the kingdom-in-glory, with Jesus's garments glistening intensely white "as no fuller on earth could bleach them," and Moses and Elijah appearing alongside him.

Why Moses and Elijah? Both fasted forty days in the desert (as Jesus does at the start of Mark). Both are prophetic figures of cosmic-transition magnitude. Both are part of Mark's vision of the glorified messianic age. The transfiguration is Mark's way of saying: this is what the age to come looks like, this is what resurrection-bodies look like, this is what the kingdom-in-power looks like.

### Mark 12 + Luke's Clarification — Like Angels in Heaven

The Sadducees test Jesus with the seven-husbands question (Mark 12:18-27). Jesus answers: "when they rise from the dead, they neither marry nor are given in marriage, but are like angels in heaven." Mark stops there. Luke, copying Mark, adds the clarifying phrase: "those who are accounted worthy to attain *that age* and the resurrection from the dead" (Luke 20:35).

Tabor: Luke is making explicit what is implicit in Mark — there is an age to come, and resurrection bodies in that age are angelic, not flesh-and-blood. This matches Paul's "spiritual body" of 1 Corinthians 15. It does not match a millennial-kingdom theology where the resurrected ride horses through Jerusalem. Mark, like Paul, is talking about the cosmos being dissolved and a new heavenly age beginning. "The Jerusalem above is the mother of us all," says Paul in Galatians 4:26. Mark's eschatology fits that frame.

### Mark 10:45 — The Pauline Crescendo and the Open Question

This is the climax of the talk and the most important verse in the episode. Mark 10:45: "the son of man came not to be served but to serve and to give his life as a ransom for many."

Tabor's framing: this *is* a Crescendo verse — one of the structural pillars of Mark's gospel — and it *is* an allusion to Isaiah 53 ("my servant shall make many righteous"). The word "many" is the giveaway, just as in Video #30 and Video #04. So Mark is reflecting Pauline soteriology at the textual surface.

But then Tabor opens the harder question. Does Mark teach the evangelical-Christian "nothing but the blood of Jesus" substitutionary-atonement reading? Tabor's answer is more nuanced than either "yes" or "no." Isaiah 53 itself is *corporate* — the Suffering Servant is the little flock of righteous Israelites whose collective suffering brings the kingdom, not a single individual paying off a sin-debt to a wrathful deity. If Mark inherits Isaiah 53 through Paul, and if Paul reads Isaiah 53 corporately, then Mark 10:45 is about participation in the Servant pattern, not vicarious satisfaction. The "ransom for many" is the cost of the messianic age — paid by Jesus *and* by all who follow him on the cross-road.

### Tabor's Own Open Question About Paul

The most interesting confession in the episode is at the very end. Tabor says: "I'm not completely convinced. There's several things about Paul that I'm starting to think... I don't think he thinks Jesus is God. He does think Satan is the god of the world. He does think Jesus is at the right hand of God. But he also believes that the suffering of Jesus pouring out his blood through which people are forgiven is also something *that everybody's called to do.*"

The key Pauline passage: Romans 8:17 — "we will be glorified with him, *provided that we suffer with him.*" That "provided that" is the conditional clause that breaks the Lutheran-Augustinian reading. Salvation is not signed-sealed-delivered when you accept the blood. It is participatory. You suffer with him *or* you are not glorified with him.

Tabor's working hypothesis: Paul has been "Lutheranized" and "Augustinized" and turned into a mystery-religion preacher. The actual Paul — read directly — is closer to Mark, and Mark is closer to Jesus, and the message is closer to the Suffering Servant pattern of Isaiah 53 read corporately. The blood is part of the framework. So is forgiving enemies, keeping the commandments, not defiling your heart, and following on the cross-road. You don't get to skip the second half.

### Mark's Practical Soteriology — The Cluster

Tabor lists Mark's actual salvation requirements as a cluster, not a single transaction:

- Satan is overthrown (Mark 3 — binding the strong man)
- The Temple is removed (Mark 13 — not one stone on another)
- The gospel goes to the nations (Mark 13:10)
- Followers repent (Mark 1 — John's baptism)
- They forgive others (Mark 11:25)
- They follow the commandments (Mark 10:19, Mark 12 — love God and neighbor)
- They are not defiled by the 19 inner things of Mark 7 — evil thoughts, fornications, thefts, murders, adulteries, coveting, etc.
- They love God and neighbor as the great commandment
- They are prepared to sacrifice everything (Mark 8:34, Mark 10:21)
- They suffer with the community (Mark 13 — persecution)
- They gain a hundredfold community in this age (Mark 10:30)
- They gain resurrection and eternal life in the age to come (Mark 12, Mark 10:30)

This is *not* "accept the blood of Jesus and you have eternal security." It is the full Suffering-Servant participation pattern.
