## Summary

Dr. Tabor's "core view" of Easter morning: a careful historical reconstruction that throws out both the typical Christian apologetic and the typical skeptical "they stole the body" claim, and instead reads the four gospels plus the Gospel of Peter in strict chronological order. The empty tomb is real — but it has a mundane explanation. Joseph of Arimathea, a sympathetic Sanhedrin member, obtained Pilate's permission to remove Jesus from the cross before sundown Thursday because Jewish law (Deuteronomy 21:22-23, confirmed by Josephus) forbade leaving an executed body up past sunset, much less through Passover. With no time for proper burial rites, Joseph laid Jesus in a *temporary* nearby tomb — not his family tomb — and blocked it with a stone. The plan was always to move the body to a permanent grave once the Sabbath ended Saturday night. By the time the women arrived at dawn Sunday they were already twelve hours too late: Joseph had returned at sundown Saturday and moved the body. The tomb was empty because it was always meant to be empty.

The second move is reading the four resurrection accounts as a development, not a harmony. Mark — our earliest gospel, originally ending at 16:8 — has *no resurrection appearances at all*, only the young man saying "go tell the disciples he goes before you to Galilee." John 20:1-10 (Tabor's pick for the most realistic account) treats it exactly the way the women themselves did: "they have taken the Lord out of the tomb and we do not know where they have laid him." The folded linen and head-cloth left behind fit a burial party tidying up a temporary spot, not a resuscitated corpse. Then Matthew adds angels and earthquakes and Roman guards. Luke and John (the latest) add Jerusalem appearances where Jesus eats fish and lets Thomas finger his wounds. The trajectory is unmistakable: a visionary tradition with Galilean roots is being progressively physicalized for apologetic reasons. The earlier you read, the less corpse-revival you find.

The bombshell evidence is the Gospel of Peter's abrupt ending. After the empty-tomb scene, the text says **the disciples "wept and mourned" through all eight days of unleavened bread**, then went home to Galilee — Peter and Andrew back to fishing on the Sea of Galilee. This fits Mark ("you will see him in Galilee"). It fits the appendix at John 21 (Peter fishing). It fits Paul, who never mentions a Jerusalem appearance and lists Galilean apostles as primary witnesses. It does *not* fit Luke and John's "Jesus appeared the same evening in Jerusalem and stayed forty days" framework. The where, when, and what of the appearances are flatly contradictory between the early and late sources, and Tabor refuses to harmonize them. The internal evidence decisively favors Mark, Paul, and the Gospel of Peter: faith in the resurrection arose **in Galilee, weeks after the crucifixion, as a visionary experience** — not in Jerusalem on Easter Sunday with a resuscitated corpse.

## Key Teachings

### Joseph of Arimathea's "Burial of Emergency" Explains the Empty Tomb

Jewish law required executed bodies to come down before sunset (Deuteronomy 21:22-23). Josephus confirms the practice. Jesus died around 3 p.m. Thursday, just hours before Passover began at sundown — followed immediately by the weekly Sabbath. There was no window for proper burial rites (washing, anointing with oil and spices, the seven-day mourning structure that Jewish family burial required). So Joseph of Arimathea, a sympathetic Sanhedrin member, obtained the body from Pilate and placed it in **the nearest available unused tomb** — not his family tomb (Jewish law would have prohibited mixing an executed criminal with his ancestors) and not a permanent burial site.

John 19:41-42 preserves the key detail Mark omits: the tomb was new, unused, and "close at hand because of the Jewish day of preparation." It was a holding spot to keep the body safe from predators over the holiday. **Joseph's intention was always to come back when the Sabbath ended Saturday night and move the body to a permanent grave.**

### The Women Arrived Twelve Hours Too Late

Mark 16:1 says Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of Jesus, and Salome came at dawn Sunday to begin the proper anointing rites — assuming Joseph would meet them and cooperate. They had no idea Joseph had already returned the moment the Sabbath ended Saturday at sundown. By Sunday morning the body had been gone for twelve hours. The tomb was empty *exactly as one would expect* given a temporary burial — not because of theft, not because of stolen-body conspiracy, not because of resurrection. The realistic story is hiding in plain sight in John 20:1-10: "they have taken the Lord out of the tomb and we do not know where they have laid him."

### John 20:1-10 Is the Earliest Layer of Empty-Tomb Tradition

Tabor's pick for the closest we can get to what actually happened Easter morning is **John 20:1-10 read as an independent account, ignoring everything from verse 11 onward**. Mary goes to the tomb alone before dawn, sees the stone removed, runs to Peter and the Beloved Disciple. They race to the tomb. They see the linen wrappings and the folded head-cloth left behind. They go home. **There is no angel, no Risen Christ, no proclamation, no resurrection claim — only the matter-of-fact observation that someone has moved the body.** The disciples "went back to their homes" — and their homes are in Galilee.

This passage reads like it predates Mark. Embedded in our latest gospel is what may be our earliest empty-tomb tradition, before the apologetic embellishments overwhelmed it.

### Mark Originally Ended at 16:8 — No Resurrection Appearances

Mark's oldest manuscripts end at verse 8: the women flee in fear and "said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid." Everything that follows in modern Bibles (the "longer ending" with Jesus appearing to disciples) was added by later scribes who could not stomach a gospel without appearances. **The original ending is significant precisely because it is so awkward.** If Mark knew of Jerusalem appearances on Easter Sunday with Jesus eating meals and showing his wounds, why omit them? Because he did not know of any such tradition. He knew the empty tomb. He knew the disciples would eventually see Jesus *in Galilee*. That was the entire tradition he had received.

### The "Galilee vs. Jerusalem" Contradiction Is Not Reconcilable

Tabor refuses the standard apologetic move of harmonizing all four gospels into a composite ("they all give different details, but combined you get the truth"). The contradictions are too sharp:

- **Where:** Mark and Matthew place all appearances in Galilee. Luke and John place them in Jerusalem on Easter Sunday itself.
- **When:** Mark/Matthew imply weeks later (after the Passover week ends and disciples travel home). Luke/John have appearances on the same Sunday and continuing for forty days *in Jerusalem*.
- **What:** Matthew's mountain scene is a visionary epiphany — "some doubted" even while seeing him. Luke and John have a resuscitated corpse eating fish and being touched.

These are not minor variations. They are mutually exclusive narratives. The early layer (Mark + Matthew + Paul + Gospel of Peter ending) consistently places the first sightings in Galilee, weeks later, and visionary in character. The late layer (Luke + John) compresses everything into Jerusalem on Easter Sunday and physicalizes the encounters.

### The Gospel of Peter's Ending Is a Bombshell

A fragmentary 8th-century copy of the Gospel of Peter, discovered in 1886 in an Egyptian monk's grave, breaks off mid-sentence at a stunning passage. After the empty-tomb scene, the text says:

> "Now it was the last day of unleavened bread, and many went to their homes because the feast was at an end. But we the twelve disciples of the Lord wept and mourned, and each one grieving for what had happened returned to his own home. But I Simon Peter and my brother Andrew took our nets and went to the sea, and there was with us Levi the son of Alphaeus, whom the Lord—"

[text breaks off]

**The disciples spent the entire week of Passover in Jerusalem weeping and grieving — not celebrating any resurrection appearances.** Then they went home to Galilee, still in despair, and Peter and Andrew resumed their fishing business. This matches Mark ("you will see him in Galilee"), matches the appendix at John 21 (Peter fishing on the Sea of Galilee when Jesus appears), and flatly contradicts Luke and John's "Jerusalem on Easter Sunday" timeline.

### The Late Embellishments Are Apologetic, Not Historical

Why did Luke and John move the appearances to Jerusalem and physicalize them? Tabor: by the late first and early second century, sophisticated Greek critics like Lucian, Trypho, and Celsus were charging that Christianity thrived among "ignorant simple-minded gullible classes" who fell for "foolish tales of deluded women and hallucinations passed off as visions." Rival accounts of divine men with similar resurrection traditions existed — Apollonius of Tyana, born around the same time as Jesus, was reportedly fathered by Zeus, healed the sick, raised the dead, and was taken bodily to heaven. A stone inscription in Asia Minor reads: "The tomb in Tyana received his body, but in truth heaven received him."

In this competitive religious marketplace, **a vague tradition of Galilean visions wasn't going to win**. So Luke and John concretize the encounters: Jesus eats fish, shows his wounds, lets Thomas touch him, walks through locked doors. The narrative says "I am not a ghost. I am flesh and bones." This is **directly contradicting Paul's emphatic claim** in 1 Corinthians 15 that Jesus became a "life-giving spirit" — embodied, yes, but not corruptible physical matter.

### Paul Is the Decisive Earlier Witness

Paul wrote roughly a decade after the crucifixion. He never mentions a Jerusalem appearance. He never describes Jesus eating, being touched, or walking around in physical flesh. His own experience (Damascus road, ~37 CE) was a visionary "seeing." His framework for resurrection is **re-embodiment in a new spiritual body**, with the old body discarded "like clothing" (2 Corinthians 5). A resuscitated corpse would not be resurrection in Paul's sense at all.

When Paul says in 1 Corinthians 15:3-4 that Jesus "was buried" and "raised on the third day," he is acknowledging the burial tradition without endorsing the late gospels' resuscitation imagery. What was raised, for Paul, was not the physical body but a new spiritual existence. **The old body in the tomb is not a problem for his framework.**

### Why Reading the New Testament Backward Restores the Original Story

Tabor's methodological insight: most people start with Luke and John (the latest sources) and read Paul through that lens. **Reverse it.** Start with Paul (earliest), move to Mark (no appearances), then Matthew (Galilee mountain, "some doubted"), then the John 20:1-10 fragment, then the Gospel of Peter ending — and only finally to Luke 24 and John 20:11+ and 21. Read this way, the trajectory of embellishment is obvious. Paul becomes "the shadow behind all four of our gospels," but to understand what Paul actually meant you have to put the later texts aside.

## Misconceptions Addressed

| Misconception | Tabor's Response |
|---------------|------------------|
| "The empty tomb proves the resurrection" | The empty tomb has a mundane historical explanation — Joseph of Arimathea returned at sundown Saturday and moved the body to a permanent grave. The women came twelve hours too late. |
| "Joseph buried Jesus in his own family tomb" | No — John 19:41 says it was a *new, unused* tomb just happened to be nearby. Jewish law would have prohibited Joseph from mixing an executed criminal with his ancestors. |
| "The disciples saw Jesus in Jerusalem on Easter Sunday" | Mark, Matthew, Paul, and the Gospel of Peter all place the first appearances in Galilee, weeks later. The Jerusalem-Sunday appearances are a late layer in Luke and John. |
| "All four gospels agree on the resurrection — just minor details differ" | They contradict on where (Galilee vs. Jerusalem), when (immediately vs. weeks later), and what (visionary vs. resuscitated corpse). These cannot be harmonized without ignoring the earliest sources. |
| "If the body were still in a tomb somewhere, the disciples couldn't have believed in resurrection" | This assumes resurrection = corpse-revival. For Paul (and likely Jesus), resurrection meant *re-embodiment in a new spiritual body* — the old corpse left behind like discarded clothing. The location of the bones was irrelevant. |
| "Luke's 'flesh and bones' Jesus eating fish settles the physical question" | This is the latest layer, written for second-century apologetic purposes against critics like Celsus who said "everyone has visions — prove it was physical." It directly contradicts Paul's "life-giving spirit." |
| "Stolen body / hallucination / swoon theories explain it best" | None of these explain the evidence as cleanly as the temporary-burial-then-permanent-move framework. The body wasn't stolen — it was moved by the same people who put it there for the explicit purpose of moving it. |
