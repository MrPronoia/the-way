## Summary

This is Tabor going further than he has gone before on a thesis he had been circling for years: Mark's Gospel is not an independent stream of tradition from Peter. It is, functionally, **a mouthpiece for Paul**. The episode opens with a roughly seven-minute solo introduction Tabor recorded just before a Zoom session for his "Creating Jesus" Mark course — a kind of live-thinking-out-loud preface — and then transitions into the second half of his recent long-form interview with Kerry Shirts ("the Backyard Professor"). Tabor announces in the opening minutes that he is "becoming more and more convinced" New Testament theology as a whole goes back to Paul. Mark, he now thinks, is "pristine" — relatively untouched by later editorial smoothing — and is reflecting what scholars call Pauline Christianity in something close to its original shape. Matthew copies 90% of Mark and Luke copies most of it too, so the Pauline framework gets embedded in the synoptic core before the church even notices.

The textual case occupies the middle of the conversation. Tabor walks through specific Markan passages and shows where each one maps directly onto Paul: the divorce decree in Mark 10 quoted verbatim from 1 Corinthians 7; the Last Supper in Mark 14 lifted almost word-for-word from 1 Corinthians 11 (a private meeting of Jesus and the Twelve that Mark could not have witnessed and that Paul claims he received "from the Lord" by revelation); the Christology of Mark 10:18 ("why do you call me good, there is none good but God") fitting Paul's Adam Christology rather than the high pre-existence Christology of later texts; the *Shema* answer in Mark 12 ("the Lord our God is one") matching 1 Corinthians 8:6 almost identically; Mark's vice list in chapter 7 (13 things that defile from inside) tracking the Pauline vice lists in Romans 1 and 1 Corinthians 6; and the great misreading of Mark 7 — "thus he declared all foods clean" — which Tabor insists is NOT in the Greek and is actually a punchline joke about ritual handwashing (the food goes into the toilet, *that's* what cleans it). Above all, Mark ends with no resurrection appearances, an empty tomb, and a young man (not an angel) telling the women Jesus has gone to Galilee — exactly what you would expect if Mark's resurrection theology is Paul's "spiritual body" of 1 Corinthians 15 rather than the resuscitated corpse of Luke and John.

The closing third of the conversation broadens to suffering, glory, and the cost of discipleship. Tabor draws on Dietrich Bonhoeffer's *The Cost of Discipleship* ("when Christ calls a man, he calls him to die") and Nietzsche's barb ("there was only one Christian, and he died on a cross") to drive home Mark's actual ethic: take up the cross literally, become a servant, lose everything, expect crucifixion — not "accept Christ and get the glory." The disciples in Mark never get it, they flee at the cross, they never reappear. That ending isn't a manuscript problem — it's the theology. Tabor closes with reflections on Paul's "thorn in the flesh," the resurrection body as "life-giving spirit" rather than resuscitated corpse, and the absurd medieval-Catholic mythology of Mary not defecating because she was too holy. The headline thesis remains: if you read Mark carefully and read primary Paul carefully, you are reading the same theology in two different genres.

## Key Teachings

### Mark as Pauline Mouthpiece — The Core Thesis

Tabor's most direct statement of the position appears in the opening monologue: "I would even go so far as to say maybe Mark is not an independent testimony to a version of Christianity but is actually a mouthpiece for Paul and his ideas — that he's emulating Paul and repeating Paul." This is a sharper claim than "Mark was influenced by Paul." Tabor is arguing that Mark's gospel is essentially Pauline theology dramatized in narrative form. The author may never have met Paul personally — Tabor isn't claiming biographical contact — but he has so thoroughly absorbed Pauline Christianity that his Jesus *is* Paul's Christ projected back onto Galilee.

The downstream implication is significant: because Matthew copies 90% of Mark and Luke copies the bulk of it as well, the Pauline framework becomes the **structural skeleton** of three of the four canonical gospels before anyone notices. The historical Jesus is harder to extract than scholars usually admit, because the synoptic substrate itself is already a Pauline product.

### The Last Supper Lifted From 1 Corinthians 11

The single clearest textual smoking gun. Mark 14's Last Supper — "this is my body... this is my blood... shed for many" — is almost verbatim from 1 Corinthians 11:23-26. Tabor's challenge: how did Mark know what Jesus said at a private meal between Jesus and the Twelve? "It's almost just directly lifted from that. And what is really amazing... is Paul says 'I received this from the Lord and I'm delivering it now to you, that on the night he was betrayed he took bread...' So how did Mark have this idea of what Jesus said on the Last Supper? That's a private meeting of Jesus and the Twelve. So how does he get this? You say, well there's probably a tradition going around of what Jesus said. Not according to Paul. He said 'I got it from the Lord, from Jesus.'"

In other words: the chain of custody runs Paul (by revelation) → Paul's letter → Mark. Not Jesus → Peter → Mark. The Eucharistic words enter the gospel tradition through Paul.

### The Divorce Decree (Mark 10 ↔ 1 Corinthians 7)

A smaller but telling parallel. Mark 10:11-12's divorce decree — "whoever puts away his wife and marries another commits adultery, and if she puts away her husband and marries another she commits adultery" — appears in Paul's earlier letter in 1 Corinthians 7. Paul knows this saying. Mark, writing later, reproduces it. Tabor flags this as one of several places where the synoptic Jesus is quoting Paul's letter rather than Paul quoting Jesus.

### Mark's Christology Is Adam Christology, Not Pre-Existence

Mark 10:18 — "Why do you call me good? There is none good but God" — is unintelligible if Jesus is the second person of the Trinity. It makes perfect sense if Mark shares Paul's Adam Christology. Tabor: "Paul has an Adam Christology. He believes that flesh-and-blood human beings have the potential to become divine — not just Jesus, but he's 'the firstborn of many brothers.'"

The Philippians 2 hymn ("though he was in the form of God, he did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped") is, on this reading, NOT about a pre-existent Christ descending from heaven and returning. It's about Jesus as the new Adam: in the form of God (because Adam was made in God's image), refusing to grasp at equality (which is what the serpent told Eve she would gain by eating the fruit), and therefore exalted as the prototype of a transformed humanity. Mark's Christology fits this frame: a fully human Jesus who is glorified through suffering and resurrection, not a divine being slumming on earth.

### The *Shema* in Mark 12 ↔ 1 Corinthians 8:6

When a scribe asks Jesus the great commandment in Mark 12, Jesus quotes the Shema: "Hear O Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is one." Paul writes in 1 Corinthians 8:6: "For us there is one God the Father from whom are all things, and one Lord Jesus Christ through whom are all things." Same monotheism. Same framework. Mark's Jesus and Paul are reciting the same theology. The "through whom are all things" in Paul, Tabor argues, refers to the **new creation** in Christ, not Jesus creating the universe in the beginning. Later Paul (Colossians, Ephesians) develops a cosmic-creator Christology; early Paul and Mark do not.

### Mark 7's Vice List Tracks Paul's Vice Lists

When Jesus in Mark 7:21-22 lists what truly defiles a person, he names 13 things: "evil thoughts, fornications, thefts, murders, adulteries, coveting, wickedness, deceit, licentiousness, envy, slander, pride, foolishness." Tabor: this is the same list Paul gives in Romans 1, 1 Corinthians 6, and Galatians 5. The synoptic gospel and the Pauline epistle are running on the same ethical operating system. Tabor reads Paul's vice list aloud and notes, "Sounds like Romans 1, sounds like 1 Corinthians 6. Mark is reflecting Paul."

### "Thus He Declared All Foods Clean" Is Not in the Greek

The most pointed scholarly correction in the episode. The famous parenthetical gloss in modern translations of Mark 7:19 — "(thus he declared all foods clean)" — is **not present in the Greek text**. It is a translator's interpretation. The Greek literally describes the food going into the stomach and being passed out through the bowels — *cleansing all foods*, meaning the body excretes the impurity.

Tabor reads the passage as a joke: "He's not even talking about food. He's talking about ritual purity. He says what goes into the mouth doesn't defile — the thing you're worried about will come out in the toilet. He's playing with them. The body takes care of it. But here's what you should worry about — what comes out of the heart." The whole point of Mark 7 is *ritual* purity (the Pharisaic minhag of handwashing before meals), not dietary law (kosher rules). The misreading "Jesus declared all foods clean" has been used for centuries to justify Christian abandonment of kosher. It is not what Mark says.

This matters for the Mark-Pauline thesis because Paul says essentially the same thing in Romans 14 and 1 Corinthians 8-10: it's not the food, it's the disposition of the heart. Mark's Jesus has been retrofitted with Paul's ritual-purity position.

### Mark's Resurrection Is Paul's Resurrection — Empty Tomb, No Appearances

The original ending of Mark (16:8 — the women fleeing the tomb in fear, telling no one) has no resurrection appearances. A young man (not an angel) tells the women Jesus has gone to Galilee. No earthquake. No stone-moving angel. No Jesus showing his wounds. No fish-eating. No "doubting Thomas." Just an empty tomb and a promise.

Tabor: "You see, Mark doesn't have any appearances. In fact, in Mark a young man — not an angel — there's no angel moving a stone, there's no earthquake. Matthew would think this is really deficient." Why does Mark end this way? Because Mark's resurrection is **Paul's resurrection**: a transformation into a "life-giving spirit" body (1 Corinthians 15:45), glorious, immortal, no longer flesh and blood. You cannot photograph it. Paul, asked "what kind of body?", calls the questioner a fool and says God gives a body suited to its status — not a resuscitated corpse.

The later gospels (Luke, John) add resuscitated-corpse stories — Jesus eats fish, Thomas touches the wounds, the body is solid enough to be handled. Tabor argues these are apologetic additions, written in response to second-century critics like Celsus ("how do you know they aren't just hysterical women full of fables?"). Mark's quieter ending is closer to Paul's actual theology and probably closer to the earliest stratum of Easter belief.

### Mark's Disciples Never Get It — And That's the Point

A striking pattern Tabor highlights: "The disciples in Mark never get it. Ever. In Mark. And they run away at the cross and you never see them again. They all forsook him and fled." Mark juxtaposes the disciples' incomprehension against Jesus's teaching, scene after scene. They argue about who is greatest. They ask to sit at his right and left hand in glory. Peter rebukes Jesus for predicting suffering. They flee at the arrest. Peter denies. They are absent from the crucifixion. They are absent from the resurrection.

This anti-disciple tone matches **Paul's lone-wolf apostolic self-presentation** in Galatians: "what they are means nothing to me," "the pillars added nothing to me," "I did not consult flesh and blood." Mark's Twelve are functionally the same dramatic foil Paul's Jerusalem pillars are in his letters. The man who really understands Jesus, in both Paul and Mark, is *not* one of the Twelve.

### Mark's Suffering Servant Ethic = Paul's Suffering Servant Ethic

Both texts root discipleship in suffering, servanthood, and the cross — not in glory and power. Tabor: "Mark is suffering servant, Paul is suffering servant. I even think — as you know from my *Paul's Ascent to Paradise* — Paul found himself in Isaiah 49. 'Before I was in my mother's womb he called and appointed me as a messenger to the nations.' He thinks Jesus is the Isaiah 53 character — wounded for our transgressions — and he believes he is filling up what is lacking in the sufferings of Christ."

Mark's Jesus repeatedly insists: take up the cross (literal — they will nail you up too), be the least, become a servant, become like a child. The disciples want the Transfiguration glory of Mark 9; Jesus pivots immediately to Jerusalem and execution. This is the same ethic Paul preaches in Romans 8: "the sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the glory that shall be revealed... provided that we suffer with him, that we may be glorified with him." Tabor calls Romans 8 "the main passage for Paul on glory" and says: "That's Mark right there. That's Mark. Mark has absorbed that message."

### Mark Is the First Jewish Document of the Post-Temple Period

A novel claim Tabor floats and says he has not heard others make: "I sometimes have said — and I've never heard anybody say this actually, but I'm sure somebody has — that Mark is the first Jewish document from the post-Temple period, and it is proposing a new way to follow the God of Israel without the Temple."

The widow's two copper coins (Mark 12:41-44), placed immediately before the Temple destruction prediction (Mark 13:2 — "not one stone shall be left upon another"), reframes the religion. The widow has given more than the wealthy donors. True devotion is the self-emptying of the powerless, not the ritual machinery of the Temple cult. Mark, writing after 70 CE (or anticipating it), is showing his community how to be the people of God without sacrifices, without a holy mountain, without ritual purity codes. Paul had begun that work in his letters ("the kingdom of God is not food and drink... touch not, taste not, handle not"). Mark dramatizes it in narrative.

### Mark Anticipates the Bonhoeffer Reading — "When Christ Calls a Man He Calls Him to Die"

The most emotionally weighted moment of the episode is Tabor's invocation of Dietrich Bonhoeffer. Bonhoeffer, the Lutheran pastor executed by the Nazis for his role in the Hitler resistance, threw a copy of Mein Kampf off his pulpit on the Sunday Hitler ordered Lutheran pulpits to display it next to the Bible. He was arrested and ultimately killed. From prison he wrote *The Cost of Discipleship*. Its opening line: "When Christ calls a man, he calls him to die."

Tabor: "It's pure Mark. Pure Mark." And then Nietzsche: "There was only one Christian. He died on a cross." Not as an attack on Christianity but as an indictment of how few have actually done what Jesus said. Mark's ethic is not "accept Jesus and you have eternal security." It is "follow me to Jerusalem and you will be killed." The contemporary church has domesticated this into self-help. Mark is trying to reverse that drift — even within his own first-century moment, when believers were already drifting into a "glory hallelujah mode" that Mark sees as a betrayal.

### Paul's Thorn in the Flesh as the Necessary Counterweight to Paradise

Late in the episode Tabor pivots to 2 Corinthians 12: Paul's ascent to paradise, where he heard "things unutterable," is paired in the very next verses with the "thorn in the flesh" — "a messenger of Satan to buffet me, lest I be exalted above measure." Paul asked the Lord three times to remove it. The answer: "My strength is made perfect in weakness."

Tabor reads this as the same Pauline pattern Mark dramatizes throughout: glory comes through weakness, not around it. The widow with two coins. The Jesus on the cross. The Paul with the thorn. The disciples who are supposed to become "the least." It is one theology in three voices.

### Mark Is Tough — Don't Domesticate It

Tabor closes with what he calls his "professorial preacher mode" — a warning not to soften Mark. The Mark course he is teaching, the Sunday Zoom session he is prepping for, the Bonhoeffer reading he is recommending — all converge on the same message: Mark is brutal and uncompromising, and the church has spent two thousand years making it less so. "Don't misread this text and don't domesticate it. Don't make it into some kind of easy thing — 'I believe in Jesus, I've got it made.'" The cost of discipleship in Mark is everything. That is also the cost of discipleship in Paul (Philippians 3:8 — "I count everything as loss"). The two texts agree, and the agreement is not accidental.
