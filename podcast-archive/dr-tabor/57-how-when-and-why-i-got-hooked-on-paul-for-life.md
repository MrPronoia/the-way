## Summary

A professionally narrated reading of the Preface to Tabor's 2012 book *Paul and Jesus: How the Apostle Transformed Christianity* — autobiographical, personal, and the closest thing Tabor has to a career origin story. He frames the whole 45-year quest around a single observation: **the Apostles' Creed jumps from "born of the Virgin Mary" directly to "crucified, died, and buried,"** skipping the life of Jesus entirely. Christmas and Easter — the entrance and exit points of the heavenly visitor — became the religion. The middle dropped out. Tabor's investigation: how did that happen, and where did that framework come from? His answer, stated up front: from **one man — Saul of Tarsus** — not from Jesus, Peter, John, or James. And there was a pre-Pauline Christianity, the Christianity of James the brother of Jesus and the original apostles, with tenets "sharply opposed" to Paul's, with "little in common beyond the name Jesus itself."

The personal arc: Tabor encountered Paul as an 18-year-old undergraduate Greek student. Day by day, phrase by phrase, in a small seminar, he read Paul's letters in the original Greek — and was "hooked for life." He decided by sophomore year to pursue New Testament scholarship; double-majored in Greek, Bible, and history; went on to the University of Chicago where his dissertation became his first book, *Things Unutterable*, on Paul's mystical experiences and the Hellenistic religious context. He has taught a course simply titled "Paul" for decades. His opening-day line to students: **"Paul is the most influential person in human history."** The piece closes with Tabor's May 2010 pilgrimage to the newly excavated tomb of Paul at the Basilica San Paolo Outside the Walls in Rome — Vatican carbon-14 testing in 2009 had dated the bones to the late 1st or early 2nd century. Kneeling before the sarcophagus, Tabor felt his 45-year quest had come full circle. This episode and #32 are essentially the same Preface; #32 is the longer treatment.

## Key Teachings

### From Abilene to Chicago — The Intellectual Journey

The biographical spine. Tabor's "first serious encounter" with Paul was an undergraduate Greek reading course called "The Letters of Paul" — day by day, phrase by phrase, the original Greek text in a small seminar. By sophomore year he had decided on a professional career as a New Testament scholar, with majors in Greek, Bible, and history. He calls it the most fascinating thing he had ever encountered — "probing into the very foundations of our civilization in an effort to assay our most basic assumptions, what the Germans call *Weltanschauung*."

Years later at the University of Chicago he wrote his PhD dissertation on Paul. His first book — *Things Unutterable* — dealt with Paul's mystical experiences set in the context of the Hellenistic religions of his time. The methodological lineage: Greek text first, Hellenistic context second, theological tradition only after both. This is the inverse of how most pastors and pew-Christians encounter Paul.

### The Jacob-at-the-Jabbok Vow

Tabor's framing for the engagement: "Like Jacob wrestling the angel at the river Jabbok, I lay hold on Paul and swore that I would not let go until I understood his complex ideas and probed his mysterious depths." Forty-five years later when he reads this preface, he is still wrestling. The image matters — it concedes from the outset that Paul is difficult, dense, often opaque, and that wrestling is the only honest posture. Even *2 Peter 3:16* admits that Paul's letters "contain things hard to understand." Tabor's career stance: **engage Paul; don't dismiss him and don't domesticate him.**

### "Paul Is the Most Influential Person in Human History"

The startling first-day assertion Tabor opens his "Paul" course with every semester. He admits the language is "somewhat extravagant" but defends it: Western civilization's basic assumptions about reality, ethics, selfhood, and salvation rest in a singular way upon **the heavenly visions and apparitions of the Apostle Paul.** Every Christian service across every major tradition — Catholic, Protestant, Greek Orthodox — runs on Pauline vocabulary: the creeds, the hymns, the sermons, the sacraments. Birth, baptism, confirmation, marriage, death — "it is predominantly Paul who is invoked to express meaning and significance."

The cost: "Jesus as a historical figure — a Jewish Messiah of his own time who sought to see the kingdom of God established on Earth — has been largely lost to our culture." The Pauline overlay is so total most Christians don't notice it's an overlay.

### The Pilgrimage to Paul's Tomb (Rome, May 2010)

The piece closes with Tabor's trip to the Basilica San Paolo Outside the Walls — described as both research and personal pilgrimage. Vatican archaeologists had announced in December 2006 that they had unearthed an ancient stone sarcophagus dated to the 4th century AD beneath the central altar, inscribed *Paulo Apostolo Mart* ("To Paul Apostle Martyr"). Tradition holds Paul was beheaded under Nero at Tre Fontane, buried two miles north along the Ostian Way. Constantine built the original small basilica over the site in AD 324.

On June 29, 2009, Pope Benedict XVI announced that Vatican scientists had conducted a clandestine carbon-14 test — drilling a small hole into the sarcophagus by night, extracting bone and fabric samples. The bones dated to the late 1st or early 2nd century AD. Tabor notes that most Catholic relic sites "lack any historical authenticity" — but this one "seems different." Kneeling before the sarcophagus, surrounded by pilgrims and photographers, Tabor was "deeply moved": "Somehow that physical proximity to what might likely be Paul's earthly remains marked a milestone to my 45-year search for the historical Paul. I felt I had come full circle."

### Why Write This Book — The Stakes Tabor Names

Tabor's stated audience: "Christian believers as well as those of any religion or no religion who want to understand the deeper roots of our culture." The portrait of Paul that emerges, he warns readers, "is not the pious apostle of well-worn ecclesiastical tradition, Sunday school piety, or arcane theological discussions." What you get instead is **"Paul afresh, as he emerges in his own words, with his own voice, drawn exclusively from his earliest authentic letters"** — set in critical context with the rest of the New Testament and ancient sources, including documents recovered only in the last 100 years (Nag Hammadi, the Dead Sea Scrolls). The stakes are cultural, not just theological — if Paul is the most influential person in human history, we owe ourselves an accurate picture of him.
