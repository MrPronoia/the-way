## Summary

This is the short, tight version of the argument Tabor expands across 87 minutes in Video #06. The thesis lands fast: **Paul is the only first-person witness to Jesus's resurrection in the entire New Testament** — and when you actually press Paul on what he saw and what resurrection means, the answer has almost nothing to do with the empty-tomb stories everyone defaults to. The gospels say "I was told" and "they reported." Paul says "I saw the Lord" (1 Cor 9), "he appeared to me" (1 Cor 15), "it pleased God to reveal his Son in me" (Gal 1). Twenty to twenty-five years after the crucifixion, Paul is the only voice writing in the first person, and the resurrection he describes is not a resuscitated corpse. It is metamorphosis — apotheosis — the transformation of a dust being into a glorified life-giving spirit.

Tabor walks the 1 Corinthians 15 logic patiently. The Corinthians ask him directly: *what kind of body*? Decayed bodies, corpses lost at sea, cremated remains — what are you talking about? Paul refuses to describe a body. He won't even do the standard Hebrew Bible epiphany move ("I saw a figure clothed in white, shining like the sun"). What he says is that the body is glorious, powerful, immortal, no longer of dust, no longer flesh and blood. The analogy is Adam: first Adam was of the dust, second Adam became a **life-giving spirit** (pneuma zōopoioun). Paul borrows the Romulus-ascent vocabulary of Hellenistic apotheosis (Ovid has Romulus rising up on a Roman hill as his body "melts away") but grounds it in Genesis. Resurrection is not standing-up-out-of-a-grave (anastasis in the literal sense). Resurrection is the dead being raised **incorruptible** — meaning they don't come up corruptible first and then get fixed. The dust never comes back. "Flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God."

The juicy move at the end: if Paul is the only first-person witness and Paul describes a glorified spirit-body, then **everyone else's experience must have been the same kind of experience**. When Paul says "he appeared to Peter," he uses the same verb he uses for himself. So Peter must also have seen a glorified Jesus — an apotheosis — not a resuscitated corpse walking around Jerusalem showing wounds. The gospel scenes of Jesus eating fish and inviting Thomas to touch the wounds are later, derivative, and contradict the framework of the only first-person witness we have. Tabor's punchline: "Resurrection is transformation. That would be my motto to leave with: resurrection = transformation." And tucked inside that argument is Paul's greatest idea — the one Video #06 unpacks across an hour and a half — that humanity is destined to become a new genus of glorified sons of God, a god-family, "firstborn of many brothers and sisters," ruling above the angels. The church kept Paul's letters and lost Paul's actual gospel.

## Key Teachings

### Paul Is the Only First-Person Witness to Jesus's Resurrection

This is the load-bearing observation everything else stands on. Tabor: "Paul is our only first-hand witness of the resurrection of Jesus because we don't have anybody in the New Testament talking about Jesus's resurrection that says in the first person *I saw the Lord*."

The three first-person passages:
- **1 Corinthians 9:1** — "Have I not seen Jesus our Lord?"
- **1 Corinthians 15:8** — "Last of all, as to one untimely born, he appeared to me."
- **Galatians 1:15-16** — "When it pleased God to reveal his Son in me / to me."

The gospels are second-hand reports — Matthew, Luke, John, all written by people who say "they saw," not "I saw." Mark in his original ending doesn't have appearances at all. So when you want to know what the earliest Christian view of resurrection actually was, you go to Paul first, not the gospels. Tabor's frame: **read backwards from the only witness we have.** That witness describes a glorified spirit-body, not a corpse.

Dating: Paul's testimony is from roughly 54-55 CE — within 20-25 years of the crucifixion (working date 30 CE). That's the closest source we have to the event.

### "Sightings" — and Why Paul Refuses to Describe the Body

Tabor uses an almost cheeky word: **sightings**. Like Elvis-sightings — he immediately apologizes for the comparison ("I'm not trying to trivialize it") but uses it to make a point about the grammar. The Greek verb in 1 Cor 15 is the aorist passive: *he was seen by me, he appeared to me*. Some sort of manifestation.

Here's the move that should rearrange anyone's thinking: when Paul gets the chance to describe what he saw, **he doesn't**. The Hebrew Bible has a whole genre of epiphany descriptions — Daniel sees "a man clothed in linen, his face like lightning, his arms and legs like burnished bronze." Paul doesn't do any of that. He won't tell you what Jesus looked like. He says the body is **glorious, powerful, immortal, no longer of dust, no longer flesh and blood** (Phil 3:20-21) — and that's all.

The implication Tabor draws: Paul cannot describe a body because the body he saw was not a body in the normal sense. It was light, glory, a life-giving spirit. The Acts version (blinding light on the road to Damascus) matches this. The empty-tomb-and-fish-eating-Jesus of the gospels does not.

### The Corinthian Question — "What Kind of Body?"

The Corinthians put the question directly. They've buried members of their community. They're worried. They ask Paul: bodies are decayed, bodies are corrupt, people are lost at sea — *what kind of body* are you talking about?

Paul's answer in 1 Corinthians 15 is the most extensive on-the-record statement about resurrection in early Christianity. The structure:

- The dead don't come up as half-rotted zombies that *then* get fixed. They come up **already incorruptible**.
- People lost at sea — the oceans give up "the dead that are in them" (Rev 20:13). Not physical bodies. Star bodies.
- Flesh and blood **cannot** inherit the kingdom of God (1 Cor 15:50).
- The first Adam was of the dust. The last Adam became a **life-giving spirit** (1 Cor 15:45).

Tabor's verdict: "Paul's view of resurrection has nothing to do with the physical body, doesn't depend on the physical body, I don't even think it involves the physical body."

This is not a denial of bodily resurrection — it's a redefinition. The new body is real, but it is **not** the dust body refurbished. It's a new creation.

### Resurrection Is Metamorphosis, Not Resuscitation

The motto Tabor wants you to leave with: **resurrection = transformation**.

The Greek word **anastasis** literally means "standing up" — from lying down, like a corpse rising. That's the gospels' frame: Mary Magdalene comes to the tomb, the angel says "he has been raised up." That's resuscitation language.

Paul's language is different. He calls it transformation (*allagēsometha*, 1 Cor 15:51-52), being clothed with a new body (2 Cor 5:1-5), being conformed to Christ's glorious body (Phil 3:21). The mechanism is metamorphosis — a passing from the mortal sphere of existence to the immortal sphere.

For those alive at the parousia, there's no death-and-rising at all. Paul says **"we shall not all sleep, but we shall all be changed"** — in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trumpet. The living aren't resurrected because they're not dead. They're **transformed**. Same end state, different mechanism.

So even if Paul did believe Jesus's tomb was empty (and Tabor argues he probably didn't care either way), the empty tomb is not what resurrection *is*. Resurrection is the transformation that happens *after* the dust body has perished or been left behind.

### The Romulus Parallel — Hellenistic Apotheosis

Tabor draws the comparison Paul's first-century Greco-Roman audience would have heard immediately: **Romulus**, the founder of Rome, gives a discourse on a Roman hill and his body "melts away as he rises." Ovid recounts this. It's the standard Hellenistic apotheosis narrative — a human being is taken up, transformed, divinized.

Paul has the same vocabulary. The Greek term for what he's describing is **apotheosis** — divinization, becoming a god. In Hellenistic religion this happened to emperors, heroes, founders. In Paul's framework it has happened to Jesus, and through Jesus is happening to the whole community of believers, and will eventually transform the entire cosmos.

This is the framework Tabor's University of Chicago teacher Jonathan Z. Smith called the "utopian" mode of Hellenistic religion — escape and ascent to a higher plane. Paul takes that framework but grounds it in Genesis: the dust creature is good, the world is good, and salvation is not escape but **transformation of the cosmos itself**. (Video #06 unpacks the Jonathan Z. Smith framework at length.)

### Reading Backwards From the Only Witness

The interpretive move that ties everything together. Tabor: "If I go backwards from our only witness, he says he appeared first to Peter — now he uses the **same term** as 'last of all he appeared to me.' So why should I assume that he appeared as a corpse resuscitated for Peter but for Paul it's a transformed being into this heavenly state?"

The grammar Paul uses for "he appeared to Peter" and "he appeared to me" is identical. If Paul saw a glorified spirit-being — which he clearly did — then by his own grammar that's what Peter saw too. And James. And the 500 brethren. And the apostles.

The gospel scenes where Jesus eats fish, shows his wounds, says "a spirit does not have flesh and bones as I have" — these are **later traditions** that fundamentally contradict the framework of the earliest first-person witness. They're not what Peter actually experienced. They're what the gospel writers, decades later, built around the bare fact of Easter to make sense of it for communities increasingly distant from Paul's mystical/apocalyptic framework.

### The Sons of God Family — Paul's Actual Gospel

This is the punchline Video #06 builds toward at length; here Tabor lands it in a paragraph at the end. Jesus is "the firstborn of many brothers and sisters" — the **firstborn of this whole family of God** (Rom 8:29). The new creation is not just Jesus being divinized as a one-off cosmic event. It's the prototype.

Jesus is the engineering mockup proving that the dust creature can become a glorified spirit being. The followers are next. The whole creation, in labor pains (Rom 8:18-25), is birthing the new race of glorified sons and daughters of God. Humans become "above the angels" — Paul tells the Corinthians elsewhere, "Don't you know that we shall judge angels?" (1 Cor 6:3).

This is **apotheosis as Paul's actual gospel** — the redemption of the cosmos through the transformation of human dust creatures into a new genus of divine beings. And this is what the church forgot when the apocalyptic timeline failed and the framework flipped from horizontal (this age → age to come) to vertical (soul ascends to heaven at death).

### Even Luke Tucks In a Glimpse of the Original View

Tabor finds the Adam-Christology / glorification framework surviving in one corner of the gospels: **Luke 20:34-36**. When the Sadducees pose the trick question about the woman with seven husbands, Jesus answers that those who attain the age to come and the resurrection don't marry, don't die anymore — they are **"like angels"** (isangeloi).

That's the same framework Paul is working with. Resurrection is not corpse-revival; it's becoming an angelic-class transformed being. Tabor: "Paul says they will actually judge angels" — Paul's version is even more exalted than Luke's. But the underlying frame (resurrection as ontological transformation, not biological resuscitation) is preserved in both.

This is one of those moments where the older view leaves a fingerprint on a later text, even as the surrounding gospel material has drifted toward the resuscitation framework.

## Misconceptions Addressed

| Misconception | Tabor's Response |
|---------------|------------------|
| "The empty tomb is the foundation of the resurrection" | Paul, the only first-person witness, mentions Jesus being buried but never mentions an empty tomb. The empty-tomb tradition is Mark and later. Paul's resurrection is metamorphosis, which doesn't require any particular state of the buried body. |
| "Resurrection means a corpse coming back to life" | The Greek word is anastasis (standing up), but Paul redefines it as transformation — sown perishable, raised imperishable. The dust body never comes back. A new body emerges, like a plant from a seed. |
| "Paul and the gospels agree on the resurrection" | They don't. Paul has a glorified spirit-body apotheosis. The gospels (especially Luke and John) have a flesh-and-bones resuscitated body that eats fish and invites people to touch wounds. Paul explicitly says "flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God." |
| "Paul saw Jesus in the same form Jesus had before the crucifixion" | No. Paul saw a glorified spirit-body — pure light in the Acts account, an indescribable glory in Paul's own letters. Paul refuses to describe a body. |
| "If the resurrection wasn't physical, it wasn't real" | For Paul this is exactly backwards. A physical resuscitation would be unimpressive — the resuscitated person still dies again. What matters is metamorphosis into immortal, glorious, powerful existence — that's resurrection. |
| "Mary touched Jesus, Thomas touched the wounds, Jesus ate fish — these prove physical resurrection" | These are gospel scenes from texts written 40-70 years after Paul, by people who weren't first-person witnesses. They contradict the framework Paul (the only first-person witness) lays out. |
| "Paul thought Jesus's resurrected body was made of flesh" | Paul says Jesus became a "life-giving spirit" (pneuma zōopoioun, 1 Cor 15:45). Spirit-being, not flesh-being. |
