## Summary

This is the launch video of Tabor's "Lost in Translation" series — a focused, surgical treatment of **Habakkuk 2:4**, the single Hebrew Bible verse on which Paul builds his entire doctrine of justification by faith. Paul quotes it in Romans 1:17 and Galatians 3:11 in a specific re-grammared form — "*he who through faith is righteous shall live*" — and uses it as the textual proof that sinners get declared not-guilty through faith in the blood of Jesus. Martin Luther read this verse, had his conversion, and broke from Rome on it. Protestant theology rests on this one verse. Tabor's claim: Paul has flipped what the verse actually means.

The original Habakkuk context is not soteriological at all. The prophet is addressing a community that expected God's promised judgment on Babylon and is becoming discouraged because the vision is delayed. God's word through Habakkuk: "If it tarries, wait for it; it will surely come. The righteous shall live by faith [in the vision]." The verse describes **how a righteous person lives** while trusting through delay — not **how an unrighteous person becomes righteous**. Tabor pairs the misreading with Jesus's own parable in Luke 18 (the Pharisee and the tax collector), where forgiveness flows from humble repentance with no blood-credit mechanism in sight. The video closes by previewing the next installment on Genesis 15:6 — the second pillar verse that gets the same treatment.

## Key Teachings

### Paul's Re-Grammared Habakkuk in Romans 1:17

Paul introduces his gospel in Romans 1:16-17: "I'm not ashamed of the gospel... for in it the righteousness of God is revealed through faith for faith, as it is written: *he who through faith is righteous shall live.*" Note the word order. The traditional translation of Habakkuk 2:4 is "*the just shall live by faith*" — describing how a just person lives. Paul's quote reverses the logic: faith is the *mechanism* by which a person becomes righteous, then lives. Same words, restructured grammar, completely different theological claim. This is the verse Luther read in his cell and from which the Protestant Reformation grew. The "Bedrock of Christian faith" — as Tabor calls it elsewhere — rests on this single re-reading.

### Same Verse, Same Pivot in Galatians 3:11

Paul quotes the verse again in Galatians 3:11, contrasted with a quote from Leviticus 18:5 ("he who does them shall live by them"). Paul's argument: nobody can keep the commandments, therefore Torah-keeping cannot justify, therefore justification must come "by faith." He then adds in Romans 5:9: "we are now justified by his blood." That's the full Pauline construct: faith in the shed blood of Jesus is the only way to be declared not-guilty. The entire structure depends on the Habakkuk verse meaning what Paul has it mean — and on Torah-keeping being something nobody can actually do, which is itself a caricature of Hebrew Bible theology.

### What Habakkuk Actually Said

Habakkuk is a short three-chapter prophetic book set in the 7th century BCE, prophesying the rise of Babylon and the eventual judgment-and-vindication of Israel. Habakkuk 2:3-4 in context:

> "For still the vision awaits its time; it hastens to the end; it will not lie. If it seems slow, wait for it; it will surely come, it will not delay. Behold, he whose soul is not upright in him shall fail, but the righteous shall live by his faith."

The framework is **delayed apocalyptic expectation**. The vision is true but tarrying. The test is internal — your soul, your trust, your willingness not to give up on God's promise. The righteous one *lives by his faith* in the sense of remaining faithful through the delay. This is not about how to become righteous. It is about how a righteous person endures.

### How a Righteous Person Lives vs. How a Sinner Gets Righteous

Tabor's central distinction: Paul has converted a verse about **the endurance of the righteous** into a verse about **the conversion of the unrighteous**. These are categorically different claims. The Hebrew Bible's actual theology of forgiveness — visible in Psalm 51, Psalm 103, and elsewhere — is that anyone who turns to God in humility and repentance is forgiven. No blood-of-Jesus mechanism required. The "righteous person" in the Hebrew Bible is not the sinless person — it is the person who orients toward God, walks humbly, and repents when they fail. The verse Paul claims as his pillar is about that person continuing to trust God when God's timing seems wrong.

### Jesus Agrees With Habakkuk, Not With Paul — Luke 18

The video's closing turn: Jesus's own parable in Luke 18 (the Pharisee and the tax collector). Two men pray in the temple. The Pharisee thanks God he is not a sinner, lists his tithes and commandment-keeping, and looks down at the tax collector. The tax collector won't lift his head, beats his breast, and says: "God, be merciful to me, a sinner." Jesus's verdict: "I tell you, this man went down to his house justified rather than the other." How are you justified in Jesus's teaching? Through **humbleness and repentance** — exactly as the Hebrew Bible has always taught. No faith-in-blood-credit mechanism. No "imputed righteousness" framework. Just turning toward God.

## Misconceptions Addressed

| Misconception | What the Text Actually Says |
|---------------|----------------------------|
| Habakkuk 2:4 teaches justification by faith — the mechanism by which sinners get declared righteous | Habakkuk 2:4 teaches the endurance of an already-righteous person through the delayed fulfillment of God's promised vision against Babylon. The verse is about trust under tarrying, not soteriological mechanism |
| Paul's reading "he who through faith is righteous shall live" is just a translation of Habakkuk | The Hebrew word order and meaning is "the righteous shall live by his faith." Paul's re-grammaring changes faith from describing *how* the righteous live into *what* makes them righteous. Different claim entirely |
| The Hebrew Bible has no theology of forgiveness — everyone is damned until faith in Jesus's blood | Psalm 51, Psalm 103, Luke 18's tax collector, and the entire prophetic tradition all teach forgiveness flowing from humble repentance. Paul's framework retroactively recategorizes this as "Law that no one could keep" |
| "Righteous" in the Hebrew Bible means sinless or perfectly law-keeping | "Righteous" means oriented toward God, humble, trusting, willing to repent. David is called righteous after adultery and murder because he repented |
| Jesus and Paul preach the same gospel of justification by faith in the blood | In Luke 18 Jesus explicitly teaches that justification comes through humble repentance with no blood-credit framework. Two men pray in the temple — no sacrifices, no offerings, no shed blood — and one goes home justified. Paul's framework is the novelty |
| Habakkuk 2:4 is just a passing rabbinic allusion in Paul, like Matthew's "out of Egypt I called my son" | Paul explicitly calls Romans 1:17 the foundation of "my gospel which I preach." This is not a literary flourish — it is the textual cornerstone of the entire Christian justification-by-faith doctrine |
